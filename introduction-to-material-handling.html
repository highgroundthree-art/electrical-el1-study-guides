Why this guide exists

You do not need to work in construction to support a construction student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning and put it in their own words. Material handling is about moving things without hurting anyone, so the habit worth building is stopping to think, dressing for the job and asking for help before a lift or a move goes wrong.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 20 to 30 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Good to know before you start

This guide explains material handling so your student can talk about it. It does not train or certify anyone to operate equipment. The module says operators of motorized material handling equipment must be trained, certified and authorized for each type of equipment they use. Weights, heights and limits come from the textbook. Always follow the rules that apply at your student's school or job site, and the manufacturer's instructions for each piece of equipment. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Material Handling Basics

By the end of this section your student can explain why manual handling is a leading cause of injury, plan a task before touching the load, dress safely for it, and describe a safe lift and a safe lowering.

A

Read it together

about 30 min

Lifting, stacking, carrying and unloading are routine on a job site. Most of the harm comes from skipping the thinking part. The habit this section builds is simple: plan first, dress right, then lift with your legs.

Why it matters (1.0.0)

Manual material handling is on almost every construction site, and it is one of the leading causes of non-fatal injuries in the industry. The module cites the Bureau of Labor Statistics: each year more than one million workers suffer back injuries, and about one of every five workplace injuries or illnesses involves the back.

A load is the quantity of material a person, machine, vehicle or piece of equipment carries, transports or relocates at one time. Wood, brick, lumber and pipe are all loads. Whenever you handle material there is a risk of injury, so stay aware of what you are doing and follow the basic rules.

Pre-task planning (1.1.1)

Being mentally fit matters as much as being physically fit. Most handling accidents happen to workers who are new and inexperienced, or to very experienced workers who think an accident cannot happen to them. Before you lift anything, assess the situation:

  • Check that the load is not too big, too heavy or too hard to grasp.
  • Look for protruding nails, wires or sharp edges.
  • Make sure it is something you can lift by yourself. If not, ask a co-worker.
  • Inspect the path of travel for slip, trip and fall hazards. Move them or go around them.
  • Read any warning labels or instructions on the material, so you know the dangers of mishandling that product.

Personal protective equipment (1.1.2)

  • No loose clothing that can catch on moving or protruding parts. Button sleeves and tuck in shirt tails.
  • Remove rings and jewelry. Tie long hair back and keep it under the hard hat.
  • If you wear a wristwatch, make it one that breaks away easily if it gets caught in machinery.
  • Gloves whenever cuts, splinters, blisters or other hand injuries are possible. They must fit properly: gloves that are too tight add hand fatigue, but loose gloves reduce grip strength and can catch on moving objects. They should be snug with reasonable dexterity.
  • Take gloves off around rotating machinery and equipment with exposed moving parts.
  • Follow your employer's policies and the job site's policies.

Proper lifting and lowering (1.1.3)

Find the weight of the load first and plan the lift. Know where it is going and clear slip and trip hazards from the path. Know your limits. If the load is too heavy alone, ask for help.

  1. Get firm footing, space your feet for balance, bend your knees and get a good grip.
  2. Lift with your legs. Keep your back straight and your head up.
  3. Keep the load close to your body. Do not reach out with it.
  4. Never turn or twist until you are standing straight. Then pivot your feet and body.
  5. To set a load down, use your leg muscles. Move your fingers out of the way so they do not get pinched or crushed. Where possible, attach handles to the load.
Ask a co-worker for help when
SituationExamples from the module
Too heavyWeighing 50 pounds or more for one person to carry alone.
Too longObjects longer than 10 feet, such as lumber, conduit, pipe and scaffolding poles.
Wind can grab itPlywood and tarps, which wind gusts can push around.

Sometimes the trouble is the shape of the load, not its weight. Secure long or cumbersome objects. Tie floppy rods, pipe or lumber together in several places before carrying, and never carry more pieces than you can control. When you turn with a long object, watch for people and things it could strike, and never swing around quickly. Never pass material to another worker unless that person is looking directly at you and expecting it.

Lowering heavy loads from overhead (1.1.3)

Reaching and lowering from overhead is very dangerous. When lifting you can always stop and put the load down if it is too heavy. Lowering from overhead, it is usually too late by the time you realize it. Reaching above shoulder height also stresses the shoulders and back and forces awkward hand and wrist positions.

  • Size up the load. If it looks too heavy to have been lifted there by one person, it is probably too heavy for one person to take down. Ask yourself how it got up there. The way it went up is probably the best way to bring it down.
  • Lower it the way you would lift it: knees bent, back straight. To place it to one side, move your feet instead of twisting.
  • Treat the area below as a fall zone. Do not let anyone enter while the load is coming down.
Protect your backExercise, good posture, a healthy weight and diet can help prevent back injuries, and a doctor can advise on muscle-strengthening for the back.

    Stacking, Storing and Working from Heights

    By the end of this section your student can explain why materials are stacked and stored properly, give the stacking rules for common materials, and describe how to get tools and material to a higher level safely.

    A

    Read it together

    about 25 min

    Everyone on the site, and everything on it, should be safe from unexpected movement such as falling, slipping, tipping, rolling or blowing over. Good stacking and storing is how that happens.

    Why stacking and storing matter (1.2.1)

    Stacked materials stay out of the way of other work, are better organized and easier to reach, make safe storage and free up room. Store materials and equipment properly when they are not in use, and learn your company and site rules and the guidelines of the US Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA). Poor storage can lead to accidents, injuries, wasted material, higher job costs and project delays.

    The module's examples: lumber stored with the nails still in can puncture workers who pull it out again. Materials stacked on the ground that belong on a pallet have to be moved again and can be damaged. Tools left out make an untidy site that invites accidents and lost tools. If you are unsure how to store something, ask your supervisor.

    Stacking and storing rules
    MaterialRule
    AislesKeep aisles and passageways in the storage area clear.
    CartonsKeep them away from rain and moisture. Never stack them higher than the height listed on the carton.
    LumberStack on level, solidly supported surfaces. Remove all nails.
    PipeStack neatly, chocked so it cannot roll. Stack pipe and fittings by size so nobody has to dig for the piece they need.
    Drums, tanks, reels, trailers, wagonsChock all material and equipment as needed to stop shifting or rolling. The chock size and shape must suit the material.
    Light, large-surface materialTie down or band anything the wind might move.
    Bagged materialDo not stack the same width to the top. Step the layers back and cross-key the bags at least every 10 bags high.
    BricksNo higher than 7 feet. Taper the stack back 2 inches for every foot above 4 feet.
    Masonry blocksTaper back one-half block per tier above 6 feet.
    Flammables such as gasolineStore in a well-ventilated area away from ignition, or in an approved flammable storage cabinet.

    Working from heights (1.2.2)

    Moving material to a higher location takes good planning and safety awareness.

    • Use a safety harness with a fall-arrest lanyard connected to an appropriate anchor point when working from heights over six feet. A positioning belt with a lanyard can be added if required.
    • Never throw tools or materials up to a worker on a higher level or down to a worker on a lower level. Use a rope to raise and lower them. Once the material is up, tie off the rope and secure the material so it cannot fall.
    • Before climbing a ladder, check your pockets and tool belt so nothing falls out or snags. Keep your hands free. Never carry tools or material by hand up a ladder. Put objects in a bucket to send them up and down.
    • Never stack or store materials on scaffolds or runways. It raises the chance of falling-object hazards, especially in high-traffic areas.
    Going greenWhen cleaning up around the site, check whether materials can be reused or recycled before throwing them in the trash.

      Knots for Material Handling

      By the end of this section your student can use the knot-tying vocabulary, and say what the square knot, bowline, half hitch and clove hitch are used for and where each is weak.

      A

      Read it together

      about 30 min

      Knots and hitches fasten ropes to each other or to an object such as a beam or container. On a job site they secure loads, steady scaffolding, and raise and lower tools and supplies. Using the right knot for the job is critical.

      Knot basics (1.3.0)

      Most knots are permanent. They have to be untied to come apart. The ones called hitches are not permanent: pull the ropes in the reverse of the direction they hold and they undo. This section covers four knots: the square knot, the bowline, the half hitch and the clove hitch.

      Knot-tying words
      TermMeaning
      Standing endThe long end of the rope, the end not being knotted.
      Working endThe short end, the end being used to tie the knot.
      Standing partThe portion of rope between the standing end and the working end.
      CapsizeTo change the form and rearrange the parts of a knot, usually by pulling on certain ends. It can be done on purpose to undo a hitch. A knot used wrongly can capsize on its own, and that is a serious hazard.

      Two more points: many knots can be tied more than one way, but if the technique is followed correctly the finished knot is the same. And knots can be tied right-handed or left-handed. A left-handed knot is the mirror image of the right-handed one. A few knots have both a right-handed and a left-handed element, so keep both or you will tie the wrong knot.

      The square knot (1.3.1)

      Also called a reef knot. It is made of two reverse half-knots. Use it to tie the ends of a rope around an object such as a parcel or the neck of a sack, or to join two lengths of rope in low-strain jobs. The two ropes should be about the same diameter, or the knot is more likely to slip.

      Never for critical loadsSquare knots can capsize easily. Extra half knots are often added for security.

      To tie it: bring the two working ends together, one pointing left and one right. Cross left over right, then pass the left one under the right. That is one half knot. Repeat the over-and-under, but right over left this time. The end that started on top must stay on top when you switch direction. Pull the ends tight for a neat, symmetrical knot.

      The granny knotThe most common mistake is tying the second half knot in the wrong direction. That makes a granny knot, which is highly insecure.

      The bowline (1.3.2)

      The bowline makes a secure loop in the end of a rope that neither slips nor jams. It is nicknamed the rescue knot and the king of knots, because it is reliable enough for rescue work (usually backed up with a stopper knot, or tied twice as a double bowline). It is handy for hoisting a sack of material or a bucket of tools up to a platform. Two bowlines linked together can join two ropes when the strain is more than a square knot can handle.

      • It does not slip or bind under load and is easy to untie when there is no load.
      • It cannot be tied or untied with a load on the standing end, so avoid it if the rope may need to be released under load.
      • It can reduce the strength of the rope it is tied in by as much as 40 percent.
      • It needs a long tail for safety. The module's rarely practiced rule of thumb is a tail as long as 12 times the rope's circumference, which is more than 18 inches for a half-inch rope.

      To tie it: form a small loop in the rope, leaving enough tail for the size of main loop you want. Pass the tail through the small loop, then under the standing end, then back down through the small loop. Adjust the main loop, tighten, and keep a long enough tail. A memory trick from the module: the rabbit comes out of the hole, goes around the tree, and goes back into the hole.

      The half hitch (1.3.3)

      A half hitch ties a rope around an object such as a rail, bar, post or ring. It is used to hang items from overhead beams and to carry light loads that must come off easily. It is also a building block in other knots, such as the clove hitch. On its own it is not very stable, so it is not suitable for heavy loads or any task where safety is paramount. It is often used to back up another knot that is already tied.

      To tie it: form a loop around the object. Pass the tail around the standing part and through the loop. Tighten by pulling the working end and the standing part at the same time.

      The clove hitch (1.3.4)

      Also called a builder's hitch. It is one of the most widely used general hitches, and it is made of two half hitches in opposite directions. It makes a quick, secure tension knot on a fixed anchor such as a post, pole or beam, and it can be the first knot when lashing items together. Because it is a tension knot, it comes loose as soon as the tension is removed. It can also bind. For these reasons, do not use it by itself. Extra half hitches can make it more secure.

      • Threading the end: pass the working end over the object, back over the standing part and over the object again (first half hitch), then thread it back under itself and up through the loop (second half hitch). Pull both ends evenly.
      • Stacked loops: make two identical loops, one in each hand, cross them one above the other, drop the knot over a stake or post and pull both ends. It can be tied anywhere in a rope, not only at an end.
      • Two half hitches on a post: form a loop and put it over the post, form a second identical loop and put it over the post too, then tighten.
      Did you know?The module says records of the clove hitch go back at least 10,000 years.

      The module's performance task: demonstrate how to tie two of the four knots (square knot, bowline, half hitch, clove hitch) under supervision.

        Non-Motorized Material Handling Equipment

        By the end of this section your student can name the manual equipment in the module, say what each is used for, and give the main safety rule for each one.

        A

        Read it together

        about 30 min

        Instead of lifting heavy loads by hand, use a material handling device when one is available. These devices ease the strain on muscles and joints, raise productivity and lower the chance of dropping and damaging equipment or supplies. With non-motorized (manual) equipment, your own body supplies the force to move it.

        The list (2.1.0)

        Material carts, hand trucks, cylinder carts, drum dollies, drum carts, roller skids, wheelbarrows, pipe mules, pipe transports and pallet jacks.

        Material carts (2.1.1)

        Also called platform trucks: a platform or board laid flat on four caster wheels, used to move material around a job site.

        • Check that casters roll and swivel freely. With pneumatic tires, check air pressure. Replace tires that will not hold air or have flat spots.
        • Check the surface has enough traction to avoid slips. Keep your hands away from the edges of the handles to avoid pinching, crushing or cutting them.
        • Center and secure the load so it cannot roll or fall off.
        • If material sticks out in front, have a spotter walk ahead to help you avoid objects and other workers.
        • Be careful on inclines and declines and never go over the labeled weight capacity, which must be marked in plain view. On a decline a too-heavy load can drag you down with it, and on an incline it can back over you.

        Hand trucks (2.1.2)

        Also called dollies. Two-wheeled, vertical carts with a metal blade at the bottom that slides under a large, heavy load such as an appliance or a stack of heavy boxes. Many have ratchet straps.

        • Before use, inspect the framework for stress fractures and check the tires.
        • To load: tilt the object forward, slide the blade under it, hold it against the frame and tilt back onto the wheels.
        • Secure it with a ratchet strap, buckled around the load and tightened snug. Rubber or bungee-style cords may not hold if the weight shifts.

        Cylinder carts (2.1.3)

        High-pressure gas cylinders must go on carts designed for compressed gases. Even an inert (not chemically reactive) gas under pressure can pose an explosion risk.

        • Check the label to identify the gas and confirm the cylinder is approved for transport.
        • Never move a cylinder without the safety cap screwed all the way down. Never lift it by the cap. Never move it with the regulator attached.
        • Secure the cylinder upright with chains or straps. Many sites require a partition between the two bottles. Wear the right PPE.
        Deadly projectileIf a compressed gas cylinder ruptures or cracks in transport, it can become a deadly projectile or explode into fragments of shrapnel.

        Drum dollies and drum carts (2.1.4)

        Chemicals, lubricants and other materials are often kept in 55-gallon drums, which are heavy and unwieldy when full. A drum dolly moves relatively light drums: a flat wheeled platform the drum sits on, sometimes with a strap or a pull handle. A drum cart moves heavier drums such as sealed oil drums. A common type is the rotating drum cart, with prongs, handles, wheels and heavy-duty casters.

        Rotating drum cart: with the cart held vertically, slide the prongs under the drum. Let the cart roll back to horizontal onto its wheels and casters. With the drum resting in the well, use the handles to steer it. At the destination, tip the cart vertical so the drum can be unloaded.

        Roller skids (2.1.5)

        A table surface on two, three or four roller skids. You put the material on the table and push. Heavy-equipment skids use steel or steel-chain rollers, and lighter-duty ones may use polyurethane or nylon. Inspect before use and respect the capacity. The floor must be fairly smooth, since small rocks or hardware can stop the rollers, so sweep the route first.

        Wheelbarrows (2.1.6)

        One- or two-wheeled with handles at the rear, for small loads. Use two-wheeled wheelbarrows on job sites because they are more stable with heavy loads. Check the air pressure in both tires and the body for cracks, breaks or punctures. Use proper lifting technique.

        Pipe mules and pipe transports (2.1.7 and 2.1.8)

        A pipe mule is a two-wheeled device for medium-length pipe, tubing or scaffolding. It is also called a tunnel buggy, a V-cart, or by a brand name, a grasshopper. To load, one worker lifts one end of the load while another rolls the mule underneath, with the wheels under the center of the load. Use a spotter with any long load.

        A pipe transport is similar but moves larger pipe, slung under a frame on two rubber wheels. It takes two people to operate.

        Pinch pointsWhen securing chains around the pipe on a pipe transport, watch for pinch points. Do not overload it, follow the manufacturer's recommendations and keep both hands on the handle to stay in control.

        Pallet jacks (2.1.9)

        Also called a pallet truck. It typically uses hydraulics to lift and move heavy or stacked pallets. Motorized versions exist and are sometimes called walkies.

        • Plan and review the route. Be very careful on uneven surfaces or ramps.
        • Inspect before use for malfunctions and missing parts. Check hydraulic fittings and hoses for oil leaks and repair leaks before using it.
        • Never exceed the rated capacity posted on the jack.
        • Check the wood pallet is in good condition and the load is centered between the forks.

          Motorized Equipment and Hand Signals

          By the end of this section your student can name the motorized equipment in the module, say who may operate it, describe safety around forklifts, and show why hand signals are used.

          A

          Read it together

          about 30 min

          Motorized material handling equipment runs on gasoline or electric motors. It can move far more than a person can, so the rules about who may run it and how it is checked are strict.

          Operators and general rules (2.2.0)

          Operators must be trained, certified and authorized. Training combines formal instruction, hands-on training and an evaluation of the operator's performance in the workplace, for each type of equipment, and it must be done by a person with the knowledge, training and experience. Certification is required for many, though not all, types.

          When handling material with a machine such as a pallet jack or forklift, always:

          • Know the weight of the object.
          • Know the capacity of the device and never exceed it.
          • Make sure the equipment is in good working order and free of damage.
          • Secure the load, and watch for obstructions such as people or equipment in the path.
          NameplatesA nameplate must be posted on each material handling device showing its capacity, its approximate weight and any instructions.

          Powered wheelbarrow (2.2.1)

          Also called a power buggy. Like a manual wheelbarrow, but powered by an electric or gas motor. Before use, inspect it: brakes and linkages, tires and wheels (damage and pressure), directional, dumping and speed controls, and the safety labels and decals, which must be visible and readable.

          • Avoid pinch points on the dumping mechanism.
          • Keep the dump bucket securely down except when dumping. Do not move the buggy with the bucket in the dumping position.
          • Do not mount or dismount while it is moving. No riders.
          • Avoid slopes that could tip it. Do not exceed load limits in weight or height.
          • When finished, shut off the engine and lock the parking brake.

          Concrete mule (2.2.2)

          Also called a Georgia buggy. It is used when a concrete pour is somewhere a delivery truck or pump cannot reach. It carries concrete, sand, gravel or similar material, and should not carry undesignated materials.

          • Inspect the tires, fluids, mechanical hinges and joints, and the throttle cables and steering.
          • Refueling: the engine must be off and cooled down. Use only the fuel and oil the manufacturer specifies. This applies to any gas-powered handling machine.
          • When discharging near an open pit, use a tire stop board or chocks so the mule cannot roll forward into the pit.
          • Keep obstacles and people out of the turning radius of the rear platform. Look behind you before reversing. Only the operator rides.

          Freight elevator (2.2.3)

          On multi-level sites, a freight elevator moves materials from floor to floor. Its doors usually close from the top and bottom at the same time, and unlike a passenger elevator they have no safety bumpers. The top door is pulled down by a strap or handle while the bottom door rises to meet it, with metal hitting metal. Keep arms, hands, fingers, tool belts, body harnesses and materials clear of the door, and check the weight capacity on the elevator wall before loading.

          Industrial forklift (2.2.4)

          A vehicle with a power-operated, pronged platform that is raised and lowered to go under a load. It lifts, lowers and moves large or heavy loads where the ground is smooth, such as warehouses and shops.

          Working around one:

          • Stay a safe distance from the work zone, the area where the machine may contact objects or people with its rear end or its forks.
          • Stay clear of the fall zone: directly under a load, and anywhere a suspended load could fall.
          • Use designated walkways, make eye contact with the operator and stay out of the driver's blind spot.
          • Forklifts pivot on the front wheels and steer with the back wheels, which creates a pinch point.
          • Plan an escape route. Never get trapped in a corner or against the side of a truck.

          Operating one (trained and certified operators only): wear proper PPE and fasten the seat belt. Without it, a tip-over can pitch you under the forklift or drop part of the load on you. Wear a hard hat for falling objects. No baggy clothes. Never play on a forklift or drive recklessly.

          Daily forklift inspection
          Check
          Tires · fluid levels · battery or fuel level · fire extinguisher · brakes · deadman control · warning lights · horn · backup alarm

          Use a checklist if there is one and report any defect to your supervisor. The safest operators drive with slow deliberation. Forklifts are top-heavy and can tip on a curve, go off a dock edge or fail to stop in time. Drive at a speed that suits the location and surface, take extra care on hills, corners and ramps, keep right on roads and in wide aisles, stop at stop signs, and start and stop smoothly so loads do not shift. Keep arms, hands and legs inside the cage. Pedestrians have the right-of-way. Electric models are quiet, so people may not know you are there. Reversing is especially dangerous when you are watching the load in front.

          Never modify a forkliftWorkers sometimes modify forklifts to move heavier or wider loads. That risks broken masts or forks, dropped loads and tip-overs. Never modify one without consent from the manufacturer or a mechanical engineer who knows the equipment.

          Rough terrain forklift (2.2.5)

          Built for irregular surfaces and jobs with no paved ground. It has large pneumatic tires with deep treads. Apply the same safety guidelines as for industrial forklifts. Tip-overs are one of the most common accidents, because the ground is uneven. To prevent them:

          • Study the load charts and stay within the stability limits.
          • Before placing a load: apply the brakes, shift to neutral, level the frame and engage the stabilizers if equipped.
          • Carry the load low, and avoid driving with it raised except for short distances.
          • Be careful on slopes and stay within grade limits.
          • Retract the boom and lower the forks before moving.

          Hand signals (2.2.6)

          Equipment noise and the distance between workers make talking difficult, so standard hand signals let workers and the operator lift, load, move and unload in a controlled way. There is no globally accepted standard. The ones in the module are common, and your employer may use a slightly different set. Before the task, the operator and the other workers must agree on the signals and what each one means. Keep eye contact with the operator at all times.

          Common forklift hand signals (Figure 33)
          SignalHow it is made
          Raise the tinesForearm vertical, forefinger pointing up, hand moving in a small horizontal circle.
          Lower the tinesArm extended, palm down, arm lowered vertically.
          Tilt mast backForearm vertical, thumb extended, thumb jerked over the shoulder.
          Tilt mast forwardArm extended, fingers closed, thumb pointing down, arm lowered vertically.
          Move tines in direction finger pointsArm extended, palm down, forefinger pointing the way to move.
          Dog (pause) everythingHands clasped in front of the body.
          StopBoth arms extended, palms down.

          The tines are the L-shaped parts that support the load. Dog everything means pause. Use it when a risky situation comes up, such as rain starting, a load that does not fit the planned space or a bystander who gets too close.

          Sidebars in the moduleTugs are battery-powered carts that push and pull loads, and their operators are not required to have the special training and certification a forklift operator needs. Hydrogen fuel cells are replacing lead-acid batteries in some forklifts, and they refuel in minutes. Biodiesel, a plant-oil-based fuel, can be mixed with regular diesel at any ratio. Automated forklifts are suited to repetitive horizontal travel along the same route.
