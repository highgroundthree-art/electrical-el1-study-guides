Conductors

By the end of this section your student can sort conductors by wire size, material, insulation and use, read the markings on a cable, and name the common cable types and where each one goes.

A

Read it together

about 25 min

An electrician has to pick the right wire or cable for every job, then pull it through conduit and terminate it. This section is about the picking: how wire is sized, what it is made of, how the insulation is coded, and which cable goes where.

What a conductor is (1.0.0)

The word conductor is used two ways. It can mean the current-carrying part of a wire or cable, or it can mean the whole wire assembly: the current-carrying part plus its outer covering, the insulation. In this module, unless it says otherwise, "conductor" means the whole assembly.

Conductors are identified by size and insulation material. Size means the cross-sectional area of the current-carrying part. Ampacity is affected by the conductor material and size, the insulation, and the installation location.

Trade term: ampacityThe maximum current, in amperes, a conductor can carry continuously under the conditions of use without exceeding its temperature rating.

Wire sizes and the AWG system (1.1.0, 1.1.1)

Wire sizes are given in gauge numbers. In the United States the standard system is the American Wire Gauge (AWG). The rule to remember: the larger the number, the smaller the wire. A bigger cross-section carries more current.

  • AWG numbers run from 50 up to No. 1, then 0, 00, 000 and 0000. Those four are called one aught (1/0), two aught (2/0), three aught (3/0) and four aught (4/0).
  • Any wire larger than 4/0 is identified by its area in thousands of circular mils, written kcmil.
  • For wire sizes larger than No. 16 AWG, the size is marked on the insulation.
  • NEC Chapter 9, Table 8 gives descriptive information on wire sizes. Wires smaller than No. 6 come solid or stranded. Wire sizes No. 6 or larger are shown only as stranded.

Stranding (1.1.2)

According to NEC Chapter 9, Table 8, wire sizes No. 18 to No. 2 have seven strands. Wire sizes No. 1 to No. 4/0 have 19 strands. Sizes from 250 kcmil to 500 kcmil have 37 strands.

The purpose of stranding is to increase flexibility. A conductor must be flexible and almost round, so the individual strands cannot be too large. Terminating solid wire larger than No. 8 in pull boxes, disconnect switches and panels would be very difficult and could damage equipment and wire insulation. Pulling solid wire in conduit around bends would also be a problem above No. 8. Aluminum conductors are designed with compact (compressed) stranding.

Circular mils (1.1.3)

A circular mil is a circle with a diameter of 1 mil. A mil is 0.001 inch, and one kcmil is 1,000 circular mils. A 250 kcmil wire has a current-carrying area equal to 250,000 circles, each 0.001 inch across. It can seem clumsy, but the alternative would be sizing wire in square inches. According to NEC Chapter 9, Table 8, a 250 kcmil conductor has a cross-sectional area of 0.260 square inch. It is easier to say "250 kcmil."

Conductor materials (1.3.0)

The most common conductor material is copper, because of its excellent conductivity (low resistance), ease of use and value. The value of a material for wire depends on four things: conductivity, cost, availability and workability.

  • Conductivity (1.3.1). This is how easily current travels through a conductor. Low resistance means high conductivity. Silver is one of the best conductors. Copper has high conductivity and a lower price than silver. Aluminum also has good conductivity, about two-thirds that of copper.
  • Cost (1.3.2). A cheaper material is sometimes chosen even though its properties are not as good. Copper over platinum is an easy choice, because platinum costs far too much. Copper versus aluminum is often harder to decide.
  • Availability (1.3.3). The mining industry often controls the supply of raw materials, which can cause shortages and changes the price of finished copper or aluminum wire.
  • Workability (1.3.4). Pick a material that needs less expense for tools and is easier to work with. Aluminum is lighter and more flexible than copper of the same size. But terminating aluminum often needs special tools and an anti-oxidation material on the termination surfaces, and it takes more training. Part of the reason is that aluminum expands and contracts with heat more than copper does.
Terminating aluminum wireUse listed connectors. All aluminum connections need an anti-oxidizing compound. Some connectors are precoated, and others need the compound added, so check the connectors before you start.

Conductor insulation (1.4.0)

The first attempt to insulate wire came in the early 1800s with the telegraph. That insulation was for physical protection, not electrical protection, because the telegraph ran on low-voltage DC. It was tarred hemp or cotton fiber and shellac, used mainly to weatherproof long-distance lines.

Early electrical systems used knob-and-tube wiring: bare wire wrapped around ceramic knobs and pulled through ceramic tubes in drilled framing. Some of it is still working in houses from the early 1900s. Knob-and-tube was revised to use insulated conductors and stayed in use up to 1957 in some areas.

The grounded (neutral) conductor in overhead services may be bare, and so may the concentric grounded conductor in Type SE cable when used as service-entrance cable. But all current-carrying conductors, including the grounded conductor, must be insulated when used inside buildings or after the first overcurrent protection device. NEC Table 310.4(A) lists the 600-volt insulated conductors and the letter designation for each type.

Thermoplastic (1.4.1)

Thermoplastic is a popular and effective insulation. These are widely used:

  • Polyvinyl chloride (PVC): the base material for TW and THW insulation.
  • Polyethylene (PE): an excellent weatherproofing material used mainly for control and communications wiring. It is not used for high-voltage conductors (above 5,000 volts).
  • Cross-linked polyethylene (XLP): an improved PE with better heat and moisture resistance. Used for THHN, THWN and THHW wiring and many high-voltage cables.
  • Nylon: mainly a jacketing material. THHN building wire has an outer coating of nylon.
  • Teflon: a high-temperature insulation, widely used for telephone wiring in a plenum, where other insulated conductors would need conduit.

Thermoset (1.4.2)

Many thermoplastics deform when heated. Thermoset materials keep their form when heated. Thermoset insulations include RHH, RHW, XHH, XHHW and SIS.

Color coding (1.4.4)

A color code helps identify wires by the color of the insulation, which makes them easier to install and connect correctly. A typical code:

  • Two-conductor cable: one white or gray wire (neutral), one black wire (hot) and a grounding wire (usually bare).
  • Three-conductor cable: one white or gray, one black, one red and a grounding wire.
  • Four-conductor cable: same as three, plus a fourth wire (blue).
  • Five-conductor cable: same as four, plus a fifth wire (yellow).

The grounding conductor may be bare, green, or green with a yellow stripe.

The NEC does not require color coding of ungrounded conductors, except where more than one nominal voltage system is present or on branch circuits supplied from direct-current systems [NEC Section 210.5(C)(2)]. Ungrounded conductors may be any color except white, gray or green, but color coding is good practice, and many construction specifications require it.

On a four-wire, delta-connected secondary where the midpoint of one phase winding is grounded to supply lighting and similar loads, the phase conductor with the higher voltage to ground must be identified by an orange outer finish, by tagging, or by other effective means. That identification goes at each point where a connection is made if the grounded conductor is also present (NEC Section 110.15). In most cases, orange tape is used at all termination points.

SafetyColor designations are good indicators, but never trust your life to them. Always protect yourself by testing circuits with a voltmeter.

Wire ratings (1.4.5)

A critical factor in picking a conductor is its maximum operating temperature. Think about how and where the conductor will be used so its temperature rating is never exceeded. A conductor's operating temperature depends on:

  • the ambient temperature;
  • current flow in the conductor, including harmonic current;
  • current flow in bundled conductors, which raises the ambient temperature;
  • how fast or slow heat escapes into the surroundings, which the insulation affects.

Where the conductor will be terminated matters too, because the temperature rating of the termination may limit the allowable ampacity. In general, the safe current and the maximum safe temperature depend on conductor size (in circular mils), ambient temperature, the number of conductors in a bundle, and where the conductors are installed (raceways, conduit, ducts, underground and so on).

NEC Tables 310.16 and 310.17 assume an ambient temperature of 30°C (86°F). Ampacity is based on that ambient temperature plus the heat (I²R) produced by the current. So the type of insulation is the first thing that sets the maximum permitted ampacity.

ExampleA No. 3/0 THW copper conductor in a raceway has an ampacity of 200A in NEC Table 310.16. At 30°C (86°F) the conductor is already at that temperature with no current. THW insulation is rated 75°C (167°F), which leaves a wide margin for heat from current flow.

If the ambient temperature goes above 30°C (86°F), the maximum load current must be reduced in proportion, per NEC Table 310.15(B)(1), so the total temperature stays within the insulation rating. For the same reason, the allowable ampacity must be reduced when more than three conductors are in a raceway or cable (NEC Section 310.15(C)(1)).

When designing a circuit, you choose the conductor type (TW, THW, THWN, RHH, THHN, XHHW and so on). The types are covered in NEC Article 310, and ampacities are in Tables 310.16 through 310.21 for varying conditions: in a raceway, in open air, at normal or higher-than-normal ambient temperatures. Conductors must be used in accordance with the tables and their notes.

Fixture wiring (1.5.0)

Fixture wire is used for the interior wiring of fixtures and for wiring fixtures to a power source. Guidelines are in NEC Article 402, the approved types are in Table 402.3, and the allowed ampacities are in Table 402.5. Fixture wires are insulated conductors, with or without an outer jacket, in sizes from No. 18 to No. 10 AWG.

Which fixture wire to use depends mainly on the operating temperature expected inside the fixture, so it is the insulation that decides. Wire insulated with perfluoroalkoxy (PFA) or extruded polytetrafluoroethylene (PTF) is chosen when the fixture may reach 250°C (482°F), the highest operating temperature allowed for any fixture wire. Fixture wires are suitable for 600 volts unless Table 402.3 says otherwise.

The main use is inside fixtures, but several of the wires in Table 402.3 may be used for remote-control, signaling or power-limited circuits under NEC Section 725.49. Fixture wires may never be used as substitutes for branch circuit conductors.

Cable types and applications (1.6.0)

A cable is two or more insulated wires, and may include a grounding wire, covered by an outer jacket or sheath. Cable is usually classified by its covering: nonmetallic (plastic) or metallic, also called armored cable. It may also be classified by where it can be used (NEC Table 400.4). Water is a good conductor, so moisture can cause power loss or short circuits, and cables are classified for dry, damp or wet locations. They can also be classified for sunlight exposure and rough use.

All cables are marked with important information: wire size, number of conductors, cable type and voltage rating, and sometimes a mark for an approved service or application. This is printed on nonmetallic cable. On metallic cable it is usually on a tag. The NEC requires that all cables and fittings be listed.

Cable selectionTwo things decide the cable type for an application: the type of conductor insulation and the cable jacket. Both must be right for the job.

Nonmetallic-sheathed cable, Types NM and NMC (1.6.1)

Types NM and NMC are widely used for branch circuits and feeders in homes and commercial buildings. Both are commonly called Romex, though the manufacturer applies that name only to Type NM. The rules are in NEC Article 334. The cable has two or more insulated conductors and one bare conductor inside a nonmetallic sheath. The conductors may be wrapped in paper, and the gaps may be filled with jute, paper or other material to protect the conductors and keep the shape. The sheath is flame-retardant and moisture-resistant. The NMC sheath is also fungus- and corrosion-resistant.

  • Both may be installed exposed or concealed.
  • Type NM is suitable for dry locations only. Type NMC is permitted in dry, moist, damp or corrosive locations.
  • Both may be used in one- and two-family dwellings and certain multifamily dwellings, depending on construction (NEC Section 334.10).
  • Not in ducts or plenums, because toxic gases from burning insulation would spread through the building. Not exposed in the space above suspended ceilings. Not as service-entrance cable, embedded in concrete, or in hazardous locations.

NEC Article 334 has many more rules. Read and understand the applicable sections before installing this type of cable.

Type UF cable (1.6.2)

Type UF (underground feeder and branch circuit) cable follows NEC Article 340. It looks and is built a lot like Type NMC, and is used the same way. The main difference: UF is suitable for direct burial, and NMC is not.

Permitted uses include underground and direct burial, single-conductor use, wet, dry or corrosive conditions, as nonmetallic-sheathed cable, in solar photovoltaic systems, and in cable trays. Typically it may not be used as service-entrance cable, in commercial garages, in theaters, in hoistways or elevators, or in hazardous locations. It generally cannot be embedded in poured cement, concrete or aggregate, exposed to sunlight (unless designed for that), or used as overhead cable.

Type MV cable (1.6.3)

Type MV (medium-voltage) cable is covered in NEC Article 311. It is one or more insulated conductors inside an outer jacket, for voltages from 2,001 to 35,000 volts. It may be installed in wet and dry locations and buried directly in the earth.

Type MC cable (1.6.4)

Type MC (metal clad) cable has one or more insulated conductors inside a metal tape or metallic sheath. NEC Article 330 covers it, and UL 1569 is the standard for metal clad cables. MC is used in a wide range of jobs, from small instrumentation cable to medium-voltage feeders. The conductors have thermoset or thermoplastic insulation, and MC can also combine electrical conductors with optical fiber.

Markings typically show the maximum voltage, AWG size (or circular mil area) and insulation type. If the outer covering will not take markings, they are on a tape inside the cable, along its whole length. If they are on the outside, they typically repeat every 24 inches (61 cm).

The three types of MC cable are interlocked metal tape, corrugated metal tube and smooth metal tube. The outer covering may be a nonmetallic jacket over the metal sheath. Typical uses: services, feeders and branch circuits; power, lighting, control and signal circuits; indoors or outdoors; exposed or concealed; direct burial (if identified for it); in any raceway; and other uses in Article 330. It may not be used in corrosive or damaging conditions unless the metal cladding protects the conductors or other protection is used. Uses typically not permitted include where the cable is subject to physical damage, direct burial, in concrete, or where it is subject to caustic materials.

Armored (AC) and MC cable both make installation easier. The flexible metal sheath protects the conductors and lets them bend around corners without kinking. Because the conductors are already protected, there is no need to pull them into a raceway, no worry about them touching pipes or hard surfaces, and no need for wire pullers, fish tapes or lubricants.

High-voltage shielded cable (1.6.5)

Shielding on high-voltage cable protects the conductor assembly against surface discharge or burning from corona discharge in ionized air, which can destroy the insulation and jacket. Electrostatic shielding uses both nonmetallic and metallic materials.

Channel wire assemblies, Type FC (1.6.6)

Channel wire (Type FC) is an entire wiring system: cable, cable supports, splicers, circuit taps, fixture hangers and fittings. NEC Article 322 covers it. Type FC cable is a flat assembly of three or four parallel No. 10 special stranded copper conductors, installed in an approved U-channel surface metal raceway with one side open. Tap devices can go in anywhere along the run. Their pin-type contacts penetrate the cable insulation and touch the stranded conductors in a matched phase sequence. The taps can then be wired to lighting fixtures or power outlets.

Under NEC Section 322.10 it is suitable for branch circuits that supply only small appliances and lights. It is for exposed wiring only and may not be concealed in the building structure. It is ideal for quick branch circuit wiring at field installations.

Flat conductor cable, Type FCC (1.6.7)

Type FCC is an entire branch wiring system, a lot like Type FC. NEC Article 324 covers it. FCC cable has three to five flat conductors placed edge-to-edge and separated, inside a moisture-resistant, flame-retardant insulating assembly. Accessories include cable connectors, terminators, power source adapters and receptacles.

It was designed to supply floor outlets in office areas and other commercial and institutional interiors. It runs under carpets, so no floor drilling is needed, and it can also be wall mounted. Under NEC Section 324.42(B), telephone and other communications circuits may share the same enclosure as Type FCC cable. Its main advantage is ease of installation, which makes it ideal for remodeling or expanding existing offices.

Type TC cable (1.6.8)

Type TC (power and control tray) cable follows NEC Article 336. It is two or more insulated conductors, with or without bare or fully insulated grounding conductors, covered with a nonmetallic jacket. It is listed in sizes from No. 14 AWG to 1,000 kcmil copper, or No. 12 AWG to 1,000 kcmil aluminum or copper-clad aluminum.

The T in the name means tray. TC can be used in cable trays and raceways, and may be buried directly if the sheathing is suitable. It is also good in sunlight when its markings say so. Type TC-ER identified as JP (joist pull) may be used in one- and two-family dwellings and pulled through framing members.

SE and USE cable (1.6.9)

Types SE (service-entrance) and USE (underground service-entrance) follow NEC Article 338. If the conductor type is marked on the outside of the cable, the cable's temperature rating matches the individual conductor. When that marking does not appear, the cable's temperature rating is 75°C (167°F).

  • Type SE is for aboveground installation only. For ungrounded conductors 10 AWG and smaller installed in thermal insulation, ampacity is figured at the 60°C (140°F) rating. Used as service-entrance cable, it must be installed as NEC Article 230 specifies. It may also be used as feeder and branch circuit cable. See NEC Section 338.10.
  • Type USE is for underground installation, including burial directly in the earth. In sizes No. 4/0 AWG and smaller with all conductors insulated, it is suitable for all the underground uses permitted for Type UF. It may be single conductors or a multi-conductor assembly with a moisture-resistant covering, but it is not required to have a flame-retardant covering.

USE cable may have a bare copper conductor cabled with the assembly, and single, parallel or cabled USE assemblies recognized for underground use may have a bare copper concentric conductor. These do not need an outer overall covering. When used as service-entrance cable, USE must be installed as NEC Article 230 specifies, and it may also be used as feeder and branch circuit cable. Take the time to read Article 230.

Instrumentation control wiring (1.7.0)

Instrumentation control wiring links the field-sensing, controlling, printout and operating devices that make up an electronic instrumentation control system. The style and size must be matched to the job. It usually has two or more insulated conductors, which may also have a shield and a ground wire, and an outer layer called the jacket. The conductors come in pairs, and a multi-pair cable typically has 12, 24 or 36 pairs. More on applications is in NEC Articles 720, 725 and 727.

Shields (1.7.1)

Shields protect the electrical signals in the conductors from electrical interference, or noise. They are usually aluminum foil bonded to a plastic film. If wiring is not properly shielded, noise may cause erratic or wrong control signals, false indications and improper operation of control devices.

Shield drain (1.7.2)

A shield drain is a bare copper wire in continuous contact with a specified grounding terminal. It lets all the instruments in a loop connect to one common grounding point. Always check the loop diagram to see whether the shield is to be terminated.

Shielding in instrumentation circuits is typically grounded at one end of the conductor only. That drains induced charges to ground but does not give induced current a circulating path. If the ground is not to be connected at the end you are installing, do not remove the ground wire. Fold it back and tape it to the cable. This is called floating the ground.

Jackets (1.7.3)

A plastic jacket covers and protects the parts inside the wire. Polyethylene (PE) and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) jackets are the most common. Some jackets have a nylon rip cord that lets you peel the jacket back without a knife or cable cutter, which avoids nicking the conductor insulation when you prepare for termination.

    Wire does not pull itself. After the conduit is up, the conductors still have to get through it, around bends, from box to box. This section covers the hand tools, the power tools, and the safety rules that keep people from being hurt while doing it.

    Installing conductors (2.0.0)

    Conductors are installed in all types of conduit by pulling them through, using fish tape, pull lines and pulling equipment. The module's performance task is to install conductors in a raceway system, under the supervision of the instructor.

    Trade terms in this section
    • Capstan: the turning drum of the cable puller on which the rope is wrapped and pulled.
    • Fish tape: a hand device used to pull a wire through a conduit run.
    • Mouse: a cylinder of foam rubber that fits inside the conduit and is propelled by compressed air or vacuumed through the run, pulling a line or tape.
    • Wire grip: a device used to link pulling rope to cable during a pull.

    Fish tape (2.1.0)

    Fish tape can be made of flexible steel or nylon. It comes in coils of 25' to 240' (metric sizes between 5 m and 60 m). Keep it on a reel to avoid twisting. It has a hook or loop on one end to attach to the conductors being pulled. Do not use broken or damaged fish tape. To prevent electrical shock, conductive or metallic fish tape must not be used near or in live circuits.

    The tape is fed through the conduit from its reel, usually entering at one outlet or junction box and coming out at another.

    Fish tape can get hung up in very long runs. Those jobs call for a rigid fishing tool called a rodder, available in various sizes and in lengths up to 1,000' (300 m).

    Fish tape selectionMetal fish tape generally comes in longer lengths and is the type used most often. Nylon fish tape generally comes in shorter lengths and is more flexible than metal.
    Straightening a bent fish tapeDrive five 16-penny (16d) nails into a 2 x 4, about 1 inch (25 mm) apart, in a straight line. Wind the tape through the nails in a slalom pattern and pull it through. This straightens it.

    Power conduit fishing systems (2.1.1)

    String lines can be installed with different power systems. A power system is much like an industrial vacuum cleaner. It pulls a string or rope, attached to a piston-like plug sometimes called a mouse, through the conduit. When the string comes out the other end, either the conductor or a pull rope is attached and pulled through, by hand or with power tools. The hose connection on these vacuum systems can be reversed, so the system can either suck or blow the mouse through the conduit, depending on which works better. Either way, a fish tape is then attached to the string for retrieving through the conduit.

    Connecting wire to a string line (2.1.2)

    Once the string is in the conduit run, a fish tape is connected to it and pulled back through. The conductors are then attached to the hooked end of the fish tape, or to a basket grip. In most cases, all the required conductors are pulled at one time.

    A wire grip attaches the cable to the pull tape. One kind is the basket grip (sometimes called Chinese Fingers): a steel mesh basket that slips over the end of a large wire or cable. The fish tape hooks onto the end, and the pull on the fish tape tightens the basket over the conductor.

    Pull lines (2.1.3)

    If a pull will be hard because of bends in the conduit, the size of the conductors or the length of the pull, use a pull line instead of a fish tape. A pull line is usually nylon or another synthetic fiber, made with a factory-spliced eye for easy connection to the fish tape or conductors.

    SafetyWhen using pull lines, use extreme caution and never stand in a direct line with the pulling rope. If the rope breaks, the line will whip back with great force and can cause serious injury or death.

    Safety precautions (2.1.4)

    These precautions help reduce the chance of being hurt while pulling cable:

    • To avoid electrical shock, never use fish tape near or in live circuits.
    • Read and understand both the operating and safety instructions for the pull system before pulling cable.
    • When moving reels of cable, use mechanical lifts for longer spools. For smaller spools, lift with your legs, not your back, and ask for help with heavy loads. When pulling wire by hand, spread your legs to keep your balance and do not stretch.
    • Avoid pinch points in the capstans and sheaves.
    • Select a rope with a pulling load rating greater than the estimated forces needed for the pull.

    Conductor termination (2.2.3)

    The amount of free conductor at each junction or outlet box must meet NEC rules. There must be enough free conductor so bends or terminations inside the box, cabinet or enclosure can be made to the radius the NEC specifies. The NEC specifies a minimum of 6" (150 mm) for connections to wiring devices or for splices (NEC Section 300.14). Where conductors pass through junction or pull boxes, leave enough slack for splices later.

    When a box is used as a pull box, the conductors are not necessarily spliced. They may enter through one conduit run and leave through another. The purpose of a pull box is to make pulling conductors on long runs easier. A junction box also helps with pulling, but it also provides an enclosure for splices.

    CleanupA professional always cleans up the work area, removing excess lubricant from boxes, bushings and conductors. It is good business, and it is also an NEC requirement (NEC Section 110.12).
