Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician or a builder to support a construction student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when to check the drawing, the legend or the written dimensions before acting.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not a substitute for the drawings, specifications and directions for a real job. When something on a drawing is unclear, check the legend, follow the written dimensions, and ask the instructor or supervisor. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 5

Title Block and Legend

By the end of this section your student can say what the title sheet, title block, revision block and legend are for, and where to look when a mark on a drawing is unclear.

A

Read it together

about 8 min

A set of drawings only works if you can tell what you are looking at, who made it, and what its marks mean. Two places on every sheet answer those questions: the title block and the legend.

Who makes drawings, and what goes with them

Construction drawings, traditionally called blueprints, are created by architects and engineers. An architect is a qualified, licensed person who designs the project. An engineer applies scientific principles in design and construction. Today the drawings are usually made on a computer, which is called computer-aided design, or CAD.

Drawings do not carry every detail. The specifications are the written details about the materials, the methods and the quality standards for the project.

The title sheet and the title block

The title sheet is the front cover of a drawing set. It lists project data, a sheet index and the project directory.

Every drawing sheet also has a title block, a boxed area with the key facts about the project. Looking at a title block, a student should be able to find the project name, the drawing scale, the architect or engineer, the revision date and the sheet number.

When a drawing is changed, the change is entered in the revision block. The entry includes the date of the change and the initials of the person who made it. The latest revision date on a set of drawings is found in the title block.

The legend

The legend explains the symbols, abbreviations and lines used on the drawing. It can be in a lower corner of the sheet, in or near the title block, or in a block of its own.

Key ideaAlways check the legend for each set of drawings. Many symbols are standard, but some designers use variations or company-specific notations. Never assume a symbol means the same thing on every project.

    Lines, Symbols and Gridlines

    By the end of this section your student can name the main line types, explain abbreviations, symbols and keynotes, and use gridlines and bays to name a location on a plan.

    A

    Read it together

    about 10 min

    Drawings save space by using a shared language of lines, symbols and short forms. Learn the alphabet and the sheet starts to make sense.

    The Alphabet of Lines

    The line types used on construction drawings make up a system called the Alphabet of Lines. Each kind of line has its own meaning.

    LineWhat it looks likeWhat it shows
    Object lineHeavy, solidVisible edges and outlines
    Hidden lineShort, evenly spaced dashesEdges you cannot see in this view
    CenterlineAlternating long and short dashesCenters of holes, arcs and symmetrical shapes
    Cutting-plane lineHeavy, with arrowheadsWhere a cross-section is cut
    Section linesThin, evenly spaced diagonalsThe cut surface and its material
    Break lineThin zigzag line, or thick wavy freehand lineOnly part of an object is drawn
    Dimension lineThin, ends in arrows, slashes or dotsA measurement, with the number in the break
    Extension lineThin, perpendicular to the dimension lineWhere the measurement starts and stops
    Leader lineThin, ends in an arrowhead or dotThe feature or note it points to

    Break lines save drawing space. They show that only a portion of an object is drawn, and they mark areas not included in the cutting-line view.

    Abbreviations, symbols and keynotes

    • Abbreviations are always capitalized. Examples are HVAC (Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning), NTS (Not to Scale), ELEV (Elevation) and CL (Centerline).
    • Symbols are small drawings of materials, doors, windows, and electrical and plumbing devices. Symbols are not the same on every plan in the world, and code books and specifications often include their meanings. Symbols are to be noted on the title or introductory sheet.
    • Keynotes are numbered or lettered callouts in a circle, square or hexagon. Each one points to a numbered note listed in the margin or in a schedule.

    Gridlines

    Large buildings use a grid of horizontal and vertical reference lines. Gridlines are labeled with letters in one direction (A, B, C) and numbers in the other (1, 2, 3). The purpose is to make it easy to refer to a specific location on a plan.

    • The space between neighboring gridlines is called a bay.
    • Where column gridlines cross, they locate the main structural columns and the footings that carry the load.

      Dimensions and Drawing Scale

      By the end of this section your student can read a dimension, explain what a drawing scale means, work a simple scale problem, and say why an NTS drawing cannot be measured.

      A

      Read it together

      about 8 min

      A building will not fit on a sheet of paper at full size. Dimensions give the real measurements, and scale explains how the drawing relates to the real thing.

      Dimensions

      A dimension is a measurement written as a number. In the English (imperial) system, dimensions are stated in feet and inches, such as 14'-6". In the metric system, dimensions are stated in millimeters, centimeters or meters.

      Dimensions sit between extension lines. They give exact lengths, center-to-center distances and heights. On site and civil drawings, a height can be given as an elevation (EL), which is a height above sea level or above another reference point.

      Drawing scale

      Drawings are produced at a reduced size so full-size buildings fit on standard sheets. The scale is the ratio between the drawing and the real object, and it is written like an equation.

      Scale on the sheetWhat it means
      SCALE: 1/4" = 1'-0"Every 1/4 inch on the drawing stands for 1 foot
      SCALE: 1" = 20'-0"Every inch on the drawing stands for 20 feet
      ExampleOn a site plan with SCALE: 1" = 20'-0", a driveway that measures 3 inches on the sheet is 3 x 20 = 60 feet long in real life.

      Not to scale (NTS)

      When a drawing or detail is marked NTS, it is drawn out of proportion for clarity. It cannot be scaled with a ruler. When a plan is marked NTS, the dimensions measured on the drawing cannot be used to build the project. Workers follow the written dimensions only.

        Measuring Scales

        By the end of this section your student can describe the architect's, engineer's and metric scales, say when each one is used, and explain how an architect's scale is read.

        A

        Read it together

        about 8 min

        A scale is a ruler made for drawings. The right one depends on the kind of drawing you are reading.

        The architect's scale

        The triangular architect's scale is the main scaling tool in residential and commercial construction. It combines up to 10 fractional scales on its three edges. The scales are grouped in pairs on each face, for example 1/4" and 1/8", 1/2" and 1/4", 3/4" and 3/8", and 3" and 1-1/2".

        To read a measurement, line up the whole-foot mark on the main scale, then read the leftover inches in the small divided block at the end. It is the most commonly used scale because it can combine multiple scales, turning large real measurements into small drawing measurements.

        The engineer's scale

        The engineer's scale is used mainly on civil site plans, highway drawings and utility layouts. It is based entirely on the decimal system, in multiples of 10. Its scales run from 10 to 60 parts per inch, for example 1" = 10', 1" = 20' and 1" = 100'. It is used to measure large site distances, contours and property lines.

        The metric scale

        The metric scale is a straightedge used for metric measurements, in millimeters, centimeters or meters.

        Comparing the three

        ScaleBuilt onCommon use
        Architect'sFractions of an inch to the footResidential and commercial building plans
        Engineer'sDecimals, multiples of 10Civil site, highway and utility plans
        MetricMetric unitsMetric measurements
        RememberA scale tool only works on a drawing that is drawn to scale. On a drawing marked NTS, follow the written dimensions.

          Module Section 5 of 5

          Types of Plans

          By the end of this section your student can match each type of plan to the question it answers: site, floor plan, elevation, section, structural, mechanical, plumbing and electrical.

          A

          Read it together

          about 12 min

          A full set of plans is really several kinds of drawings. Each one answers a different question, so part of reading plans is knowing which sheet to open.

          Civil (site) plans

          Civil plans give an aerial view of the building's location on the site. They include contours, trees, construction features and dimensions. Contour lines show the elevation of the ground. Cadastral plans are drawings that show property boundaries.

          Architectural plans

          Architectural plans show the design, the physical layout and the space planning of the structure.

          • Floor plans are overhead views, cut about 4 to 5 feet above the floor, that show rooms, walls, doors and built-in fixtures.
          • Elevations are flat views of exterior or interior wall surfaces, showing finish materials, door and window heights and rooflines.
          • Sections are cut-through views that reveal how the inside is built: wall framing, insulation and flashing.
          • Details are enlargements of intricate connections.
          • Roof plans show the shape of the roof and the materials that finish it.

          Structural plans

          Structural plans are prepared by licensed structural engineers to show the skeleton of the building and how it carries load. Foundation plans show concrete footings, grade beams, piers and slab thickness. Framing plans give sizes, spacing and grades of steel beams, rebar, wood joists or trusses. The materials to be used for the walls are found in the structural plan.

          Mechanical, plumbing and electrical plans

          • Mechanical plans are engineered drawings of motors, pumps, piping systems and the layout of the HVAC system.
          • Plumbing plans show the complete plumbing system. Plumbing isometrics show the layout in a three-dimensional view.
          • Electrical plans show all of the electrical supply and distribution, and can include lighting plans.
          • Schematics and P&IDs are one-line or schematic diagrams of the paths a circuit or piping system follows.
