Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Resistive Circuits

By the end of this section your student can tell series, parallel and series-parallel circuits apart, find the total resistance of each, and use Ohm’s law to find current and voltage drops.

A

Read it together

about 18 min

Real circuits are rarely a single loop with a single part. This section is about how resistors connect, how to find the total resistance, and how Ohm's law finds the voltage and current in each part.

Resistance in series

A series circuit has only one path for current. Because there is only one path, the current is the same everywhere in it. The current through the first resistor is the same as the current through the last one, and the same as the current the battery supplies.

To find the total resistance of a series circuit, add up all the resistances. A series circuit can have any number of resistors.

Series circuitWorkTotal resistance
Three resistors: 50Ω, 75Ω and 100Ω50 + 75 + 100225Ω
Three lamps: 20Ω, 40Ω and 60Ω20 + 40 + 60120Ω

The module also reminds you of the forms of Ohm's law and the power equation. Both can be used on any circuit, and each one solves for a different value.

Resistance in parallel

A parallel circuit has two or more paths that current can take. The total resistance is found by adding the reciprocals of the branch resistances (a reciprocal is 1 divided by the number), and then taking the reciprocal of that sum.

Worked exampleThree branches of 2Ω, 4Ω and 8Ω. The reciprocals are 0.5, 0.25 and 0.125, which add up to 0.875. One divided by 0.875 is about 1.14Ω. Add a fourth 2Ω branch and the reciprocals add up to 1.375, so the total drops to about 0.73Ω.

Notice what happened. In a parallel circuit, the total resistance is always less than the resistance of any single branch, and every extra branch makes the total go down, because it gives the current one more way through.

Shortcuts for parallel circuits

There are two shortcuts that save work. Both have limits, so know when each one applies.

ShortcutWhen to use itHow it worksExample
Product over sumExactly two resistors in parallelMultiply the two, then divide by their sum6Ω and 18Ω: 108 ÷ 24 = 4.5Ω
Equal resistorsAll the parallel resistors are the sameDivide one resistor's value by how many there areTwo 200Ω: 200 ÷ 2 = 100Ω

Product over sum only works for two resistors. If there are three or more that are different, go back to the reciprocal method.

Series-parallel circuits

A series-parallel circuit has some parts in series and some parts in parallel. In the series parts, use the series rules. In the parallel parts, use the parallel rules. The first job is to tell which is which, and a complicated drawing may need to be redrawn so the pattern is easier to see.

Here is a simple trick for telling the circuit type. Trace the path of current from one terminal of the source back to the other.

  • The current never splits: it is a series circuit.
  • The current splits into branches and there are no series loads: it is a parallel circuit.
  • The current splits into branches and there are also series loads: it is a series-parallel circuit.

To find the total resistance, reduce the circuit to one equivalent resistance in four steps.

  1. Redraw the circuit if needed so each series part and each parallel part is easy to spot.
  2. Find the effective resistance of each parallel group.
  3. Replace each parallel group with one resistor of that value. Now everything is in series.
  4. Add the series resistances to get the total.
Worked exampleA 50Ω resistor is in series with two 200Ω resistors that are in parallel with each other. The two 200Ω resistors are equal, so they act like one 100Ω resistor. The total is 50 + 100 = 150Ω.

Ohm's law in a series circuit

Ohm's law works on the whole circuit and also on any one part. For one part, the voltage across it equals the current through it times its resistance.

Here is the module's example. Three resistors of 20Ω, 50Ω and 120Ω are in series with a 95V source.

StepWorkResult
Total resistance20 + 50 + 120190Ω
Total current95V ÷ 190Ω0.5A
Drop across the 20Ω resistor0.5A × 20Ω10V
Drop across the 50Ω resistor0.5A × 50Ω25V
Drop across the 120Ω resistor0.5A × 120Ω60V

A voltage drop is also called an IR drop. The drops add up: 10 + 25 + 60 = 95V, which is exactly the applied voltage. In any series circuit, the voltage drops always add up to the applied voltage.

Ohm's law in a parallel circuit

In a parallel circuit, every branch connects straight to the same source. That means every branch has the same voltage across it, equal to the source voltage. Each branch is like its own little series circuit, with its own current.

  • The voltage across each branch is the same as the source voltage.
  • The branch currents can be different. A branch with less resistance carries more current.
  • The total current is the sum of all the branch currents.
  • To find one branch current, divide the source voltage by that branch's resistance.
Worked exampleTwo branches across a 240V line draw a total of 35A. One branch takes 20A. The other branch takes 35 − 20 = 15A.

Ohm's law in a series-parallel circuit

For a series-parallel circuit, work from the outside in, then back out again. The module's circuit has a 1.5V battery. Two 0.5kΩ resistors are in series, and two 1kΩ resistors are in parallel with each other. (A kiloohm, kΩ, is 1,000Ω.)

StepWorkResult
Add the two series resistors0.5kΩ + 0.5kΩ1kΩ
Reduce the parallel pair (equal, so divide by 2)1kΩ ÷ 20.5kΩ
Total resistance1kΩ + 0.5kΩ1.5kΩ
Total current1.5V ÷ 1.5kΩ1mA
Drop across each series resistor1mA × 0.5kΩ0.5V
Drop across the parallel pair1.5V − 0.5V − 0.5V0.5V
Current in each parallel branch0.5V ÷ 1kΩ0.5mA

The two equal parallel resistors share the total current evenly, 0.5mA each, and the two branch currents add back up to the 1mA total. Be careful when you simplify: every simplified value has to stay equivalent to the original, or the final numbers will be wrong.

    Kirchhoff’s Laws

    By the end of this section your student can state Kirchhoff’s current law and voltage law, and use each one to find a missing current or voltage drop.

    A

    Read it together

    about 8 min

    Kirchhoff gave electricians two simple rules, one for current and one for voltage. They sound new, but they are really the rules you already use for parallel and series circuits, written in a more general way.

    Kirchhoff's current law

    At any point in a circuit, the total current entering that point equals the total current leaving it. Current does not pile up or disappear at a junction. In a parallel circuit, this means the total current equals the sum of the currents in all the branches.

    To write it as an equation, call current flowing into a point positive and current flowing away from it negative. Then everything at the point adds up to zero.

    ExampleCurrent inCurrent outCheck
    A point with two currents in and one out5A and 3A8A5 + 3 − 8 = 0
    Point C, where the line current splits6A2A and 4A6 − 2 − 4 = 0
    Point D, where the branches rejoin2A and 4A6A2 + 4 − 6 = 0
    Key ideaCurrent in equals current out. In a parallel circuit, that is why the total line current must equal the sum of the branch currents.

    Kirchhoff's voltage law

    The algebraic sum of the voltages around any closed path is zero. In a simple circuit this means the voltage drops add up to the voltage applied. If a 240V source feeds three resistors, then the three drops must add up to 240V.

    Another way to say it is that the voltage rises and the voltage drops cancel out. A source, such as a battery, is a voltage rise. A resistor is a voltage drop.

    SourceDropsCheck
    240V30V, 120V and 90V240 − 30 − 120 − 90 = 0

    Loop equations

    Any closed path that current can follow is called a loop. A loop equation lists the voltages around one loop. You pick a direction and go around it, and each part is a rise or a drop depending on what you meet first.

    • Go around the loop in either direction. It does not matter which.
    • At each part, look at the sign (+ or −) you reach first.
    • If it is positive, count the part as a voltage rise (+). If it is negative, count it as a voltage drop (−).
    • Do not just assume that every battery is a rise and every resistor is a drop. That shortcut can fail.
    Worked exampleA 15V source drives four parts. Three resistors drop 2V, 6V and 3V, and the fourth drop is unknown. Write the loop: 15 − 2 − 6 − 3 − E = 0. Then E = 15 − 2 − 6 − 3 = 4V. The answer came out positive, so the direction of current you assumed was correct.

    In its most general form, the law says the sum of all the potential differences around a closed loop is zero. For a series circuit, that means the drops add up to the applied voltage. For a parallel circuit, it means the voltage across every branch is the same.

    Circuit typeWhat Kirchhoff's voltage law tells you
    SeriesThe voltage drops add up to the applied voltage
    ParallelThe voltage drops across all the branches are equal
