Why this guide exists

You do not need to work in construction to support a construction student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning and put it in their own words. Rigging is about moving heavy things without hurting anyone, so the habit worth building is slowing down, checking the gear and asking before acting.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 20 to 30 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Good to know before you start

This guide explains rigging equipment so your student can talk about it. It does not certify anyone to rig or lift. The module says rigging can be extremely complicated and dangerous, never to attempt a lift without the supervision of a qualified person, and to follow the manufacturer's instructions for every piece of equipment. Temperatures, ratings and limits come from the textbook. Always follow the rules that apply at your student's school or job site. The module is an elective, not required for Core completion. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Rigging and Slings

By the end of this section your student can explain what rigging is, name the three sling types, read what a sling tag must say, and describe the strengths of synthetic, chain and wire rope slings.

A

Read it together

about 30 min

Almost every construction site moves material and equipment from one place to another. The planned way of doing that is called rigging, and the sling is the link between the load and the machine that lifts it.

What rigging is (1.0.0)

Rigging is the planned movement of material and equipment from one location to another using slings, hoists or other equipment. Some jobs use a loader. Others use cranes, and the module shows two common types: overhead cranes and mobile cranes. Not every worker will rig, but nearly everyone is around it at some point.

Warning from the moduleRigging can be extremely complicated and dangerous. Do not experiment. Never attempt a lift without the supervision of an officially recognized qualified person, and only qualified persons may rig without supervision. This module does not provide any level of certification.
Around the world: heavy liftersThe module notes that the world's biggest cranes lift astonishing weights. The Taisun gantry crane in China can lift over 22,000 tons. Mammoet's stationary-ring cranes lift up to 3,200 tons, and Liebherr's LR 13000 crawler crane lifts over 3,300 tons.

Slings and the load (1.1.0)

The load is the total amount of what is being lifted, including all slings, hitches and hardware. The link between the load and the lifting apparatus is often a sling. The module covers three types: synthetic slings, alloy steel chain slings and wire rope slings.

Sling tags and rated capacity (1.1.1)

Every sling must have a securely attached identification tag. For all three types it includes the manufacturer's name or trademark and the rated capacity for the type of hitch used. Rated capacity, also called the working load limit (WLL), is the maximum load weight a sling or piece of hardware can hold or lift. It must never be exceeded, because overloading can cause catastrophic failure.

What each tag must show
SlingTag requirements
SyntheticManufacturer's name or trademark; a unique code or stock number; rated capacities for the hitches used; the type of synthetic material.
Alloy steel chainManufacturer's name or trademark; grade of steel; link size (diameter); rated load and the angle the rating is based on; sling reach; number of legs.
Wire ropeManufacturer's name or trademark; rated capacity in a vertical hitch (other hitches optional); rope diameter (optional); code or stock number (optional).
No tag, no useAny sling without an identification tag must be removed from service immediately, since its characteristics and rating can no longer be identified. A qualified person may be able to install a new tag in some cases.

Sling reach is measured from the master link, where it bears weight, to the end fitting or the lowest point of the basket. Sling legs are the parts that reach from the attachment device around the object being lifted.

Synthetic slings (1.1.2)

Synthetic slings are widely used, especially for easily damaged loads. The module covers web slings and round slings.

  • Advantages: softer and wider than chain or wire rope so they do not scratch machined or delicate surfaces; they do not rust or corrode or stain the load; most weigh less than half as much as a wire rope sling of the same rated capacity (some new ones one-tenth); they are flexible and mold to the load; they stretch much more than wire rope, which cushions shock; and loads in them are less likely to twist.
  • Concerns: do not expose web slings to temperatures above 180°F (82°C). They are vulnerable to cuts, abrasion and wear, so riggers use protective pads or softeners (old sling, fire hose, canvas or rubber work too).
  • Red warning yarns: most web slings have red-core yarns. When the red yarns are exposed, the sling is too worn or damaged to use.
Common synthetic web sling designs
DesignWhat it is
Endless (grommet)A continuous loop.
Eye-and-eyeEnds sewn to the body. Standard eyes sit on the same plane as the sling; twisted eyes sit at right angles to the body and are mainly used for choker hitches.
With end fittingsEye-and-eye slings can also have aluminum or steel hardware end fittings, in male and female types.
Round slingAn endless loop of polyester filament yarn covered by a woven sleeve (jacket).

Twin-Path (a Slingmax trademark, sometimes used generically) round slings are wound loops of yarn inside a protective jacket. Aramid round slings carry more for their size than web slings do. Jackets come in heat-resistant Nomex, polyester (light to medium duty) and bulked nylon Covermax (heavy duty, much better cut and abrasion resistance). K-Spec yarn slings weigh at least 50 percent less than a polyester round sling of the same size and capacity. Tattle-tail cords protrude from the jacket to show if the sling was stretched or overloaded, and some slings have a fiber optic cable that lights up at the far end when the strand is unbroken.

Did you know?Cuts are the main cause of synthetic sling failure, often from sharp edges on the load. Edge protectors on the load's edges help keep slings from being cut.

Alloy steel chain slings (1.1.3)

Chain slings suit high heat and rugged conditions. They are the most durable and can be adjusted over a center of gravity, but they weigh much more than wire rope slings and may be harder to inspect. Most can be used up to 500°F (260°C) with little loss of rated capacity.

  • Slings for overhead lifting must be made of alloy steel. Higher grades are safer and more durable. Common markings are 8, 80, 800 or the letter A.
  • Two basic designs: single- and double-basket slings (chain attached to the master link in permanent basket hitches, no end hardware needed) and bridle slings with two to four legs and hooks.
  • They can still be damaged by dropped loads, by sharp corners without softeners and by intense heat. Never drag chain across hard surfaces, especially concrete.

Wire rope slings (1.1.4)

Wire rope is made of high-strength steel wires formed into strands and wrapped around a supporting core. Wire rope slings are lighter than chain, can take substantial abuse, are easier to handle and tolerate relatively high temperatures. The strand wires and core slide and adjust against each other as stress changes, which protects them when bent around sheaves or loads, so a rope's rated capacity depends on it being in good condition.

Wire rope supporting cores
CoreWhat it is
Fiber coreSynthetic or natural fiber such as sisal. Can be damaged by heat at relatively low temperatures (180°F to 200°F, or 82°C to 93°C) and by caustic chemicals.
Independent wire rope coreA separate wire rope with its own core and strands; its wires are smaller and more delicate than the outer strand wires.
Strand coreOne strand the same size and type as the rest of the rope's strands.

    Sling Inspection

    By the end of this section your student can say when slings are inspected, who inspects them, and what damage makes a synthetic, chain or wire rope sling unfit to use.

    A

    Read it together

    about 30 min

    If a sling fails, the results can be devastating. So every sling is inspected, every time, no matter what tags or past inspection marks are on it. When a sling meets any rejection criteria, it comes out of service.

    Ground rules for inspection (1.2.0)

    Rejection criteria are the standards or tests used to decide an object is no longer safe and must be removed from service. Inspections must happen regardless of any existing markings or tags from earlier inspections. Along with watching for one major problem, the rigger also uses sound judgment about combinations of minor defects, which can make a sling unsafe even if none is listed. Workers who help inspect must alert a qualified person to any suspected defect.

    Synthetic sling inspection (1.2.1)

    Inspect before each use, along the entire length, both visually (looking) and manually (feeling), because some damage can only be felt. A competent person must do it. Remove the sling for any of these:

    • A missing or unreadable tag.
    • Abrasion that wears through the outer jacket, exposes the load-bearing yarn, or exposes the warning yarn of a web sling.
    • A cut or tear that severs the outer jacket or exposes the load-bearing yarns (or the warning yarn of a web sling), and any puncture.
    • Broken or worn stitching in the splice or body of a web sling.
    • A knot that cannot be removed by hand. A snag that shows warning yarn or tears the outer jacket.
    • Crushing: in a web sling it feels like a hollow pocket or depression, and in a round sling like a hard, flat spot under the jacket.
    • Overload (tensile) damage: in a web sling the weave begins to pull apart. In a Twin-Path sling, tattle-tails pulled into the jacket mean it may have been overloaded.
    • Chemical damage: discoloration, burns, melting of the fabric or jacket.
    • Heat damage: friction burns (a crusty or slick texture on webbing), glazing or charring on a round sling jacket. With heat-resistant jackets, damage may be inside: feel for brittle or fused fibers, or flex the sling and listen for cracking.
    • UV damage: bleached-out material; web slings release a powder-like substance when flexed. Round slings may just show a roughened fabric texture.
    • Loss of flexibility from dirt or abrasives, which cut the internal fibers like tiny blades each time the sling is stretched or flexed.
    Sling jacketsIf only the outer jacket is damaged, the manufacturer can replace it, then test and certify the sling. Slings removed for heat- or tension-related damage cannot be repaired and must be disposed of by a qualified person.

    Alloy steel chain sling inspection (1.2.2)

    Inspect before each use. Not every rigger is qualified to decide, so if in doubt, consult a qualified person. Chain links cannot be repaired by replacement. Remove the sling for any of these:

    • Missing or unreadable tag; cracks; heat damage.
    • Stretched links (the link gets longer and the barrels, the long sides, close up), bent links (for example from wrapping a load with sharp corners) and twisted links (from knotting or twisting the chain before load).
    • Evidence of replacement links used to repair the chain.
    • Excessive rust or corrosion that cannot be easily removed with a wire brush.
    • Cuts, chips or gouges; scraping or abrasion; excessive wear at the link-bearing surfaces.
    • Damaged end fittings such as hooks and clamps.

    Wire rope sling inspection (1.2.3)

    Inspect before each use by a competent person, who decides whether a damaged rope may be used or must be discarded. Anyone who suspects damage must tell a competent person, stopping an active lift if necessary. A wire rope sling that is simply missing its tag, or whose tag is unreadable, can be retagged by a qualified person.

    Sharp wiresBroken wires are extremely sharp and can cut or puncture skin. Never use a bare hand to handle wire rope slings or to inspect them by running a hand along them.
    Common wire rope damage
    DamageWhat it means
    Broken wiresWeaken the rope and interfere with how it moves. Counted per one-rope lay, the lengthwise distance for one strand to make one full turn around the core. External breaks are usually normal fatigue; internal or severe external breaks need close investigation and mean improper use.
    KinksDistortion of the rope. Sharp kinks stop wires from moving, so the rope must not be used. Large gradual corkscrew loops also mean removal.
    BirdcagingStrands pulled or bounced away from the core, often when a load is released too fast. Any sign means remove it from service immediately.
    UnstrandingStrands have untwisted, which weakens the rope.
    Core failureUsually seen as core strands poking out through the outer strands.
    CrushingFrom setting a load down on a sling or hammering it into place. Remove immediately.
    Corrosion and rustExcessive if there is scaling or rust that will not brush off, or if it is inside the rope.
    Heat damageWires become brittle: discoloration or melting.
    End connectionsCheck for damage, and for marks showing the connection has slipped or moved.

      Rigging Hardware and Hoists

      By the end of this section your student can describe shackles, eyebolts, lifting clamps and rigging hooks and when each is rejected, and explain how block and tackle and chain hoists lift loads safely.

      A

      Read it together

      about 30 min

      Hardware is as crucial as the crane or the sling. If the hardware that joins the sling to the load or the master link fails, the load drops just as if the crane failed. The requirements for rigging hardware are as strict as those for cranes and slings.

      Shackles (1.3.1)

      A shackle attaches an item to a load or couples slings together. It has a U-shaped body and a removable pin.

      • For overhead lifting, shackles should be forged steel, not cast. Quenched and tempered steel is preferred, and at a minimum they must be drop- or hammer-forged.
      • Every shackle must be stamped with the manufacturer's trademark, its size (set by the diameter of the body, not the pin) and its rated capacity.
      • Two classes by shape: anchor shackles and chain shackles. Pins can be screw, round or safety designs. The screw pin is the most widely used. Specialty types include wide-body shackles for heavy lifting and synthetic web sling shackles with a wide throat and wide, contoured bow.
      • Screw pin: screw it in until it is fully engaged and the shoulder touches the shackle body. A loose pin lets the shackle stretch under load. An over-tightened pin can be torqued further by load stress, which can stretch and jam the threads.
      • Position: the load goes in the bow, not on the pin. If a screw pin carries the load, shifting can twist the pin and unscrew it.
      • Never swap pins between shackles. Thread forms and capacities may not match, and there is no easy way to know how much reserve strength is left.

      Remove a shackle from use for: bends, cracks or other body damage; a wrong or substituted pin; a bent, broken or loose pin; damaged pin threads; missing or unreadable capacity and size markings. Inspect before each use.

      Eyebolts (1.3.2)

      An eyebolt has a threaded shank that screws into the load and an eye that takes the sling. For overhead lifting use drop- or hammer-forged steel. Three basic designs:

      • Unshouldered: straight vertical pulls only.
      • Shouldered: the shoulder supports the eyebolt in slightly angular pulls.
      • Swivel (hoist rings): angular pulls from 0 to 90 degrees from the horizontal plane of the load.

      In a few cases eyebolts must be torqued to a specific value when installed for lifting.

      Lifting clamps (1.3.3)

      Lifting clamps move steel plates, sheet piles, large pipe or concrete panels without slings. They bite down on the load and use jaw tension to hold it.

      • Made of forged steel and stamped with rated capacity. Some tighten as the load increases. Others use an adjustable cam that is set and tightened.
      • Each clamp lifts one item at a time. The cam and jaw must grip both sides of a single plate, so never put more than one plate in. Keep the load balanced, and use two clamps for larger loads.
      • Match the clamp type to the job. Inspect before every use.
      • Remove from use for: cracks; abrasion, wear or scraping; deformation or impact damage; excessive rust or corrosion; worn cam teeth; heat damage; loose or damaged screws or rivets; worn springs; changes in the jaw opening; missing or unreadable capacity marks.

      Rigging hooks (1.3.4)

      A rigging hook attaches a sling to a load. Hooks must be drop- or hammer-forged steel. Most have safety latches or gates, and a latch that is installed must work. Report any damaged latch to your instructor or supervisor.

      • Eye hooks: the most common end-fitting hook, with a large eye for big couplers.
      • Reverse eye hooks: the hook point sits perpendicular to the eye.
      • Sliding choker hooks: for chain slings, installed when the sling is made and used to secure the sling eye in a choker hitch.
      • Grab hooks: for chain slings, fit the chain link so choker hitches can be made and chains shortened.
      • Shortening clutches: a more efficient grab hook that fully supports the links, so no capacity is lost in the shortened leg.

      Remove a sling for hook defects: wear, scraping or abrasion; cracks; cuts, gouges, nicks or chips; excessive rust; a wider throat opening (easy to spot because the latch no longer bridges it); a twist in the hook; elongation; a broken or missing safety latch.

      Hoists (1.4.0)

      A hoist uses pulleys or gears to give a mechanical advantage so objects can be lifted that cannot be lifted by hand. Some ride on trolleys and run on electricity or compressed air.

      • Block and tackle: a simple rope-and-pulley system. Fixed pulleys and a wire rope let a rigger lift or lower a load by pulling the rope or using a winch, and it multiplies the power applied.
      • Chain hoists are manual, electric or pneumatic. Electric and pneumatic are powered chain hoists. All use a gear system turning a sprocket, with a chain and hook attached. The hoist hangs from a hook on an appropriate anchorage point or from a trolley system.

      Operating a chain hoist (1.4.1)

      • Hand chain hoist: suspend it above the load by its suspension hook or trolley mount, attach the hook to the load, and pull the hand chain. Which side of the chain you pull decides whether the load rises or falls.
      • Powered chain hoist: position it on the trolley above the load, attach the hook and use the control pad. Only qualified persons may use powered chain hoists.

      Hoist safety and maintenance (1.4.2)

      • Wear PPE: at minimum a hard hat, safety glasses, safety shoes and a high-visibility vest.
      • Make sure the load is balanced and attached correctly. Unbalanced loads can slide or shift and cause the hoist to fail.
      • Keep gears, chains and ropes clean, and lubricate gears so the wheels do not freeze up. Maintenance must be done by a competent person.
      • Never do a lift of any size without proper supervision.
      Come-along is not a lifting hoistNever use a common cable come-along for vertical overhead lifting. Use it only to move loads horizontally over the ground. Do not confuse it with a ratchet lever hoist, which has both a friction-type holding brake and a ratchet-and-pawl load control brake. A come-along has only a spring-loaded ratchet, so if the ratchet and pawl fail an overhead load falls.

        Hitches and the Emergency Stop Signal

        By the end of this section your student can name the three basic hitches, say when each is used and how it changes sling capacity, and demonstrate the Emergency Stop hand signal.

        A

        Read it together

        about 25 min

        The way a sling is arranged to hold a load is called the hitch. The right hitch depends on the load and on how it will move, because the rigger has to both secure the load and control it.

        Choosing a hitch (1.5.0)

        A hitch is the rigging configuration by which a sling connects the load to the hoist hook, built from the sling alone or with connecting hardware. The three basic types are vertical, choker and basket. Load control means moving the load safely and efficiently with proper communication and handling. Some loads go straight up and down, while others are lifted, turned in midair and set down elsewhere, so the rigger must consider the planned movement too.

        Only a qualified person selects the hitchEvery rigging operation is dangerous. A straight up-and-down lift is as dangerous as one that turns a load in midair.

        Vertical hitch (1.5.1)

        • A single vertical hitch lifts a load straight up and forms a 90-degree angle between the hitch and the load. It needs attachment hardware such as a shackle, and it lets the load rotate freely. To stop rotation, use load control such as a tag line, a rope from the load to the ground that riggers hold to keep it from swinging or spinning.
        • A bridle hitch is two or more vertical hitches attached to the same hook, master link or bull ring (a single ring that joins several slings to a hoist hook). It connects slings to one load without a spreader beam (a stiff bar used when lifting large objects with a crane hook).
        • A multiple-leg bridle has three or four single hitches on the same hook, master link or bull ring and gives the load more stability. It is always counted as only two legs carrying most of the load, with the rest balancing it.

        Choker hitch (1.5.2)

        A choker hitch is used when a load has no attachment points or when they are not practical. The sling wraps the load and passes through one eye (or through a shackle) to form a constricting loop. When a shackle is used, orient it correctly so the load never presses on the pin.

        • It reduces the sling's capacity by at least 25 percent, so account for that when choosing the sling.
        • It does not grip securely and is not recommended for loose bundles, because it tends to push loose items up and out. Forcing the choke down only raises stress on the choke leg.
        • For items more than 12 feet (about 3.5 m) long, use two choker hitches spaced far enough apart for stability.
        • A double-wrap choker wraps the sling completely around the load once more before connecting. It is useful for bundles of loose pipe or structural steel, because the load's own weight makes the constricting action. There is no need to force the sling down. Loads over 12 feet use two double-wrap chokers.

        Basket hitch (1.5.3)

        A basket hitch passes the sling around (or through) the load and puts both eyes in the hook. It creates two legs from one sling, so it doubles the sling's capacity.

        • It should not lift loose materials, and the load in it should be balanced.
        • A double-wrap basket hitch combines the constricting power of a double-wrap choker with the capacity of a basket, holding the load more tightly. It needs a much longer sling than a double-wrap choker.
        • If slings are joined, the load must touch only the sling body, never the joining hardware.

        The Emergency Stop signal (1.5.4)

        Safe lifts depend on clear communication between the crane operator and the designated signal person on the ground, by radio or hand signals. In the US, hand signals for all cranes were standardized by the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME). Normally only the designated signal person gives hand signals, but the Emergency Stop is the exception: it can be given by anyone on the ground within sight of the operator.

        How to make itExtend both arms horizontally with palms down, then quickly move the arms back and forth by repeatedly extending and retracting them.
        Around the world: hand signalsHand signals differ by country. A new ISO standard, ISO 16715, was developed with 31 countries to give multinational crews one standard set. Local crews will probably keep using the signals familiar in their region.

        The module's two performance tasks: demonstrate the ASME Emergency Stop signal, and report the load capacity of a sling and whether it is too damaged to use.
