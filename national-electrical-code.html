Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do electrical work. The NEC is for trained people working under proper guidance. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Purpose and History of the NEC

By the end of this section your student can say what the NEC is for, name key dates in its history, describe how changes are made, and name the other groups that set standards for electrical products.

Read it together

The National Electrical Code is the rulebook electricians work from every day. This section is about what the code is for, where it came from, how it gets changed, and who else sets standards for electrical products.

What the NEC is

The National Electrical Code (NEC) is also called NFPA 70. It is published by the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA). Used together with the code for your local area, it sets the minimum requirements for installing electrical systems. Local or job rules may be stricter, but they are never looser.

Its main purpose is the practical safeguarding of people and property from the hazards of using electricity. It is one of the most widely used and accepted codes in the world, and it has been translated into several languages.

Key ideaThe NEC is not a design manual and it is not an instruction book for untrained people. It is a set of minimum safety rules. Learn it with proper guidance, use the edition your employer or local area requires, and follow all local ordinances too.

A short history

YearWhat happened
1881The National Association of Fire Engineers meets in Richmond, Virginia. The idea of a national electrical code comes out of this meeting.
1895The first nationally recommended electrical code is published by the National Board of Fire Underwriters (NBFU).
1896The National Electric Light Association (NELA) works to bring insurance and utility requirements together, and helps start a conference to write one national code.
1897The first edition of the NEC is published. It is the first cooperatively produced national code.
1911The NFPA takes over administration and control of the NEC. The NBFU keeps publishing it until 1962.
1923 and 1937The NEC is rearranged and rewritten (1923), then editorially revised (1937).
1949The NFPA reorganizes the NEC into its present structure, with a Correlating Committee and Code-Making Panels.

Since 1911 the code has been updated on a regular three-year cycle.

Who keeps the code up to date

The NEC is looked after by a Correlating Committee and a group of Code-Making Panels (CMPs). Each panel has experts on particular subjects and is assigned certain articles to supervise and revise. The Correlating Committee makes sure there are no conflicts, that the different parts fit together, that NFPA rules were followed, and that a practical schedule is kept.

Panel members represent many interest groups: trade associations, electrical contractors, designers and engineers, electrical inspectors, manufacturers and suppliers, testing laboratories, and insurance organizations. No more than one-third of a panel can come from a single interest group. Government lobbyists are not on the list.

How a change gets made

Anyone can suggest a change. You do not have to be on an NFPA committee. A proposal goes through four steps.

StepWhat happens
1. Public inputThe NFPA takes proposals through its website at www.nfpa.org/X, where X is the standard number. For the NEC the address is www.nfpa.org/70.
2. Public commentPeople comment on the first draft. The technical committee reviews the comments at its second draft meeting.
3. NFPA technical meetingMore discussion is held at the annual NFPA Conference and Expo.
4. Standards Council actionThe council considers any appeals and decides whether to issue the standard. If it does, the standard takes effect 20 days later.

Other organizations that matter

The NFPA is not the only group that sets standards for electrical products.

GroupWhat it does
Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratories (NRTLs)Product safety certification labs. They test new products against established standards, and field representatives make unannounced factory visits to check products that carry their safety marks. OSHA publishes the list of recognized labs. Underwriters Laboratories (UL) is one of them.
National Electrical Manufacturers Association (NEMA)Founded in 1926. Made up of companies that make electrical equipment. It works to keep products high in quality and reliability, supports safety standards, and develops product standards for things like ratings and testing. NEMA helps develop the NEC.
International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC)Prepares and publishes international standards for electrical and electronic technologies.
Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE)A professional organization that develops international standards for electronics, telecommunications, information technology and power generation.

The IEC and IEEE, along with other groups, are working toward the globalization of electronics standards. That matters because a single product may be made, shipped and assembled in many countries.

Why it mattersUnderwriters Laboratories began after the 1893 Chicago World's Fair, where faulty, worn wiring in the Palace of Electricity helped start a fire. The engineer called in to inspect it, William Henry Merrill, pushed for standards and set up a testing laboratory. Standards usually exist because something went wrong.

    Module Section 2 of 2

    Navigating the NEC

    By the end of this section your student can describe how the NEC is laid out, tell mandatory rules from permissive rules, and follow the steps to find a specific requirement.

    A

    Read it together

    about 18 min

    The NEC is a big book, but it is organized the same way every time. Once you know the layout, you can find almost any rule by working from the chapter, to the article, to the section.

    How the book is built

    The NEC is organized into nine chapters, followed by informative annexes, an index, a schedule for the next code cycle, and information on how to submit proposed changes. Its main body starts with Article 90, Introduction, which covers the purpose and scope of the code, how it is arranged, enforcement, mandatory and permissive rules, formal interpretations, examination of equipment for safety, wiring planning and units of measurement.

    PieceWhat it isExample
    ChapterA group of articles on a broad categoryChapter 2, Wiring and Protection
    ArticleA specific subject in a chapterArticle 250, Grounding and Bonding
    PartA subdivision of some articles, marked with Roman numeralsArticle 210, Part I, General Provisions
    SectionA numbered rule inside an article, with the number after a periodSection 210.4, Multiwire Branch Circuits

    You will also see several kinds of text. Exceptions (in italics) explain when a rule does not apply. Informational Notes explain or suggest other sections to read. Figures show how an application might look, and tables give detailed requirements such as for wire sizes or box sizes.

    Mandatory and permissive rules

    Section 90.5 defines two types of rules.

    TypeWords to look forMeaning
    Mandatory“shall” or “shall not”Must be obeyed
    Permissive“shall be permitted” or “shall not be required”Allowed but not required. Usually options or alternative methods.
    Key ideaA state, city or county may add requirements, exceptions or prohibitions to the NEC. The local rules apply in that area.

    The nine chapters

    ChapterWhat it coversA few examples
    1, GeneralDefinitions and general installation requirementsArticle 100 definitions. Article 110 marking, working space, and neat and workmanlike installation (Section 110.12).
    2, Wiring and ProtectionThe information electricians need most oftenGrounded conductors (200), branch circuits and feeders (210, 215), load calculations (220), services (230), overcurrent protection (240), surge protection (242), grounding and bonding (250)
    3, Wiring Methods and MaterialsRules on what wiring methods and materials to useGeneral wiring methods (300), conductors (310), boxes and cabinets (312, 314), cables, conduit, tubing, wireways, cable trays (392)
    4, Equipment for General UseCommon equipmentFlexible cords (400), switches (404), receptacles (406), switchboards and panelboards (408), luminaires (410), appliances (422), motors (430)
    5, Special OccupanciesPlaces where sparks or heat could cause an explosion or fireHazardous locations (500 to 503), garages and fuel dispensing (511, 514), theaters (520), temporary installations (590)
    6, Special EquipmentSpecial equipment not covered elsewhereElectric signs (600), elevators (620), welders (630), electric vehicle charging (625), swimming pools (680), solar PV (690)
    7, Special ConditionsEmergency and legally required standby systems, fire alarms and other conditions that do not fit elsewhereEmergency lighting and exit signs
    8, Communications SystemsWiring for telephone, radio and TV, satellite dish and similar systemsNot subject to Chapters 1 through 7 unless they say so
    9, TablesDetailed tables used when other chapters point to themConduit bends, raceway fill, conductor dimensions

    A handy way to remember the groups: Chapters 1 through 4 give the general rules for designing and installing electrical systems and apply to nearly every installation. Chapters 5, 6 and 7 deal with special occupancies, equipment and conditions, and can modify the earlier chapters. Chapter 8 stands mostly on its own, and Chapter 9 supplies tables.

    For hazardous locations, Chapter 5 sorts areas into classes. Class I has flammable gases or vapors, Class II has combustible dust, and Class III has easily ignitable fibers. Each class has Division 1 (normal operating conditions) and Division 2 (abnormal conditions).

    Two terms to know: labeled and listed

    Article 100 defines these. Labeled equipment carries a label or mark from an organization the authority having jurisdiction accepts, which inspects production and certifies compliance. Listed equipment, materials or services appear on a list published by such an organization, which states that they meet standards or have been tested and found suitable for a specified purpose. Testing laboratories such as UL do this work.

    The informative annexes

    Annexes A through J are helpful but not mandatory.

    AnnexWhat it holds

    BAmpacity of conductors under engineering supervision
    CConduit fill tables
    DExamples of branch circuit, feeder, service and load calculations
    ETypes of building construction
    FCritical operations power systems

    Finding a specific requirement

    1. Get familiar with Articles 90, 100 and 110 first.
    2. Open the Table of Contents at the front.
    3. Find the chapter for the category you need.
    4. Find the article for your specific application.
    5. Turn to that page. Each application starts with a bold heading.

    The index at the back is also helpful when you know the topic but not the category.

    Worked exampleType SE cable on the side of a house. The Table of Contents leads to Article 230, Services, and then Section 230.51. It says to support service-entrance cable within 12 inches of the service head or the connection to the enclosure, and at intervals of no more than 30 inches.
    Worked exampleTrack lighting behind a curtain. Article 100 defines lighting track, Chapter 4 and Article 410 cover luminaires, and Part XIV covers lighting track. Section 410.151(C) lists places track is not allowed, including “where concealed.” Article 100 defines concealed as rendered inaccessible by the structure or finish of the building. A curtain can be pulled back, so the track is still accessible and is not concealed.

    When you look something up, you will usually need more than one section. The code often points you to other articles. It can be confusing at first, but time and practice make it easier.

    SafetyThe NEC is for trained people working under proper guidance. If you are not sure whether something meets the code, stop and ask a qualified person or the authority having jurisdiction.
