Why this guide exists

You do not need to use power tools to support a student who does. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning and put it in their own words. Power tools do the same work as hand tools, only faster, so the safety habits learned here matter every time one is picked up.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 20 to 30 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Good to know before you start

This guide explains power tool safety rules so your student can talk about them. It does not replace hands-on training, a supervisor's instructions, the employer's safety program or the manufacturer's directions for any tool. Sizes, pressures and limits come from the textbook. Always follow the rules that apply at your student's school or job site, and only use a tool you have been trained on. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 4

Power Drills and Drivers

By the end of this section your student can explain the four kinds of power tools, the rules that apply to every power tool, and how drills, hammer drills, impact drivers and pneumatic tools are chosen, loaded and used safely.

A

Read it together

about 40 min

Power tools do the same jobs as hand tools, only faster, which also means they can hurt you faster. The module starts with the rules that apply to every power tool, then walks through drills and drivers, the most common tools on any job site.

Four kinds of power tools (1.0.0)

The module sorts power tools by what powers them:

  • Electric tools. Run on either alternating current (AC), which plugs into a wall receptacle with a power cord, or direct current (DC) from rechargeable batteries. Battery (cordless) tools make up the vast majority of power tools on today's job sites.
  • Pneumatic tools. Powered by air. An electric or gas-powered compressor makes the air pressure. Air hammers and pneumatic nailers are examples.
  • Specialty tools. Powder-actuated tools use gunpowder to drive pins and studs into concrete and steel. They need special training and certification.
  • Hydraulic tools. Powered by fluid pressure from a hand pump or electric pump. Pipe benders, jackhammers and wrenches are examples.

Rules for every power tool (1.1.0)

Used the wrong way, any power tool can be dangerous. The module says Basic Safety (Module 00101) must be finished before this one, and PPE must be worn by anyone operating a power tool or standing near one.

  • Read and follow the tool's operating instructions. Never use a power tool without proper instruction and supervision.
  • Confirm you have the right tool for the job.
  • Disconnect the tool from its energy source when it is not in use and before maintenance or changing bits, blades or discs.
  • Inspect tools, cords and hoses regularly and report damage to your supervisor.
  • Wear safety glasses with every power tool, plus a face shield for some. Wear safety shoes. Tight-fitting gloves are required for most tools, since loose gloves are a hazard of their own. No loose clothing.
  • Keep other workers who are not involved at a safe distance.
  • Never use electric tools in wet conditions unless they are approved for it. A three-prong plug goes into a grounded receptacle, and the tool's switch is Off before you plug in.
  • Never carry an electric or pneumatic tool by its cord or hose.
  • If you get distracted or interrupted while using a tool, turn it off.
Trigger locksIf a tool has a trigger lock (a small lever or switch that holds the trigger on without finger pressure), do not use it. Locking a power tool On can be very dangerous, and some employers and job sites prohibit it.

Drills and bits (1.2.0 and 1.2.1)

A power drill is most often used to make holes by spinning a bit into wood, metal, plastic or other material. With the right attachment it can also work as a sander, polisher, screwdriver, grinder or countersink. Most drills have a pistol grip and a trigger. On a variable-speed drill, the farther back you pull the trigger, the faster it spins. A reversing switch lets you back out a bit that gets stuck.

Bits come in fractional-inch and metric sizes, and the two are not interchangeable. Twist bits run from 1/64 to 1 inch, or 0.5 to 25.0 mm. Number and letter sets (#80 up to #1, then A to Z) are used when the exact hole size matters. For example, a #8 bit is 0.199 inch, or 5.055 mm.

The chuck holds the bit. A keyed chuck opens and closes with a chuck key. Keyless chucks are typical on cordless drills. The size of the chuck limits the size of bit a drill can take.

Cordless tools and batteries (1.2.2)

Cordless tools are safer (no cord to trip over) and more mobile (they work where there is no power source). Three battery types are in common use: nickel-cadmium (NiCd), nickel-metal hydride (NiMH) and lithium-ion (Li-ion). Li-ion gives the most charge cycles and the longest run time on one charge. A charge cycle is one full charge and discharge.

  • Amp hour (Ah) is the most amps a battery can supply continuously for up to 60 minutes. A 4 Ah battery can supply 4 amps for one hour, but drawing 8 amps cuts the run time to 30 minutes.
  • Voltage rating measures the tool's power. Higher voltage means more power for the job.
  • Some cordless drills have an adjustable clutch, so the drill works as a power screwdriver without too much force on the screw. Only heavy-duty cordless drills use keyed chucks.
Going greenRechargeable batteries contain heavy metals such as nickel, lithium, cadmium, mercury and lead, which can be toxic if they end up in a landfill. If a battery is rechargeable, it is recyclable. Many cities have hazardous waste collection centers that take them.

Drill safety (1.2.3)

  • Wear PPE, especially safety glasses. Keep your hands away from the bit and chuck.
  • Disconnect the cord or battery before maintenance or changing bits.
  • Corded drills should be double-insulated with ground fault protection. A ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) opens the circuit if it detects a difference between the positive and negative flow. A tool that is not double insulated needs the third (ground) prong. Never use one with a broken ground prong.
  • Make sure the trigger is NOT on before connecting the drill to power.
  • Find out what is inside a wall or on the other side of a surface before drilling. Spaces between studs often hold wiring, plumbing or insulation.
  • Use a sharp, correct bit, tightened securely. Tie a keyed chuck's key to the power cord.
  • Hold the drill with both hands, apply steady pressure, never ram it, stay balanced, and keep it clean.
  • When tools are handed from one worker to another, disconnect them from power, and hand them off by the handle or body, never by the bit.

Loading and using a drill (1.2.4 and 1.2.5)

Pick the right drill. A right-angle drill fits in tight spaces such as between studs and joists. To load a bit, disconnect power first.

  1. Keyed chuck: open the chuck by turning it counterclockwise, insert the bit shank, hand-turn the chuck until the jaws grip, then tighten with the key (turn clockwise), in each hole on larger chucks. Remove the key. Otherwise it can fly out when the drill starts.
  2. Keyless chuck: remove the battery, open the chuck counterclockwise, insert the shank, and tighten the chuck by hand so the bit does not move.

Then, to drill:

  1. Put on PPE and mark the spot. Use a small punch in wood or a center punch in metal.
  2. Clamp or support the work. Hold the drill square (at a right angle) to the surface and start slowly. Check that the bit turns clockwise when facing away.
  3. Hold the drill with both hands and use moderate pressure. If the sound shows it is slowing a lot, ease off.
  4. Reduce pressure as the bit comes out the other side, especially in metal. If the bit sticks, release the trigger, reverse the drill and back it out gently.
Bit can grabAs a bit breaks through the far side, it may grab. With the power on, the drill tends to rotate the operator instead of the bit, so keep a firm grip. Never use the tool's motor to tighten the chuck while you are holding it.

When drilling metal, use a small amount of non-combustible cutting oil or tapping fluid on softer metals to cool the bit and leave a smoother hole. Wood does not need it. On deep holes, pull the bit partly out now and then to clear shavings.

Mag drill presses (1.2.6)

An electromagnetic (mag) drill press is a portable drill on an electromagnetic base. Once it is on a clean, flat piece of carbon steel and the magnet is turned on, the base holds the drill in place for drilling thick metal. Only trained workers should use one.

  • Clamp the material securely so it cannot become a flying or spinning object.
  • Keep power on to the magnet. Attach a Do Not Unplug tag to the cord.
  • Use a safety chain, since losing power while the drill is running can make it fall and hurt the operator.
  • Check whether the site requires shields or safety lines.

Hammer drills (1.3.0 to 1.3.2)

A hammer drill pounds lightly while it turns, so it drills concrete, brick or tile faster than a regular drill. With the hammering turned off it can do basic drilling. It needs bits built to take pounding, with very hard carbide tips (tungsten carbide and cobalt, baked on at extreme heat).

Rotary hammers look similar but do much heavier jobs. They turn slower than hammer drills and strike harder and less often. Most use special bit-holding designs (SDS Plus and SDS Max are two) in a chuck that is not keyed, so the masonry bit must match the chuck.

  • Wear PPE with safety glasses and hearing protection. Use both hands, and never run one with one hand.
  • Keep away from flammable material, since sparks may ignite it. Clamp small work and never hold it with your body.
  • Use a masonry bit. A regular bit can be damaged or destroyed in concrete.
  • Concrete dust brings the silica rules from Basic Safety.
  • To drill: mark the spot, set the blows per minute, start slow, drill a guide hole about 4 inches deep, bring the speed up to finish, then clean out the hole.

Impact drivers (1.3.3 to 1.3.5)

An impact driver is lighter and more compact than a drill or hammer drill, and it delivers more torque (turning force). That makes it great for driving or loosening many screws, nuts and lag bolts, and for harder materials such as pressure-treated lumber, hardwood or steel. It usually has no variable speed, so it is less suited to delicate work. When the bit meets resistance, it applies greater force. It holds bits in a hexagonal collet instead of a chuck because of the extra torque.

Too much torqueAn impact driver can split, break or rip through softer or more brittle material. Be extra careful with wood and soft material, and check that the fastener will not be damaged by the torque.

To use one: put on PPE, choose the bit, disconnect power, pull the collet forward and insert the bit, reconnect power, set the direction (tighten or loosen), seat the bit or socket squarely on the fastener, and start slowly. Release the trigger when the fastener is attached or removed.

Pneumatic drills and impact wrenches (1.4.0)

Pneumatic tools get their power from an air hose connected to a compressor, and they have more power for their weight than similar electric tools. Pneumatic drills are compact (no motor) and are used where there is no electricity or where a high rate of production is needed. Their size, such as 1/4, 3/8 or 1/2 inch, is the largest bit shank the chuck can grip, not the drilling capacity.

Impact wrenches (pneumatic, or electric corded and cordless) tighten and loosen nuts and bolts. The electric ones are not as strong as pneumatic wrenches but are easier to maintain, since there are no hoses or compressors to service. Some of the best cordless models reach up to 1,500 ft-lbs of breakaway torque.

  • Wear PPE including eye, hand and ear protection, and read the manufacturer's instructions.
  • Turn off the air and disconnect the hose before changing attachments or doing maintenance.
  • Secure the hose connection and add a whip check where required. An unsecured air hose can whip around violently.
  • Use only impact sockets made for pneumatic wrenches. Hand sockets can cause damage and injury.
  • Set the compressor regulator to the right pressure, keep the air clean and dry, keep the tool lubricated (many need an oiler), and use a backup wrench when tightening a nut and bolt.
Air pressureThe air pressure that runs a pneumatic drill is higher than OSHA recommends for cleaning a work area. Misusing pressurized air can injure people and damage tools.

    Power Saws

    By the end of this section your student can name the common power saws, choose the right blade, and explain how to use each one safely, including how to avoid kickback.

    A

    Read it together

    about 30 min

    Using the right saw for the job makes the work much easier, and the right blade for the material matters just as much. This section covers five kinds of power saws: circular, jigsaw and reciprocating, portable band, miter and cutoff, and table saws.

    Circular saws (2.1.0)

    The portable circular saw cuts lumber and boards to size. Many people still call it a Skilsaw, after the company that introduced it. It comes in corded and cordless models. The size is the diameter of the blade. In the US, blades run from 3 3/8 to 16 1/4 inches. The most popular size for corded saws is 7 1/4 inches, and many smaller cordless saws use a 6 1/2 inch blade. The hole in the center of the blade fits on the saw's arbor (shaft), and the most common arbor size is 5/8 inch.

    Metric saws use blades of 165, 190 or 235 mm, with a 20 mm arbor (some have 25.4 mm). Fractional-inch and metric blades are not interchangeable. A 7 1/4 inch blade with a 5/8 inch hole is not the same as a 190 mm blade with a 20 mm hole.

    • Most weigh 7 to 14 pounds. The trigger switch is in the handle, and the motor is in a rigid housing. The blade's teeth point in the direction it spins.
    • Two guards protect you. The upper guard shields you from flying debris and the spinning blade. The lower guard is spring-loaded and slides up and under the upper guard as the saw is pushed forward.
    • Standard steel blades need regular sharpening. Carbide-tipped blades stay sharp much longer, but you have to pick the right one for the job.

    BladeWhat it does
    RipCuts with the grain. Square chisel teeth, generally larger than other blades' teeth.

    Circular saw safety and care (2.1.1)

    • Wear PPE, especially safety glasses, and always know where the blade is in relation to your hands and body.
    • Before connecting power, make sure the blade is tight and the guard works. Check what is inside a wall or behind a partition before cutting.
    • Keep both hands on the grips. Never force the saw, since that causes binding and overheating. Never reach under the work and never stand directly behind it. Stand to one side.
    • Clamp small pieces. Never hold material with your hands.
    • Always know where the power cord is. Cutting through it can cause electrocution.
    • The most important maintenance is at the lower guard. Sawdust builds up and makes it stick, and then a bare blade may still be turning when you set the saw down. Disconnect power, let the saw cool and clean the guard's track. Do not oil or grease it. Keep blades clean and sharp to reduce friction and kickback. Take care with flammable cleaners such as isopropyl alcohol and mineral spirits.

    Using a circular saw (2.1.2)

    1. Put on PPE. Secure and support the material. If it is not heavy enough to stay put, weight it or clamp it.
    2. Mark the cut. Unplug or remove the battery, then set the blade depth to the thickness of the wood plus 1/4 inch.
    3. Set the front edge of the baseplate on the work, with the guide notch and the blade in line with the mark.
    4. Start the saw, let it reach full speed, and then move it slowly forward.
    5. Plan for the kerf, the channel the blade leaves, about 1/8 inch (3.2 mm) wide. Line the blade up with the waste side of the line, and mark an X on the waste side so the piece does not end up short.
    6. Grip the handles firmly with two hands. When the cut is nearly done, the guide notch moves off the work, so use the blade as the guide. Release the trigger and wait for the blade to stop before setting the saw down.

    If the saw wanders off the line, stop, back up slightly and restart. Do not force it.

    The worm-drive sawA worm-drive saw drives the blade from the rear through two gears. It delivers much more torque, so it can cut a double thickness of lumber, but it is almost twice as heavy as a conventional circular saw and should be used only by an experienced craftworker.

    Jigsaws and reciprocating saws (2.2.0)

    Both saws can make straight and curved cuts, and both have a blade that moves back and forth. Reciprocating means moving backward and forward on a straight line.

    • Jigsaw. Very fine blades, so it suits delicate and intricate work, such as cutting patterns or irregular shapes in wood or thin metal. The blade moves up and down, so it cuts only on the upstroke (about half the time it runs). A wide baseplate keeps the blade lined up and stops the work from vibrating, and many tilt for beveled cuts. Fine-toothed blades are for thin material and smoother cuts, and coarse blades are for faster cuts in thicker material. Low speed is for hard material and high speed is for soft.
    • Reciprocating saw. Often called a Sawzall, after a trademark. It was the first saw designed as an electric hacksaw. It is for heavier work than a jigsaw, with longer, tougher blades, so it is great for demolition. It can saw through walls or ceilings and open up space for windows, plumbing lines and more. The low-speed setting is best for metal, and high speed is for wood and other soft materials.

    Blades are often labeled for the material or use. As a general rule, metal-cutting blades have more and smaller teeth per inch than wood-cutting blades.

    Jigsaw and reciprocating saw safety (2.2.3 and 2.2.4)

    • Wear PPE with safety glasses. Check what is inside a wall or partition before cutting.
    • Disconnect power before changing a blade or doing maintenance. Seat the blade in the collar as far as it will go before tightening the setscrew, and look for broken pieces of an old blade stuck in the collar.
    • Use a sharp blade made for the material (a metal blade for metal). Never force the blade. Forcing or leaning on it can throw you off balance. Beeswax on the blade helps with tight turns and cuts the chance of breaking it.
    • Clamp the work to sawhorses or in a vise to reduce vibration. To start a cut in the middle of a board, drill a hole large enough for the blade.
    • Start the saw before the blade contacts the work. Do not lift the blade out of the cut while the saw is running. Let the blade stop before taking it out.
    • Use both hands on a reciprocating saw, with the baseplate firmly against the work. Otherwise the pull from the blade's grip can jerk the saw out of your hands. If the blade jumps back when it first hits, stay steady. Forcing it in is a common way to break blades.

    Portable band saws (2.3.0)

    A portable band saw cuts pipe, metal, plastic, wood and irregular shapes, and it is especially good for heavy metal. On the job site it is used almost only for metal. The blade is a thin, flat, one-piece loop that runs in one direction around guides, and it must be the right length for the pulleys. Blade coarseness is rated in teeth per inch (TPI). More teeth per inch gives a smoother cut, and thicker material needs a coarser blade, but a blade that is too coarse can lose teeth. It cuts best at low speed, because high speeds make the teeth rub instead of cut, and the heat shortens blade life.

    It cuts toward youA portable band saw always cuts in the direction of the user. Keep hands and fingers out of the blade's path and never use your thumbs to push material toward the blade.
    • Use only a band saw that has a stop, and set the stop firmly against the work. The blade's motion can pull the saw toward the stop, so a loose stop makes the saw jump when you start it.
    • Disconnect power before maintenance or blade adjustment. Check blade tension and that both blade guides are close to the blade.
    • Before cutting lines or pipes, confirm they do not contain hazardous or combustible material.
    • Let the saw do the cutting. Little or no downward pressure is needed. Turn it off and let the blade stop before backing out of a cut, and plan cuts so you do not have to back out of curves.

    Miter saws and abrasive cutoff saws (2.4.0)

    • Power miter saw. A miter box combined with a circular saw for straight and miter cuts. The blade pivots horizontally and locks to cut angles from 0 to 45 degrees right and left, with preset stops for common angles.
    • Compound miter saw. Also tilts the blade vertically, so it can make a compound cut (a bevel and a miter at once).
    • Compound-slide miter saw. Has a rail so the blade assembly can slide forward and back, which lets it cut wider material.
    • Abrasive cutoff saw (chop saw). Makes straight or angled cuts in angle iron, flat bar and channel. The abrasive blade is 10 to 18 inches (250 to 350 mm) across and spins so fast that the friction is hot enough to burn through the material. Expect streams of sparks.
    • Wear PPE with safety glasses. Do not wear a watch or jewelry. Keep other workers away.
    • Lock the blade at the right angle. Keep fingers clear and never adjust the saw while it runs. Check the blade and all guards, and never pull back a guard to see the cut.
    • Keep flammable materials such as chemicals and rags away from sparks. Support the far end of long material.
    • A blade's maximum rpm must meet or beat the saw's spindle speed. Dust from some materials brings the silica rules from Basic Safety.
    • To cut: set the angle, hold the material firmly against the fence, raise the blade arm and start the saw, wait for full speed, then lower the blade into the work. When done, turn off the saw and raise the arm.

    Table saws (2.5.0)

    A table saw has a circular blade that sticks up through a slot in the table. Saws are sized by blade: 10-inch for most carpentry, 8-inch for smaller material, 12-inch for larger jobs. They are used for ripping (cutting lumber to a width) and crosscutting (cutting to a length). The fence lets you feed lumber into the blade in a controlled way, so table saws make precise cuts. The blade can be raised or lowered, and some tilt up to 45 degrees. Many are now portable, with folding legs, and some are cordless.

    • Wear PPE, use a blade made for the job, and make sure it is sharp. Check that the guard moves freely before connecting power.
    • Use a push stick or board when your hand would pass near the blade. Keep the bench clear.
    • When crosscutting, do not use the fence without a block between the work and the fence, so the cutoff piece does not get trapped between blade and fence.
    • Expect kickback when ripping. Do not stand in line with the blade. Stand to one side. Keep the board flat on the bench and snug against the rip fence so it cannot bind.
    • Disconnect power to adjust the blade or do maintenance.
    • To rip: fit a rip blade, set the blade no more than 1/4 inch above the thickness of the material, set the fence to the width you need, then slowly push the board along the fence. Use a push stick for the last few inches. Turn the saw off when done.
    Wait for full speedDo not push lumber into the blade before it reaches full speed. Contacting it any earlier may cause kickback or serious injury.

      Grinders and Oscillating Multi-Tools

      By the end of this section your student can name the kinds of grinders, explain the safety rules for grinding and describe what an oscillating multi-tool can do.

      A

      Read it together

      about 25 min

      Grinders and oscillating multi-tools are two of the most useful tools in a tool bag, and grinders throw high-speed sparks and debris. Eye and face protection come first.

      Before you grindWear safety goggles and a face shield. Clear the area of combustibles such as rags and flammable liquids, and keep a fire extinguisher close. Clothes should be snug, with no cuffs at the wrists or ankles.

      Types of grinders (3.1.0)

      Grinders the module describes
      GrinderWhat it does
      Angle grinderAlso called a side or right-angle grinder. The disc is at a right angle to the motor shaft. Grinds away hard, heavy material and surfaces such as pipes, plates and welds.
      End grinderAlso called a horizontal or pencil grinder. The disc turns in line with the motor shaft. Smaller. Smooths the inside of material such as pipe.
      Detail grinderAlso called a die grinder. Small attachments called points fit on its arbor to smooth and polish intricate metal work. The shaft of a point is called the spindle (1/16 to 1/4 inch, or 3 mm and 6 mm in metric).
      Bench grinderStationary, usually with two wheels. Used for grinding, rust removal and metal buffing, and for renewing worn edges and keeping cutting tools sharp, such as smoothing the mushroomed head of a cold chisel.

      The main difference between an end grinder and a detail grinder is power. The end grinder has more.

      Heavy-duty grinder wheels run from 6 3/4 to 10 inches (150 to 250 mm) across, and each wheel has a maximum speed in rpm. Never use a wheel above its rated speed. Its rating must be equal to or faster than the maximum speed of the tool. A bench grinder has an adjustable tool rest that supports the work, set only 1/8 inch (about 3 mm) from the wheel.

      Tool restsNever adjust a tool rest when the grinder is on or the wheels are spinning. Disconnect power before adjusting any grinder.

      Grinder safety (3.1.1)

      Angle, end and detail grinders

      • Wear PPE, especially gloves and safety glasses. No loose clothing or jewelry. Disconnect power before maintenance. Keep your hands away from the wheel.
      • Use standing screens near walkways, other workers or storage so sparks do not hit others or ignite flammable material. Aim sparks and debris away from coworkers. Use a flame-retardant blanket to catch sparks when grinding on a platform.
      • Never use a grinder without the manufacturer's guard around the wheel. Choose a disc made for the job.
      • A new wheel's maximum rpm must be equal to or greater than the grinder's maximum speed. Check that the wheel is secure and in good condition. Cracked discs can disintegrate instantly, so inspect for cracks.
      • Individual wires can fly off a wire wheel at high speed and cause injury.
      • Have firm footing and a firm grip with both hands. Let the grinder reach full speed before grinding. After shutting off, stay until the disc stops.

      Bench grinders

      • Wear PPE, especially gloves and safety glasses. Keep the area clean, since grinding metal makes sparks.
      • Keep tool rests within 1/8 inch (about 3 mm) of the wheel, so the work cannot get wedged between rest and wheel. Keep 1/4 inch (about 6 mm) between the wheel and its guard (the spark arrestor).
      • Never use a wheel above its rated speed. Check for chipped edges and cracks before mounting a wheel.
      Do not grind soft metals or woodOn a grinding wheel, soft metal or wood wedges between the grit and builds stress that can shatter the wheel. Grinding tungsten carbide tools with ordinary wheels is also a bad idea and most likely damages the wheel.

      Using a grinder (3.1.2)

      1. Put on PPE, especially a face shield, and secure the material in a vise or clamp it to the bench.
      2. Angle grinder: one hand on the handle and one on the trigger. End or detail grinder: grip the handle at the shaft end with one hand and cradle the motor end with the other.
      3. Let the grinder reach maximum speed before grinding. Do not force it or overload the motor. Heavy pressure drops the rpm.
      4. Turn it off and wait until the wheel stops before putting it down. Finish by removing loose material with a wire brush.

      Bench grinder: use the tool rest to support the work, check that the grinder is firmly secured, let it reach full speed, and keep the metal cool because too much heat destroys its temper (hardness). Always work on the face of the wheel, not the side.

      Grinder attachments and accessories (3.2.0)

      Beyond grinding, cutting, sanding and polishing, grinders take many attachments. Guards and shields add protection for special discs (diamond discs, flap discs, cup brushes), side handles improve grip, and dust shields can connect to a vacuum hose. Adapters and backing pads go on the arbor so different sanding discs, wheels and buffing pads can be used.

      Coring bitCutting holes in stone or composites such as countertops.
      Burr bitRemoving rough edges and burrs from the inside and outside of pipes and other objects.

      Burr bits run at high rpm and can catch and make the grinder shift around, so you need steady hands. They also heat up the bit and the work, so work in short bursts. Whatever the attachment, it must fit the grinder, be rated for the right rpm, and be used the way the maker says.

      An abrasive is a material such as sandpaper that wears material away. Grit is the sand-like material used to make it, graded by texture. The higher the grit number, the finer the abrasive.

      Oscillating multi-tools (3.3.0 and 3.3.1)

      An oscillating multi-tool (OMT) looks like a handheld grinder but does a wider range of jobs because its arbor accepts many attachments. It moves them back and forth at up to 21,000 oscillations per minute, so fast that you cannot see the motion. That, plus its small size, makes it perfect for small cutting and sanding jobs, and it can fit into tight, confined places.

      • Typical jobs: scraping grout, cutting metal and PVC pipe, removing adhesives and paint, sanding and preparing surfaces, undercutting low baseboards, cutting small openings in drywall, and cutting pipe where there is little room for larger tools.
      • Most on job sites today are cordless.
      • Wear PPE, especially gloves and safety glasses. Disconnect power when changing an attachment or doing maintenance. Make sure the attachment is made for the job. Clamp the work. No loose clothing or jewelry. Keep your hands away from the attachment while it is on.

        This last section covers two jobs that power tools do on every site: fastening with nailers, and lifting or moving heavy loads with hydraulic jacks. Both use a lot of force, so they call for care.

        Power nailers (4.1.0)

        Power nailers (nail guns) greatly speed up installing wallboard, molding, framing members and shingles. Pneumatic nailers run on compressed air from a compressor through an air line. They are made for specific jobs: roofing, framing, siding, flooring, sheathing, trim and finishing. Each uses a specific type of nail, in coils or strips, loaded into the gun. A key safety feature is that the nailer will not fire unless it is pressed against the material and the trigger is pressed.

        Pressure limitNever exceed the maximum operating pressure of a pneumatic nailer. It will damage the nailer and may cause injury.
        Power screwdriversPower screwdrivers (battery-powered in the module's example) speed up drywall, floor sheathing, decking, fencing and cement board. A chain of screws feeds automatically into the firing chamber. Most have a back-out feature to drive screws out.

        Powder-actuated tools

        Powder-actuated tools drive steel pins or threaded studs into masonry and steel to anchor static loads to steel and concrete beams, walls and so on. They do not need compressed air. They look and fire like a gun, using a gunpowder load (typically .22, .25 or .27 caliber). How deep the pin or stud goes depends on the density of the base material and the strength of the powder load.

        • Operators must be trained and certified by the manufacturer of the tool.
        • Do not fire into easily penetrated materials. The fastener can pass through and become a flying missile on the other side.
        • If a powder load fails to fire, wait 10 seconds, remove it from the tool, and dispose of it in a bucket of water or oil.
        • Local codes may prohibit powder-actuated tools because of safety, seismic concerns or because the building is occupied, so check with local authorities.
        • Load the drive pin before the charge. Do not fire close to the edge of the material (especially concrete), since pieces can chip off and hit someone. Never place your hands behind the material being fastened.

        Cordless electric nailers are an alternative. They are battery operated, strong enough to fasten pins, straps and clips to concrete and steel, with no hose and no need for the training and certification that powder-actuated tools require. They may not match the power of a powder-actuated tool, but they handle most concrete fastening jobs.

        Power nailer safety (4.1.1)

        • Wear PPE, especially safety glasses. Read the manufacturer's manual.
        • Pay close attention to where the gun points. Never point a nail gun at another worker or any part of your body. Treat every nailer as loaded and ready to fire.
        • Pick the right nailer and fastener, and do not load the tool until you are ready to fire.
        • Disconnect power before loading or repairing it. Never leave a pneumatic nailer connected to a compressor hose or power when not in use. Keep it oiled as the manufacturer says.
        • Check for pipes, wiring, vents and other things behind wallboard. Be careful nailing into paneling or thin surfaces, since the fastener may go through and strike another worker.

        Using a power nailer (4.1.2)

        1. Put on PPE. Disconnect a pneumatic nailer from the air hose.
        2. Inspect for damage and loose connections. Load the fastener. (For a powder-actuated tool, feed the pin or stud into the piston first, then the cartridge.)
        3. Connect a pneumatic nailer to the air hose and set the compressor to the recommended pressure. Most nailers run at 70 to 120 psi (about 480 to 830 kPa).
        4. Test it on scrap. If the nail does not go in deep enough, follow the manufacturer's steps to raise the power.
        5. Hold a pneumatic or battery nailer firmly against the material and press the trigger. Press a powder-actuated tool firmly against the mounting surface.
        6. When done, disconnect the power. Turn off the air and disconnect the hose, remove the battery from cordless nailers, and remove the powder charges from powder-actuated tools.
        Not a toyA nail gun is not a toy. Nails can pierce a hand, leg or eye. Never point one at anyone, and use it only the way the manufacturer says.

        Hydraulic jacks (4.2.0)

        Hydraulic tools are used when a job needs extreme force applied in a controlled way. They do not work fast, but the forces can damage equipment or injure people if the manufacturer's procedures are not followed exactly. Hydraulic jacks are portable. They move or lift heavy equipment and material, position heavy loads precisely, and straighten or bend frames. A hydraulic jack has two basic parts: a pump and a cylinder (also called a ram).

        • Jack with an internal pump. A general-purpose jack in many capacities. Pumping the handle pressurizes the hydraulic fluid, and that pressure moves the cylinder to lift or move the load.
        • Lever-operated pump kit. Often called a Porta-Power, after one brand. A kit has a hydraulic hose, a hand pump and attachments for different jobs. It is especially useful for horizontal jacking.
        • Cylinder ratings. Rated by the weight they can lift (tons or metric tonnes) and the distance they can move it, called stroke. Cylinders can lift more than 500 tons, and strokes run from 1/4 inch (about 6 mm) to more than 48 inches (about 122 cm).

        Hydraulic jack safety (4.2.1)

        • Wear PPE, especially safety glasses, and follow the manufacturer's guidelines.
        • Check the area so other workers are clear and the load will clear all obstacles. Stay clear of the object being lifted in case the load slips off.
        • Use the right jack and never exceed its lifting capacity. Check the fluid level before use and watch for leaks. With a Porta-Power, keep the hose from twisting or kinking, and do not move the pump while the hose is under pressure.
        • Put the jack base on a solid, even, level surface, never bare soil or anything that can compact or shift. Center the load over the jack so its weight is spread evenly.
        • If the load could move, chock and restrain it. Never jack metal against metal. Use wood as a buffer.
        • Do not use an extension bar (a cheater) or step on the handle for extra leverage.
        • Never leave a jack under a load as a support. Block the load up as you lift, so it falls only a short distance if the jack fails, and add blocking at the final height so the load is supported while the jack comes out.
        • Remove the jack's lever any time you are not pumping, to reduce tripping hazards.
