Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do any hands-on trade work. The skills here are for any worker, but job-site safety rules always come first. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 3

Opportunities in the Construction Industry

By the end of this section your student can describe the roles in the construction business, write the key parts of a resume, find job openings, and decide whether a job is the right fit.

A

Read it together

about 16 min

Getting a job in construction takes more than finishing your training. You need to know where the opportunities are, present yourself well on paper, and find the right fit.

The construction business

Construction runs on specialized skills. Laborers (blue collar) and professionals (white collar) work together, and the industry reaches around the world. Skilled tradespeople are needed on big building sites and in places like war zones. There are also trade jobs that are not new construction at all, such as wind turbine technicians, welders, powerline workers, crane operators and instrumentation technicians.

Companies of all sizes specialize in one or more kinds of work. A small company might install heating and cooling systems in homes, while a larger one handles the systems for a hospital. Building one large project takes many roles.

RoleWhat they do on a large project
ArchitectCreates and designs the shape, color and spaces of the structure
Civil engineerStudies the drawings to work out how to build the design, suggests changes, and picks suitable materials
Project managerPlans, coordinates and controls a project's progress, or a part of it
Heavy equipment operator, concrete pourer, crane operatorDig the site, pour the foundation, lift the steel beams
Ironworker, welderCut and fit the steel, and secure the framework
Electrician, plumber, HVAC technician, pipefitterInstall wiring and devices, fixtures, comfort systems, and water and process piping
Carpenter, instrumentation technician, landscape designerFrame the walls, install measuring and control instruments, and plan the look of the grounds
Good to knowA company spends about 35 percent more than a worker's wage once benefits like healthcare and vacation are counted. A worker paid $25.00 an hour costs the company about $8.75 more per hour.

Many companies have a mission statement, which explains how the company does business. Learn about a company before you apply. The more you know about what matters to them, the better your interview answers will be.

Entering the construction workforce

The first step of a job search is a resume that gets an employer's attention. Good resumes follow these guidelines.

  • Use a readable font, like Times New Roman or Arial.
  • Write your objective using key words from the job description.
  • List jobs in chronological order, with your current or most recent job first.
  • Use short sentences and bulleted lists to describe your skills.
  • Include certifications and training credentials, such as NCCER training certificates or wallet cards, or an automated external defibrillator (AED) certification.
  • Make sure your contact information is current, accurate and easy to find.
  • Keep personal information, such as hobbies or volunteer work, brief.
  • Check for inaccurate or missing dates. Large gaps may need an explanation.
  • Have someone proofread it for spelling, typos and grammar.
  • Give clear references, or say that a list can be provided on request.
Worked exampleA resume that starts with a plain, readable font, an objective that uses the same words as the job posting, and a work history with the newest job first is easy for an employer to follow. A resume with a fancy, unusual font and a missing certification line is harder to read and sells the worker short.

Look for jobs that match your skill level. Applying for positions far above your skill level usually leads to disappointment. Most employers advertise online and take online applications. You can use a job-search website or go to a company's own site. Libraries and unemployment offices have public computers, and local newspapers and trade magazines list openings too.

One of the most important and productive ways to find a job is networking. Studies show that as many as 70 percent of available jobs are never publicly advertised, yet job seekers spend about 70 percent of their time looking at public postings. Tell loved ones and friends, use social media, name companies you would like to work for, and look for contacts inside them.

References are people who can confirm that you have the skills, experience and work habits on your resume. Ask your current supervisor, co-workers and friends who know you well, and give accurate contact information. Both personal and professional references are recommended.

Before you accept

Ask yourself these questions before saying yes to an offer.

  • Is the salary enough to meet my needs?
  • Does the benefits package cover what I need?
  • Will the work be interesting and challenging, but not more than I can handle?
  • Does the company have a good reputation, and a good safety record?
  • Is travel required, and can I agree to it?
  • Does the company offer training and advancement?

Some trades test your skills before an offer. Many companies require a drug test. You must show identification, such as a driver license, Social Security card or passport. After you accept, get an organizational chart, which shows the structure of the company and who is responsible for what.

    Critical Thinking and Problem Solving

    By the end of this section your student can describe critical thinking, name the barriers to solving problems, use the five-step process, and name the four kinds of planning and scheduling problems.

    A

    Read it together

    about 18 min

    Problems on a job site will happen. The ability to think them through and fix them is one of the most valuable skills a worker can have.

    Critical thinking

    Critical thinking means evaluating information and then using it to reach a conclusion or make a decision. You can think critically by following these steps.

    • Do not let personal feelings get in the way. Put them aside and stay objective.
    • Work out the cause (why it happened) and the effect (what happened as a result).
    • Think about the expertise of your sources, and ask experts and people you trust.
    • Compare new information with what you already know. If it does not fit, question it and try to separate fact from fiction.
    • Weigh the merits of each option.
    Worked exampleA co-worker tells you something about a work procedure that does not match what you were taught. Do not just accept it, and do not just brush it off. Question it, ask someone with experience, and look it up in a trusted source before deciding who is right.

    Barriers to problem solving

    BarrierWhat it looks like and what to do
    Closed-mindednessDistrusting any new ideas. Be open, because the best answer may be one you would never have thought of.
    Personality conflictsIgnoring good advice because you do not like the person. Separate the message from the messenger.
    Fear of changeOften the lack of change is the real problem. Embrace change and you will keep finding new solutions.

    A problem is a difference between how something is and how it should be. Do not ignore it. Treat it as a chance to show your skills.

    The five-step problem-solving process

    StepWhat to do
    1. Define the problemKnow exactly what it is, so you do not solve the wrong problem
    2. Analyze and explore alternativesGather information and opinions, and compare cost, time and quality, plus short- and long-term results
    3. Choose a solution and plan itPick the best option and plan the tools, materials, tasks, who does what, and how long each takes
    4. Put it into effect and monitorFollow the plan closely to be sure it moves toward the result you want
    5. Evaluate the final resultIf it does not work, go back to Step 3 and choose another solution
    Worked exampleA welding crew needs several hundred electrodes by the end of the shift, but the nearest tool room has only 60. You could bike to the other tool room, or call its attendant and ask him to bring them. You choose to call, take the 60 you have back to the crew, and suggest that attendants inventory consumables at the end of every shift. Afterward, no tool room runs low. That plan solved the short-term problem and kept it from happening again.

    Not every solution works. When one fails, do not lose your confidence. Go back, think through the options, and move forward. When the problem is solved, look back for lessons.

    Planning and scheduling problems

    Every task has a logical place in the project schedule. Before you start, know what is expected, where your work begins and ends, which materials to install, and what order the steps go in. Extra time is built into most schedules, but complex problems can still throw a project off track. Project managers and site planners are generally responsible for keeping a project on schedule, but team members may be asked to help solve problems. Problems usually fall into four categories.

    CategoryTypical problems
    MaterialsErrors in quantity or type, delivery delays, backorders, waste, theft or vandalism. If a substitute is not in the contract specifications, the change must go through an approval process, which takes time.
    EquipmentNot available on the scheduled day, no qualified operator, breakdowns, extended maintenance. If a crane is not available, ironworkers and welders can stand idle.
    ToolsBreakage, damage, loss, theft, or a lack of skilled users. Workers usually bring common tools. Special expensive tools are usually the employer's job.
    LaborThe most important and often one of the most expensive parts of a project. Problems include tardiness, absenteeism, lack of experience, too few workers, and idle time.

    For delays, watch ahead in your part of the project. If you see a possible cause of delay, tell your supervisor right away. Know your responsibilities, be ready to solve the problem yourself if it is your job, and keep your supervisor updated on delicate situations.

    TipCheck where information comes from. Web addresses ending in .gov or .us are government pages, good for codes and regulations. Addresses ending in .org are usually nonprofits, which you can trust if they set the standards for your industry. For product information, go straight to the manufacturer's website.

      Relationship and Social Skills

      By the end of this section your student can describe good personal habits and work ethic, handle conflict and criticism, explain what to do about harassment, bullying and drug and alcohol abuse, and describe teamwork and leadership styles.

      A

      Read it together

      about 28 min

      Skill gets you hired. Habits, attitude and how you treat people decide whether you keep the job and move up.

      Personal and social skills

      A relationship forms whenever you interact with other people, and both real actions and how people see them matter. No one wants to work with an unprofessional person. Your actions reflect on you, your co-workers, your company and your trade. Self-presentation is the way a person dresses, speaks, acts and interacts with others. It includes personal habits (your appearance and behavior) and your work ethic (how you do your job). First impressions are hard to change, so look people in the eye and stand with confidence. A firm, sincere handshake makes a good impression.

      Personal habitWhat it looks like
      DependabilityYou follow through, you show up on time every day, and you do not stretch breaks
      OrganizationYou keep tools and work areas clean, and plan each day before you start
      Being qualifiedYou know how to use tools safely, keep learning, and never attempt a task you do not know when there is a safety hazard
      Willingness to helpYou pitch in, but you do not promise more than you can do
      HonestyNo fake sick days, no having someone punch you out, no stealing, and tell your supervisor the truth about problems
      ProfessionalismIntegrity and work-appropriate manners. Respect the rules and company confidentiality, which means privacy of information. Follow the dress code and keep good grooming.

      A strong work ethic is essential for getting hired and promoted. Employers want a fair day's work for a fair day's pay. Initiative means working without constant supervision. If you finish early, look for another task or ask if co-workers need help.

      Worked exampleYour supervisor shows you five steps, and you notice one step looks unnecessary. Leaving it out on your own is not the right initiative. Tell your supervisor and ask. Either you save the company time on every future job, or you learn why the step matters. Both are good outcomes.

      The two most common problems supervisors face are tardiness (habitually showing up late) and absenteeism (consistently failing to show up). Schedules are built around workers, so each one costs the company money. To stay on time and present, allow enough time to get to work, follow your company's reporting policy, tell your supervisor early if you will be out more than a day, explain a late arrival as soon as you get there, and do not abuse lunch and breaks.

      Conflict resolution

      Conflict can happen anywhere. Be tactful, which means being aware of how your words and actions affect others. Do not accuse, embarrass or threaten. Never let a conflict affect job performance, morale or site safety. To cool a situation, think before you react, walk away to think, do not take it personally, and stay out of other people's disagreements. If it escalates, walk away and tell your supervisor right away.

      With co-workersWith your supervisor
      Be respectful and open-minded. Admit there is a conflict, then each person describes their view. It may be only miscommunication.Think about the cause first and consider writing it down. Ask for a time to talk and meet when it suits your supervisor.
      Discuss solutions. You will probably need to compromise, meaning each side makes concessions to reach a solution everyone agrees on. If it fails, ask your supervisor to help.Speak calmly and clearly. State only facts you can prove, and do not name co-workers who are not involved. Accept the final decision.
      If you were wrong, apologize. An apology is a sign of respect.Serious disagreements with a supervisor should be reported to Human Resources.

      Giving and receiving criticism

      Constructive criticism is a positive offer of advice meant to help someone correct mistakes or improve. People who offer it do so because they think you are worth their time.

      • Offering it: use positive, supportive words and give facts, not opinions. Do it occasionally, never in front of others, suggest alternatives, and limit it to what you can also compliment. Starting with "In the future" lands better than starting with "You."
      • Receiving it: take it seriously, even from experienced co-workers. Take responsibility, do not be defensive, and ask for specifics if it is vague. You may respectfully disagree if it is wrong, and explain why.
      • Destructive criticism is meant to hurt, not help. Stay calm, tell the person how it made you feel, and ask for positive suggestions if there is a real point inside it.

      Social issues in the workplace

      Today's job sites include people of many backgrounds. Three issues come up: harassment, stress and drug and alcohol abuse.

      • Harassment is a type of discrimination that can be based on race, age, disabilities, sex, religion, cultural issues, health or language barriers. It creates a hostile or offensive working environment.
      • Sexual harassment is discrimination from unwelcome sexual advances, requests for favors, or other behavior with sexual overtones. It is illegal and can happen between people of any gender. Report it to your supervisor right away.
      • Bullying is unwanted, aggressive behavior involving a real or perceived power imbalance. Adults usually call it hazing, stalking or harassment. It can come from one person or a group and can cause anxiety, depression and high blood pressure. Cyberbullying happens through email, texts and social media. The most critical thing a victim can do is document the events carefully, because documentation lets those in authority handle it properly.

      For alcohol and drugs, employers have zero tolerance: no use of alcohol, marijuana or illegal drugs in the workplace, and no misuse of prescription drugs. Companies test before hiring, at random, and often after an accident. Working under the influence can cause fatal accidents and serious errors, and can lead to personal liability. You can be fired on the spot.

      Drug classWhat the module says
      Amphetamines and methamphetaminesAddictive stimulants ("uppers") that affect the nervous system. They can cause dizziness, loss of coordination and collapse.
      BarbituratesSedatives ("downers") that relax the body and slow reactions, with slurred speech and mood swings
      HallucinogensDistort reality so people see things that are not there and cannot react safely
      OpiatesNarcotic painkillers from the opium poppy or made synthetically. They relieve pain but cause serious problems over time.
      Synthetic drugsDrugs with effects like known substances but a slightly changed chemical makeup. They can be extremely dangerous because users do not know what is in them.
      If someone is strugglingIf you or someone you know has trouble with alcohol or drugs, talk to your supervisor. Many companies offer substance abuse counseling and will support you with periodic testing. Encourage others to get help.

      Teamwork and leadership

      Teamwork is everyone contributing so the team reaches its goals. Teams can be two people or a whole company, and often mix trades, like a crew with a backhoe operator, pipefitters and electricians burying lines. Good team members are goal-oriented, which means making sure all activities benefit the team's final objective. To be a good team member, follow your leader's directions, accept that others may be better at some tasks, keep a positive attitude, work with people at different speeds, trust others, and share credit and take responsibility for mistakes. Finish your work on time, because others may be waiting on you. When you are asked to train someone, be patient, teach by example, and give constructive criticism.

      Leadership is the ability to set an example for others to follow by using authority and responsibility. You can become a leader at any stage, even as an apprentice, by doing your task quickly, correctly and independently. Leaders lead by example, have drive and persistence, communicate well, motivate their team, plan well and have self-confidence.

      Leadership styleHow decisions get made
      AutocraticThe leader makes all decisions alone
      DemocraticThe leader asks the team for ideas and considers them before deciding
      Hands-offThe leader leaves all decisions to the team

      Pick the style that fits the situation. Leaders must also be ethical. There are three kinds of ethics: business or legal ethics (follow laws and regulations), professional ethics (be fair to everybody) and situational ethics (respond appropriately to a specific situation). Effective leaders motivate people by praising good work, giving a sense of accomplishment, offering chances to advance, and rewarding effort.
