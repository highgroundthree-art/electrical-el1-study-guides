Why this guide exists

You do not need to work in construction to support a construction student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning and put it in their own words. Safety is the one subject where that matters most, because the habits learned here are the ones that bring them home at the end of the day.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 20 to 30 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Good to know before you start

This guide explains safety rules so your student can talk about them. It does not replace hands-on training, a supervisor's instructions, the employer's safety program or the manufacturer's directions for any tool or piece of equipment. Distances, ratings and limits come from the textbook. Always follow the rules that apply at your student's school or job site. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 6

Safety and Hazard Recognition

By the end of this section your student can explain why safety is everyone's job, tell an incident from an accident, name the fatal four, and describe how hazards are found, reported and controlled.

A

Read it together

about 30 min

Safety is a way of working, not a poster on the wall. The module says everyone on a job site shares the job of staying safe, but your own safety is ultimately in your own hands.

Safety is everyone's job (1.0.0)

You owe it to your employer, co-workers, loved ones and yourself to work safely, and to make sure anyone you work with is working safely. The module boils this down to three rules:

  • Follow safe work practices and procedures, both regulatory and corporate.
  • Inspect safety equipment before you use it.
  • Use safety equipment properly.

A safety culture is created when everyone from management to laborers sees the value of a safe work environment and supports it through their actions. Companies with strong safety cultures usually have fewer at-risk behaviors, lower incident and accident rates, less turnover, lower absenteeism and higher productivity. A good safety record also helps a company win more bids, and contractors with poor records can be left out of bidding. Part of a strong culture is encouraging workers to question things and empowering them to stop work when they see a potential hazard.

Incidents and accidents (1.1.0)

People often use these words as if they mean the same thing. OSHA defines them this way:

  • An incident is an unplanned event that does not result in personal injury but may result in property damage or is worth recording.
  • An accident is an unplanned event that results in personal injury and/or property damage.

Either way, both are unwanted events that hurt projects and workers.

Categories (1.1.1)

How the module sorts incidents and accidents
CategoryWhat it means
Near-missNo one was hurt and nothing was damaged, but either could have happened. Always report it.
Property damageDamage to tools, materials or equipment, but no personal injuries.
Minor injuriesMinor cuts, bruises or strains. The worker is back to full duty on the next regular shift.
Serious or disabling injuriesTemporary or permanent disability, including lost-time and restricted-duty cases.
FatalitiesDeaths resulting from unplanned events.

Studies found that for every serious or disabling injury there were 10 less serious injuries and 30 property damage incidents, and about 600 near-misses. That is why near-misses matter.

The four leading causes of death in construction are the "fatal four" (also called the "big four" or "focus four"): falls, struck-by hazards, caught-in or caught-between hazards and electrical hazards. Deaths from falls far exceed all the others. In 2018, 1,008 of the 4,779 worker deaths in US private industry (21.1 percent, about one in five) were in construction, and the fatal four caused nearly 3 out of 5 of those construction deaths.

Costs (1.1.2)

Incidents and accidents cost billions of dollars a year and cause needless suffering. The National Safety Council estimates the organized safety movement has saved more than 4.2 million lives since 1913. An injury affects the worker, the worker's loved ones, co-workers and the employer. A worker injured while breaking safety rules may have insurance claims denied. A poor safety record can also cost a company future contracts, which can mean layoffs and hiring freezes.

Half-measureMost workers who die from falls are wearing harnesses but did not tie off properly. Always follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Causes of incidents and accidents (1.2.0)

The ten causes the module lists
CauseWhat the module says
Failure to communicate (1.2.1)Different people, companies and job sites do things differently. Do not assume. Do not use jargon. Learn the signs: informational (blue), safety (white background, usually a green panel with white letters; exits often white letters on red), caution (yellow), danger (red, black and white) and temporary warning tags. Toolbox talks are 5 to 10 minute safety meetings.
At-risk work habits (1.2.2)Procrastination, carelessness and horseplay. Pranks can seriously injure a co-worker.

Intentional acts (1.2.5)Purposely causing damage or injury. Tell your supervisor at once if someone threatens to get even. Stay aware of strangers on the site.
Unsafe acts (1.2.6)Changing from the accepted procedure. Examples: not using required PPE, lifting improperly, making a saw guard inoperable, servicing equipment in motion, using defective equipment.
Rationalizing risk (1.2.7)Ignoring safety warnings because you think you can justify it, such as not tying off because you only lean over a foot. If you think it is unsafe, it is unsafe: stop and tell your supervisor.
Unsafe conditions (1.2.8)A physical state that differs from normal and safe: clutter, defective tools, poor lighting or ventilation, unguarded moving parts, radiation, hazardous atmosphere. All workers should have authority to stop work when they see one.

Self-careThe module says good self-care helps a long construction career. Quality sleep matters most for safety, since sleep loss hurts judgment and raises the risk of being distracted. Healthy eating, exercise and staying hydrated also help.

Hazard recognition, evaluation and control (1.3.0)

Spotting hazards early is the foundation of an effective safety program. For any situation or piece of equipment, ask: How can it cause harm? What energy sources are present and how big are they? What could go wrong to release the energy? How can the energy be eliminated or controlled? Will I be exposed to hazardous materials?

HAZCOM and hazardous chemicals (1.3.0)

The Hazard Communication (HAZCOM) Standard is also called the Right-To-Know requirement. It requires US contractors to teach employees about the hazardous chemicals they may be exposed to. The module says many people wrongly think few hazardous chemicals are on job sites: solvents, paint, concrete and even wood dust all count. Under HAZCOM you must know where the SDSs are, report hazards to your supervisor, know the hazards of materials on the job and the precautions to take, know what to do in an emergency, and know where your employer's written hazard communication program is.

Energy sources and hazard recognition tools (1.3.1 and 1.3.2)

Equipment usually causes an incident through an uncontrolled release of energy. Energy types listed: mechanical, pneumatic, hydraulic, electrical, chemical, thermal, radioactive, gravitational and stored energy.

  • A job safety analysis (JSA) (also called job hazard analysis, JHA) breaks a task into steps and studies each step for hazards, then recommends fixes.
  • A task safety analysis (TSA) (also called task hazard analysis, THA) is similar but usually uses a pre-printed fill-in-the-blank checklist. The supervisor or team leader reviews it with the crew before work begins, and some companies have workers sign it.

Risk assessment (1.3.3)

Risk is a measure of the probability (chance an event occurs), the consequences (the results) and the exposure (how long or how much someone is exposed). A safe operation has an acceptable level of risk. Climbing a ladder is acceptable only if the right ladder is used as intended, set up correctly and in good condition.

Reporting (1.3.4)

All incidents and accidents, no matter how minor, must be reported to your supervisor. Small injuries can turn into big problems from infection. US employers with more than 10 employees must keep a log of significant work-related injuries and illnesses on OSHA forms. Reporting helps keep similar events from happening again.

Safety Data Sheets (1.3.5)

An SDS is a fact sheet from the chemical manufacturer or importer. Every product used on a construction site must have one. It describes the substance, its hazards, safe handling, first aid and emergency spill procedures. New SDSs follow a standard 16-section format (they used to be called MSDSs). The most important things to find are the specific hazards, personal protection, handling procedures and first aid information, and most SDSs list a 24-hour emergency number. In the module's example (a PVC pipe cement), Section 2 shows the product is flammable and an eye and skin irritant. Section 7 says ventilation is needed, Section 8 covers eye and skin protection, Section 4 covers first aid and Section 5 covers fire fighting.

SDSs must be kept in the work area and be readily accessible to all workers. Ask your supervisor where they are.

    Module Section 2 of 6

    Elevated Work and Fall Protection

    By the end of this section your student can describe fall hazards, name the parts of a fall arrest system, and set up ladders and scaffolds the safe way.

    A

    Read it together

    about 30 min

    Falls from elevated areas are the leading cause of workplace fatalities and about one-third of all construction deaths. About 85 percent of injured workers lose time from work, and about 33 percent need hospitalization.

    Two jobs: prevention and arrest (2.0.0)

    Employers must provide both and make sure workers are trained and certified in using fall protection equipment.

    • Fall prevention stops a fall from happening: covered floor openings, climbing aids, barricades and guardrails.
    • Fall arrest protects a worker if a fall does happen: body harnesses, lanyards, connecting devices, lifelines and safety nets.

    Fall types and hazards (2.1.0)

    Falls come in two groups: from an elevation (scaffolds, platforms, decking, concrete forms, ladders, stairs, near excavations) and on the same level (usually tripping or slipping). In the US, fall protection is required at unprotected sides or edges 6 feet or more above the ground or level below. This is the six-foot rule. Some countries and companies require protection at lower heights.

    Walking and working surfaces (2.1.1). Slips, trips and falls on these surfaces cause 15 percent of incidental deaths in construction. Keep areas clean, dry and clear. Run cables, cords and hoses overhead or through crossover plates, and never run on scaffolds, platforms, decking or roofs.

    Unprotected sides, wall openings and floor holes (2.1.2). Any opening in a wall or floor is a hazard. It can be guarded or covered. Guard a wall opening if its bottom edge is less than 39 inches above the floor and a person could fall 6 feet or more.

    Barriers and barricades
    TypeWhat it does
    RailingsBarrier across wall openings or around floor openings.
    Warning barricadesWarn only, no real protection. Red = danger, do not enter. Yellow = caution, enter carefully. Yellow and purple = radiation warning, no entry without authorization, training and PPE.
    Protective barricadesVisual warning plus real protection: wooden posts and rails, posts and chain, or steel cable.
    Blinking lightsPut on barricades so they can be seen at night.
    Hole coversSecured so they cannot slide, extend past the hole, clearly marked and strong enough for twice the weight that may be put on them. Use 3/4-inch plywood if one side of the opening is under 18 inches, otherwise 2-inch lumber. Never store material on a cover.
    WarningNever remove a barricade unless you are authorized to do so.

    Guard stairway floor openings (except the entrance) with standard railing and toeboards, and guard open-sided floors and platforms 6 feet or more above the next level.

    Fall arrest (2.2.0)

    A complete personal fall arrest system (PFAS) has three parts: anchor points, a body harness and connecting devices (lanyards and hardware).

    • Anchor points (2.2.1) may be permanent or temporary and must be rated for 5,000 pounds per worker (or twice the intended load). Ideally the anchor is directly above the back D-ring to reduce free-fall and swing-zone hazards. Maximum free-fall is 6 feet. Positioning anchor points need only be rated at 3,000 pounds, and positioning lanyards are fall restraints, not fall arrest.
    • Harnesses (2.2.2) are sized by height and weight. The back D-ring is the main connection for fall arrest. When climbing a ladder, the front chest D-ring is used. Hip D-rings are for positioning and restraint, and shoulder D-rings are often for rescue. Fit it snug. Never add holes or modify it. The back D-ring sits between the shoulder blades. A suspension trauma strap lets a hanging worker stand and relieve pressure on the legs.
    • Inspection (2.2.3). Inspect your harness and lanyard before every use. A competent person inspects the PFAS at least monthly. If in doubt, take it out of service.
    • Lanyards (2.2.4). Fall arrest lanyards are shock absorbing or self-retracting (retractable is becoming the favorite). Never field-fabricate one, tie a knot in it or connect two together. Whenever wearing a PFAS, 100 percent tie-off is required. A Y-configured lanyard lets you stay connected while moving. If a PFAS stops a fall, a competent person must inspect it before reuse, and it often must be replaced.
    • Lifelines (2.2.5). Vertical lifelines hang from one anchor and need a 5,000 pound breaking strength, with a separate line for each worker. Horizontal lifelines run between two anchors, are designed and used under a competent person, and are rated for a maximum number of workers.
    • Guardrails (2.2.6) must withstand 200 pounds on the top rail and 150 pounds on the midrail, be 42 inches (plus or minus 3) high and have a toeboard at least 4 inches high.
    • Safety nets (2.2.7) are used on bridges and similar projects, no more than 30 feet below the work, inspected at least weekly, and drop-tested after installation, relocation, repair and at least every six months.
    Note from the moduleThis training does not certify anyone to use fall arrest or fall restraint equipment in the field.

    Ladders and stairs (2.3.0)

    Overloading means going over a ladder's maximum intended load, the total weight of people, tools and materials. Ladders have duty ratings:

    Ladder duty ratings (Table 2)
    RatingLoadUse
    Type IAA375 lbsExtra-heavy duty / professional
    Type IA300 lbsExtra-heavy duty / professional
    Type I250 lbsHeavy duty / industrial
    Type II225 lbsMedium duty / commercial
    Type III200 lbsLight duty / household

    Use three-point contact every time you climb: two feet and one hand, or one foot and two hands.

    • Straight ladders (2.3.1). Metal ladders conduct electricity, so never use them around electrical equipment (a portable metal ladder must be marked "Danger! Do Not Use Around Electrical Installations"). Dry wood and fiberglass are safe around electricity, but even a little water conducts. Check the safety feet. Use the 4-to-1 rule: the base is one-fourth of the height the ladder touches (a 16-foot wall means 4 feet out). To step off onto a roof or platform, the ladder must extend at least 3 feet above it. Set it on stable, level ground or secure it top and bottom. Face the ladder, keep your weight centered, never move a ladder while on it, and use a hand line for tools.
    • Extension ladders (2.3.2). Overlap at least 3 feet (up to 36 ft), 4 feet (36 to 48 ft) or 5 feet (48 to 60 ft). Adjust the movable section from the bottom. Never stand above the fourth rung from the top. Never remove the extension stop. Haul materials up on a line.
    • Stepladders (2.3.3). All four feet on a hard, even surface with spreaders locked open. Never stand on the top step or top, never climb the rear braces, and never stand with your knees higher than the top.
    • Inspecting (2.3.4). Check before every use for cracks, loose rungs and loose hardware. Do not use a damaged ladder. Do not paint wooden ladders, because paint can hide cracks.
    • Stairways (2.3.5). Used where there is a break in elevation of 19 inches or more. Four or more risers (or more than 30 inches of rise) need at least one handrail and a stair railing on unprotected sides. Stair railings are at least 36 inches high. Keep stairs clear, lit and free of stored materials.

    Scaffolds (2.4.0)

    Scaffolds are elevated platforms for workers and materials. People who assemble them must be certified, and only a competent person supervises setting up, moving or taking down a scaffold and approves its use.

    • Types (2.4.1). The two basic types are self-supporting and suspended scaffolds. Manufactured (including rolling scaffolds with wheel brakes), built-up and swing scaffolds are examples. Never unlock the wheel brakes while anyone is on a rolling scaffold.
    • Tags (2.4.2). Green = meets OSHA standards, safe to use. Yellow = does not meet all standards, so you must wear a harness tied to a lanyard. Red = being put up or taken down, never use it. Still inspect it yourself.
    • Rules. Know the weight limit. Over 10 feet high: top rails, midrails and toeboards (or use a PFAS). Pin all connections, use cross-bracing, fully plank the walkway, and screen the space if people can walk below.
    • Using (2.4.3). Firm footing under each leg. Keep scaffolds at least 3 feet from power lines up to 300 volts and at least 10 feet from lines above 300 volts. Never move a scaffold while someone is on it.

      Module Section 3 of 6

      Struck-By and Caught-in-Between Hazards

      By the end of this section your student can tell struck-by from caught-in-between hazards and explain trench, equipment and guarding safety.

      A

      Read it together

      about 25 min

      Two hazard families, one idea. Struck-by means being hit by a moving object. Caught-in-between means being trapped between a moving object and something solid.

      The module's exampleA load swings from a crane and hits you: struck-by. The crane backs up and pins you against a wall: caught-in-between.

      Falling objects (3.1.1)

      • Always wear an approved hard hat. Never stand or work under a load or in the fall zone.
      • Barricade areas where rigging is used and post warning signs. Inspect cranes and rigging, and do not exceed the rated load.
      • For overhead work, secure tools and materials. Use toeboards, debris nets, catch platforms or canopies, and tool lanyards.
      • Do not stack materials higher than a 4:1 height-to-base ratio. Block and interlock loads (place alternate layers at right angles). Watch for wind.

      Flying objects (3.1.2)

      Chipping, grinding, brushing and hammering can throw objects into the air, and high-speed tools such as pneumatic and powder-actuated tools can be very dangerous. Injuries range from minor abrasions to concussions, blindness or death. Wear eye protection (safety glasses, goggles or face shields), check that tool guards are in place, get trained on pneumatic and powder-actuated tools, and use shielding such as a welding screen.

      Vehicles and roadways (3.1.3)

      The most common cause of death for equipment operators is equipment rollover. Other dangers are being run over (especially by backing equipment) or struck by swinging backhoes and crane loads. Nearly one in four struck-by vehicle deaths involves construction workers.

      • Stay alert, keep a safe distance and make eye contact with operators. Never stand in blind spots.
      • Wear reflective or high-visibility vests. Keep off equipment unless authorized.
      • Never stand between pieces of equipment unless they are secured, and never stand under loads or near vehicles being loaded.
      • Operators: always wear a seat belt, obey speed limits, sound the horn and look to the rear before backing (use a signaler if your view is blocked), keep a working backup alarm, turn off the engine when fueling, and never use a cell phone while driving.
      Carbon monoxideA vehicle, generator or any internal combustion engine running indoors can sicken or kill without ventilation. You cannot see, smell or taste carbon monoxide.

      Caught-in hazards and trenches (3.2.0 and 3.2.1)

      Trench or excavation collapse, rotating equipment and unguarded parts are the main causes of caught-in-between deaths. An excavation is any man-made cut, cavity, trench or depression made by removing earth. A trench is an excavation deeper than it is wide, usually not wider than 15 feet.

      • Hazards: cave-ins, water accumulation, falling objects, collapse of nearby structures and toxic gases in the soil.
      • Cave-ins are the most common and deadly hazard. Most happen in trenches 5 to 15 feet deep, suddenly and with little warning. About 1,000 trench collapses happen each year in the US. Just 2 to 3 feet of soil can keep you from breathing.
      • More likely when: weather changes (freezing, thawing, heavy rain), soil is unstable or already disturbed, there is excess vibration or water collects.
      • Warning signs: cracks or settling near the edge, flakes, pebbles or clumps of soil falling in, bulges in the wall. Get out immediately and warn others.
      Soil types (Table 3)
      SoilDescription
      Solid rockWalls stay vertical as long as the excavation is open. The most stable.
      Type AFine-grained, cohesive: clay, hardpan, caliche. Particles too small to see.
      Type BAngular rock, silt and similar soil.
      Type CCoarse-grained, granular: sand, gravel, loamy sand. Particles visible. The least stable.

      To be safe, treat soil as Type C unless proven otherwise. A competent person must inspect excavations daily: before work starts, as needed during the shift and after every rainstorm or other event that raises hazards. If there are signs of trouble, workers must leave and stay out until it is fixed.

      • Protective systems are required in nearly all excavations. If the excavation is more than 20 feet deep, a registered professional engineer must design the system.
      • Sloping cuts the sides back to a safe angle with smooth inclines. Benching cuts them back in steps and cannot be used with Type C soil.
      • Shoring supports the trench walls (plating held by expandable braces) and prevents cave-in. Shielding (trench boxes) only protects workers who stay inside the box.
      • Spoil (the dirt removed) and equipment must stay at least 2 feet from the edge.
      • Trenches deeper than 4 feet need a safe way in and out (ladder, stairway or ramp) within 25 feet of workers. A loader bucket or backhoe shovel is not a safe way in or out.
      • Your company must have an emergency action plan. When in doubt, get out.
      Call before you digDial 811, the nationwide number, before any digging so utilities can mark buried lines. Hitting a line can cause outages, electrocution, gas leaks and explosions. The Common Ground Alliance's five rules: call, wait for utilities to respond, confirm the markings, respect the markings, and proceed with caution near the marked line (the tolerance zone is 1.5 to 2 feet).

      Tool, machine and equipment guards (3.2.2)

      Guards keep moving parts away from your hands and body. Grinding, shearing, cutting, pressing, punching and rolling tools, and anything with pinch points or sharp edges, must have guards. Only remove a guard to clean, change a blade or service the machine, and only when it is turned off and tagged out. Replace it immediately. Never wedge a guard open, and use only attachments made for that tool.

      Cranes and heavy equipment (3.2.3)

      Workers can be crushed by falling loads, fall from equipment, be electrocuted by power lines or be struck or trapped by vehicles. The swing radius of equipment is a hazard, so barricade the machine's working perimeter. Most heavy equipment has pinch points. In the module's example, a worker approached a backhoe from the operator's blind side and was crushed against a wall.

        Module Section 4 of 6

        Energy Release Hazards

        By the end of this section your student can explain electrical hazards, GFCIs and the lockout/tagout steps.

        A

        Read it together

        about 20 min

        Energy hazards are the ones you cannot always see. Electricity, stored energy and moving parts can hurt you the instant they are released, so the module's answer is to control the energy before you touch the equipment.

        Electrical hazards (4.1.0)

        Electrical shock is one of the fatal four. Less than 1 amp of current through the body can kill. Shock can also cause burns, falls and fires. Water, wet skin and damaged tools make shock more likely, and a metal ladder, scaffold or pipe can carry current.

        • Ground-fault circuit interrupters (GFCIs) protect people by cutting power when current leaks. A GFCI trips when it senses an imbalance of about 5 milliamps. Use one on every temporary power outlet and portable cord set outdoors or in damp places.
        • Inspect cords and tools before each use. Take damaged tools out of service and tag them. Never use a cord with a missing ground pin or bare conductors.
        • Overhead power lines. Treat every line as energized. Stay at least 10 feet away from lines of 50,000 volts or less, and farther for higher voltages. Assume cranes, ladders and scaffolds can reach a line, and call the utility if you are unsure.
        • Underground lines. Call 811 before you dig.
        • Only qualified, authorized people may work on energized equipment. Everyone else treats it as live until it is locked out and tested.

        Lockout/Tagout (4.2.0)

        Equipment can start up or release stored energy while someone is working on it. Lockout/tagout (LOTO) keeps that from happening. A lockout device (a lock) holds the energy-isolating device in the safe position. A tagout device is a warning tag that says who applied it and why. The tag does not stop the energy by itself.

        1. Notify everyone affected that the equipment will be shut down.
        2. Shut down the equipment the normal way.
        3. Isolate it from every energy source (disconnect, valve, breaker).
        4. Apply your lock and tag.
        5. Release or block stored energy: capacitors, springs, pressure, gravity.
        6. Verify that the equipment is de-energized by trying to start it, then return the controls to off.
        Your lock, your keyThe person who applied a lock is the only person who removes it, and the key stays with that person. Each worker uses a personal lock. Never remove another worker's lock or tag.

        Stored and released energy (4.3.0)

        Mechanical energy (moving parts), hydraulic and pneumatic pressure, springs, elevated loads, chemical and thermal energy can all release without warning. Compressed air and pressurized fluid can break skin and cause injury or embolism. Never point an air hose at anyone, never use it to clean clothing, and keep it away from your skin. Release pressure and bleed lines before work begins, and block raised loads so they cannot drop.

        Power tools and machines (4.4.0)

        • Use a tool only for what it was made to do, and only if trained.
        • Keep guards on. Unplug or lock out a tool before changing blades or bits.
        • Pick up and carry a tool by the body, never by the cord. Do not leave a running tool unattended.
        • Secure the work with clamps or a vise so both hands are free for the tool.
        • Wear eye protection, and remove loose clothing, jewelry and dangling items. Tie back long hair.

          Module Section 5 of 6

          Personal Protective Equipment

          By the end of this section your student can match each kind of PPE to the hazard it guards against, including respirators and harmful dusts.

          A

          Read it together

          about 15 min

          PPE is the last line of defense. First try to remove the hazard, then guard it, and change how people work. When none of that is enough, the gear between you and the hazard is what is left, so it has to fit, be clean and be worn every time.

          Head protection (5.1.0)

          Hard hats protect against falling objects, bumping into things and, for some types, electrical contact. Check the label for its class. The suspension must leave at least 1 inch of space between your head and the shell. Replace a hat that has taken a hit, is cracked or is past its date. Never drill holes in the shell or paint it unless the maker approves. Wear it with the bill forward unless it is marked for reverse wear.

          Eye and face protection (5.2.0)

          Choose the protection for the hazard: safety glasses with side shields for flying particles, goggles for dust and splashes, and face shields over glasses for grinding or chemicals. Welding needs a lens of the right shade. Look for the Z87 marking, keep lenses clean, and replace any that are scratched or cracked.

          Hearing protection (5.3.0)

          Noise damage builds slowly and cannot be reversed. A simple test from the module: if you have to raise your voice to be heard by someone within 2 feet, the noise is high enough to need hearing protection. Use earplugs or earmuffs, or both for very loud work, and keep plugs clean and sized to fit.

          Hand protection (5.4.0)

          Match the glove to the hazard: leather for rough material, cut-resistant for sharp edges, chemical-resistant for liquids, and insulated rubber for electrical work. Check for holes and tears. Never wear gloves around rotating machinery where they could be caught.

          Foot and leg protection (5.5.0)

          Wear sturdy, over-the-ankle boots with slip-resistant soles. Use safety-toe boots where objects could fall or roll on your feet, metatarsal guards where heavy items are handled, and puncture-resistant soles where nails are a problem. Boots for electrical work should be rated for it.

          Respiratory protection (5.6.0)

          Respirators are needed when dust, fumes, vapors or low oxygen could harm you and the air cannot be made safe by ventilation. Before wearing one you need medical clearance, training and a fit test. Facial hair that breaks the seal means no seal and no protection.

          The four respirator types
          TypeHow it works
          Disposable particulateFilters dust and particles. Throw it away when it is dirty or hard to breathe through.
          Half-face air-purifyingReusable mask with cartridges for particles, vapors or gases.
          Full-face air-purifyingSame filters, plus eye and face protection, and a higher level of protection.
          Supplied air / SCBABrings clean air from another source. Used where the air is deadly or lacks oxygen.

          Match the cartridge to the hazard, and replace it on schedule. An assigned protection factor (APF) tells how much a respirator reduces exposure. A half-face has an APF of 10.

          Harmful dusts and materials (5.7.0)

          • Silica dust comes from cutting or grinding concrete, brick and stone. It scars the lungs. Use a wet saw or a HEPA vacuum to control dust, never dry sweep or blow it, and wear a respirator when required.
          • Asbestos may be present in buildings built before 1980 (insulation, floor tile, pipe wrap). Do not disturb suspect material. Stop work and tell your supervisor.
          • Lead paint was banned in 1978, so older buildings may have it. Dust from sanding or demolition is the danger, so keep it contained and wash up before eating.

          Clothing and body (5.8.0)

          Wear snug long pants and a shirt with sleeves, and remove jewelry. Use high-visibility clothing near traffic and equipment. Keep long hair tied back, and wear sunscreen for outdoor work. Follow the PPE rules posted for each area, and inspect your gear before every shift.

            Job-Site Hazards

            By the end of this section your student can describe chemical, heat, cold, welding, fire and confined-space hazards.

            A

            Read it together

            about 25 min

            Job sites can hurt you in ways that do not look dangerous: heat, cold, fumes, flame and tight spaces. The module groups these as chemical, environmental, fire and confined-space hazards.

            Hazardous chemicals (6.1.0)

            A chemical can enter your body three ways: inhalation (breathing it in), ingestion (swallowing it) and absorption (through skin or eyes). A permissible exposure limit (PEL) is the amount of a substance a worker may be exposed to over a set time, set by OSHA. Read the label, read the SDS and wear the PPE it calls for.

            Under the Globally Harmonized System (GHS), labels use nine pictograms that show the hazard type at a glance, plus a signal word (Danger or Warning), a hazard statement and precautions. OSHA required these labels from 2015. Never put a chemical into an unmarked container, and never mix chemicals unless you know they are safe together.

            Heat (6.2.1)

            Heat illness gets worse in stages: heat cramps, then heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea), then heat stroke, which is a life-threatening emergency (hot dry skin, confusion, collapse). Call for help right away for heat stroke and cool the person while you wait.

            • Drink water often, before you feel thirsty.
            • Take breaks in the shade, and wear light, loose clothing.
            • Ease into hot work if you are new to it or returning after time off.

            Cold (6.2.2)

            Hypothermia is a dangerously low body temperature (shivering, slurred speech, confusion). Frostbite is freezing of skin and tissue, usually fingers, toes, ears and nose. Dress in layers, keep dry, and take breaks in a warm place. The module notes that the body loses heat up to 25 times faster in water than in air.

            Welding and cutting (6.3.0)

            Arc welding runs at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 degrees F. The light can cause flash burn, a painful eye injury, so wear a welding helmet with the right lens shade and protective clothing. Fumes need ventilation. Compressed gas cylinders must be kept upright and secured with caps on.

            • Store oxygen and fuel gas cylinders 20 feet apart, or separate them with a barrier at least 5 feet high with a fire rating of at least 1/2 hour.
            • A hot work permit is required in many places. It lists the area, the work and the precautions. A fire watch stays during the work and afterward to catch smoldering fires.

            Fire safety (6.4.0)

            Fire needs three things, the fire triangle: fuel, oxygen and heat. Take away any one and the fire goes out. Know where extinguishers and exits are, and keep exits clear. Extinguishers are rated by the type of fire:

            Extinguisher classes
            ClassFuel
            AOrdinary combustibles: wood, paper, cloth
            BFlammable liquids and gases: gasoline, oil, grease
            CEnergized electrical equipment
            DCombustible metals

            Use the PASS method: Pull the pin, Aim at the base of the fire, Squeeze the handle, Sweep side to side. Fight a fire only if it is small, you have the right extinguisher and you have a clear exit behind you. Otherwise get out and call for help.

            Confined spaces (6.5.0)

            A confined space is large enough to enter but has limited ways in or out and is not made for people to stay in (tanks, vaults, trenches, crawl spaces). Hazards include low oxygen, toxic gas, fire and engulfment.

            • The air must be tested first. Safe oxygen is 19.5 to 23.5 percent.
            • A permit-required confined space has a serious hazard, so entry needs a written permit.
            • An attendant stays outside the whole time, keeps count of who is inside and calls for help. The attendant never goes in to attempt a rescue.
            • Ventilate, and have rescue equipment ready before anyone enters.
            Do not rush inMany confined-space deaths are would-be rescuers. Call for trained rescue.
