Why this guide exists

You do not need to be good at math to support a construction student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning in their own words. Math is the skill every trade leans on, from measuring a board to ordering concrete, and it gets easier when someone listens to the thinking.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 30 to 45 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Good to know before you start

Try each problem by hand first, the way the module asks. A calculator is for checking an answer, not for finding it, unless the problem says to use one. This guide explains the math so your student can talk about it. It does not replace class instruction or the textbook. Always follow the rules that apply at your student's school or job site. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 6

Whole Numbers

By the end of this section your student can read and write whole numbers, add, subtract, multiply and divide them by hand, and use the order of operations.

A

Read it together

about 40 min

Every craft uses whole numbers, and mistakes with numbers waste time, effort and materials. The module says your skill with numbers also reflects on you, because a craft professional's reputation depends on a friendly relationship with math.

No calculatorUnless the text says otherwise, try every problem by hand first. A calculator is for checking your answer, not for finding it.

Place values (1.1.0)

Whole numbers are complete number units with no fraction or decimal part. Each digit (0 to 9) has a place value set by where it sits. In 5,316,247 the 5 is five million and the 2 is two hundred. Read it as "five million, three hundred sixteen thousand, two hundred forty-seven."

  • Numbers above zero are positive. Numbers below zero are negative. Zero is neither.
  • A zero can hold a place: 7,093 is "seven thousand ninety-three," with a zero in the hundreds place.
  • The places, right to left: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands, hundred thousands, millions.

Adding and subtracting whole numbers (1.2.0)

To add means to combine values. The result is the sum.

  1. Line up the digits by place value.
  2. Start at the right (the ones column) and add each column, moving left.
  3. If a column adds to 10 or more, write the ones digit and carry the 1 to the next column on the left.

Example from the module: 723 + 84. Ones: 3 + 4 = 7. Tens: 2 + 8 = 10, so write 0 and carry 1. Hundreds: 7 + 1 = 8. The sum is 807.

To subtract means to take away. The result is the difference. Put the larger number on top, then work right to left. When the top digit is too small, borrow 1 from the column to the left, which makes the top digit 10 larger. Negative answers are uncommon in job-site math.

Check itAdd the difference to the number you subtracted. You should get the starting number back. Example: 1,478 - 489 = 989, and 989 + 489 = 1,478.

Multiplying whole numbers (1.3.0)

Multiplication is a fast way to add a number to itself many times. The result is the product. Six people give the crew leader $8 each for lunch: 6 x $8 = $48.

When the bottom number has more than one digit, you make one row of work for each digit and add the rows. The module works 374 x 26:

  1. Multiply 374 by the 6: that is 2,244.
  2. Put a zero in the ones column (because the 2 is in the tens place), then multiply 374 by the 2: that is 7,480.
  3. Add the two products: 2,244 + 7,480 = 9,724.

There are as many rows to add as there are digits in the bottom number.

Dividing whole numbers (1.3.0)

Division is the opposite of multiplication. The number being divided is the dividend, the number you divide by is the divisor, and the result is the quotient. In 15 / 3 = 5, the dividend is 15, the divisor is 3 and the quotient is 5.

When the divisor does not fit evenly, the leftover is the remainder. Ten divided by 3 is 3 with a remainder of 1.

Long division works left to right, and every step is divide, multiply, subtract, bring down. The module divides 2,638 by 24:

  1. 24 does not go into 2, so use the first two digits. 24 goes into 26 once. Write 1, subtract 24 from 26 to get 2.
  2. Bring down the 3 to make 23. 24 does not go into 23, so write 0.
  3. Bring down the 8 to make 238. 24 goes into 238 nine times (24 x 9 = 216). Subtract to get 22.
  4. The answer is 109 remainder 22.
Sanity checkEach digit of a long-division answer is a number from 0 to 9. If you get 10 or more, something went wrong. Check a division by multiplying the quotient by the divisor and adding the remainder: 109 x 24 + 22 = 2,638.
Construction estimatesContractors must create accurate estimates and stay inside a budget. The estimate adds materials, labor and overhead (the cost of putting the labor on the job, office expenses and company staff), then adds profit. The lowest bid usually gets the job.

Order of operations (1.3.1)

When a problem has more than one operation, the order changes the answer. For simple problems the order is multiplication, division, addition, subtraction (MDAS, "My Dear Aunt Sally"). For harder ones use PEMDAS: parentheses first, then exponents, then the rest.

Look at 6 + 3 x 5. Adding first gives 45, which is wrong. Multiplying first (3 x 5 = 15) and then adding gives 21, which is right.

An exponent says how many times to multiply a number by itself. 6 squared is 6 x 6 = 36, and 6 cubed is 6 x 6 x 6 = 216.

Think of a fraction as a division problem: the top number (numerator) is the dividend and the bottom number (denominator) is the divisor. That idea leads straight into Section 2.

Masons and coursesA mason counts block rows as courses. A 15-course wall of 8-inch blocks is 15 x 8 = 120 inches, and 120 / 12 = 10 feet high.

    Fractions

    By the end of this section your student can find equivalent fractions, reduce to lowest terms, convert improper fractions, and add, subtract, multiply and divide fractions.

    A

    Read it together

    about 45 min

    A fraction is another way to write a division problem. The top number is the numerator and the bottom number is the denominator. In 1/2 the 1 is divided into 2 equal parts. When the top and bottom numbers match (4/4, 8/8, 16/16) the fraction equals exactly 1.

    Most real measurements are not whole numbers. Craftworkers cut pipe and lumber to lengths like 1/2, 3/16 or 5/64 of an inch, so everyone has to be comfortable with fractions, especially anyone using the inch-pound system. The metric system almost removes the need for fractions in daily work.

    Equivalent fractions (2.1.0 and 2.1.1)

    Equivalent fractions look different but have the same value: 1/2, 2/4 and 4/8 are all the same length of board. To make an equivalent fraction, multiply the top and the bottom by the same number.

    Example: how many sixteenths equal 1/2? What times 2 makes 16? That is 8, so multiply both numbers by 8: 8/16. A job often works best when every measurement uses the same denominator, such as sixteenths.

    Reducing to lowest terms (2.1.2)

    A measurement of 4/16 inch will get puzzled looks on a job site. Reduce it:

    1. Find the largest number that divides evenly into both the top and the bottom. For 4/16 it is 4.
    2. Divide both the top and the bottom by that number: 4/16 becomes 1/4.

    If nothing but 1 divides into both, the fraction is already in lowest terms. Always divide top and bottom by the same number, and make sure both divide evenly.

    Comparing and finding a common denominator (2.1.3)

    Which is longer, 3/4" or 5/8"? If the denominators match, just compare the numerators. If they differ, change them to a common denominator.

    • Quick way: multiply the two denominators (4 x 8 = 32). Then 3/4 = 24/32 and 5/8 = 20/32, so 3/4 is longer.
    • Lowest common denominator: reduce each fraction first. If one denominator is a multiple of the other, change only the smaller one. 8 is a multiple of 4, so 3/4 = 6/8. Compare 6/8 and 5/8: 3/4 is longer.
    • If there are no shared multiples, multiply the denominators together.
    Nominal sizesA so-called 8" x 8" x 16" concrete block really measures 7 5/8" x 7 5/8" x 15 5/8". The mason's name for it is the nominal size, which allows for a 3/8" mortar joint and comes out in whole inches.

    Improper fractions and mixed numbers (2.2.0)

    An improper fraction has a numerator larger than its denominator (like 7/4 or 67/32). A mixed number is a whole number plus a fraction (like 1 3/4). Since a fraction is a division problem, divide to convert:

    1. Divide the numerator by the denominator.
    2. The quotient is the whole number. The remainder becomes the new numerator. Keep the original denominator.

    Example: 67/32. 32 goes into 67 two times (64), remainder 3, so 67/32 = 2 3/32. Some improper fractions come out as a plain whole number, like 42/6 = 7.

    To go the other way, multiply the denominator by the whole number and add the numerator. 2 1/3 becomes (3 x 2) + 1 = 7, so 7/3.

    Adding and subtracting fractions (2.3.0)

    1. Find a common denominator and change both fractions to match.
    2. Add (or subtract) the numerators only. Keep the common denominator.
    3. Reduce to lowest terms and change any improper fraction to a mixed number.

    Example from the module: 3/4 + 5/8. Use 8 as the denominator: 6/8 + 5/8 = 11/8 = 1 3/8. (Using 32 also works: 24/32 + 20/32 = 44/32, which reduces to the same answer.)

    Subtracting works the same way. A painter has 7/8 liter and gives away 1/4 liter. 1/4 = 2/8, so 7/8 - 2/8 = 5/8 liter is left.

    To subtract a fraction from a whole number, write the whole number over 1 and change it to the same denominator. 5 - 2/7: 5 = 35/7, so 35/7 - 2/7 = 33/7 = 4 5/7.

    Check itAdd your answer back. If 5/8 + 1/4 = 7/8, your subtraction is right.

    Multiplying and dividing fractions (2.4.0)

    You do not need a common denominator to multiply or divide.

    • Multiply: top times top, bottom times bottom, then reduce. 3/4 x 7/8 = 21/32. Reducing first makes the numbers smaller and easier.
    • Divide: invert (flip) the second fraction, change the division sign to multiplication, then multiply and reduce. 7/16 / 7/8 = 7/16 x 8/7 = 56/112 = 1/2.
    • Fraction by a whole number: put the whole number over 1 and use the same steps. 1/2 / 4 = 1/2 x 1/4 = 1/8.
    • Mixed numbers: change them to improper fractions first.

    Check a division with multiplication: the answer times the divisor should give the original dividend.

    Scale drawingsIf 1/4" on a drawing stands for 1', then a line 8 1/2" long stands for 8 1/2 / 1/4 = 17/2 x 4 = 34 feet.
    StripsHow many 7/8" strips come from a 7" piece? 7 / 7/8 = 7 x 8/7 = 8 strips.

      Module Section 3 of 6

      The Decimal System

      By the end of this section your student can read and round decimals, calculate with them, and convert between decimals, fractions and percentages.

      A

      Read it together

      about 45 min

      The number 10 is at the heart of the decimal system. Decimals give simpler calculations and easier fractions, and they match the metric system, which is built on tens.

      Decimals (3.1.0)

      A decimal has a decimal point (the dot). Digits to the left are a whole number. Digits to the right are part of 1, which is why decimals are also called decimal fractions. If you do not see a decimal point, assume one to the right of the ones place.

      Places to the right of the point: tenths, hundredths, thousandths, ten-thousandths, hundred-thousandths, millionths. Everyday work usually needs two places. Extreme precision may need five.

      • Say the number as written, then name the place of the last digit: 0.56 is "fifty-six hundredths."
      • The word "and" marks the decimal point in a mixed number: 15.7 is "fifteen and seven-tenths."
      • A zero in front of the point (0.56) adds clarity. It is not spoken.

      Rounding decimals (3.1.1)

      1. Underline the place you are rounding to.
      2. Look at the digit one place to the right.
      3. If that digit is 5 or more, add 1 to the underlined digit. If it is 4 or less, leave the underlined digit alone.
      4. Drop everything to the right.

      Example: tubing costs 3.76 euros per meter and the budget is 800 euros. 800 / 3.76 = 212.7659574. To the nearest tenth, look at the 6 that follows the 7: round up to 212.8. A supplier will not sell you 0.7659574 of a meter.

      Standard rulesBudgets sometimes follow their own rounding rules to avoid overruns. Unless you are told otherwise, follow the standard rounding rules.

      Comparing decimals (3.1.2)

      Compare them digit by digit from the left, just like whole numbers. 0.4217 is larger than 0.4214. To order a list, it helps to add zeros so every number has the same number of places: 0.402, 0.420, 0.042, 0.442 becomes smallest to largest 0.042, 0.402, 0.420, 0.442.

      Adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing decimals (3.2.0)

      • Add or subtract: keep the decimal points lined up, and add zeros so the columns match. 4.76 + 0.834 = 5.594, with the answer's point in the same column. Borrowing works the same as with whole numbers.
      • Multiply: multiply as if there were no decimal points. Then count the digits to the right of the point in both numbers and put the answer's point that many places from the right. 4.5 x 7 = 31.5 (one place). If the answer has too few digits, add zeros on the left: 0.507 x 0.022 = 0.011154.
      • Divide: if the divisor (the number you divide by) has a decimal point, move it all the way to the right and move the dividend's point the same number of places, adding zeros as needed. Then put the answer's point directly above the dividend's point and divide as usual. 44.5 / 0.22 becomes 4,450 / 22 = 202 remainder 6.
      Check itMultiply the quotient by the divisor and add the remainder. You should get the original dividend.

      Calculator order: enter the first number, press the operation key, enter the second number, press equals. Clear with the On or C key. CE clears only the last entry. Example: 45.6 x 5.7 = 259.92.

      Dots and commasIn the United States the dot is the decimal point and commas separate groups of three digits. Many European, African and South American countries do the opposite, so be alert when you read paperwork from outside the US.

      Converting decimals, fractions and percentages (3.3.0)

      A percentage is a part of a whole divided into 100 parts. The decimal 0.25, the percentage 25% and the fraction 1/4 all mean the same quantity.

      • Decimal to percent (3.3.1): multiply by 100 (move the point two places right) and add a % sign. 0.12 = 12%.
      • Percent to decimal (3.3.1): drop the % sign and divide by 100 (move the point two places left). 22% = 0.22.
      • Fraction to decimal (3.3.2): divide the numerator by the denominator. 3/4 = 3 / 4 = 0.75, which is 75%.
      • Decimal to fraction (3.3.3): say it aloud, write it as a fraction, then reduce. 0.125 is "one hundred twenty-five thousandths," or 125/1000, which reduces to 1/8. So a 1/8" drill bit makes a 0.125" hole.
      • Inches to decimal feet (3.3.4): put the inches over 12 and divide. 3 inches = 3/12 = 1/4 = 0.25 foot.
      Percent on a calculatorEither enter the percent and press the % key, or convert to a decimal first. Never do both. $175,000 x 14% = $175,000 x 0.14 = $24,500.
      Profit exampleA house sells for $175,000. Overhead 27%, materials 35%, labor 24% = 86% of the price. Profit is the other 14%, or $24,500.
      Direct and indirect costsDirect costs belong to the job (materials, crew labor, subcontractors). Indirect costs, or overhead, keep the company running (insurance, office staff, marketing, property taxes).

        Measuring Length

        By the end of this section your student can read English and metric rulers and measuring tapes and use a tape measure correctly.

        A

        Read it together

        about 30 min

        Rulers and tape measures are the most common measuring tools on any job. The module's warning: people who use them only now and then forget what the small marks mean, and a misread ruler means errors, expensive mistakes and wasted material.

        Start at the markMany rulers have extra material at the ends in case the ends get damaged. Always start a measurement at the first marked line, not the physical end of the ruler.

        Vocabulary note: inch-pound, English and Imperial all name the same measuring system (the one used in the US, Canada and a few other places). This section uses "English" for rulers and tapes.

        The English ruler (4.1.1)

        English rulers divide each inch into halves, fourths, eighths and sixteenths. Some also show thirty-seconds or sixty-fourths. Everything except the whole-inch marks is a fraction of an inch.

        • On a ruler with 1/16" marks, count the marks past the last whole inch. The mark just after 3/4" is 13/16".
        • The distance between 1/4" and 3/8" is 2/16" (that is, 1/8").
        • Reduce what you read: 4/16" is 1/4" and 8/16" is 1/2".

        The metric ruler (4.1.2)

        Metric rulers show centimeters (the larger numbered lines) and millimeters (the small lines). Every measurement is built on 10: each millimeter is 1/10 of a centimeter, so 10 mm = 1 cm.

        • 7 marks after the 2 cm line is 2.7 cm, which is also 27 mm.
        • 6 marks before the 1 cm line is 0.4 cm, or 4 mm.

        The measuring tape (4.2.0)

        Tapes may be English, metric or both. Tapes that carry both systems commonly come in 16 ft (5 m) and 25 ft (8 m) lengths. Site layout uses long tapes. Carpenters and pipefitters usually prefer short, compact ones.

        Special marks on an English tape (4.2.1)
        MarkWhat it means
        Black background every 24 in24-inch spacing on center, most common for walls that do not carry a heavy load.
        Red background every 16 in16-inch spacing on center, most common for loadbearing walls.
        Small black diamond every 19.2 inAn alternate, less common spacing for loadbearing walls and special joists.
        Red foot number with a red inch numberQuick reading. Red 1F with red 2 means 1 foot 2 inches. The black number beneath it (14) is the same distance in inches.

        With the hook against the left side of one stud, the left side of the next stud is 16 inches away, so the studs are on 16-inch centers.

        Nominal and true sizesA 2x4 is not really 2" by 4". After drying and planing it measures 1 1/2" by 3 1/2" (38 x 89 mm). Other examples from the module: a 2x6 is 1 1/2" x 5 1/2", a 2x8 is 1 1/2" x 7 1/4", and a 4x4 is 3 1/2" x 3 1/2".

        Metric tapes look like English ones and also carry framing marks, spaced to fit other building codes. In the US, metric-only tapes are rare and a combination tape is the usual choice.

        Using a tape measure (4.2.3)

        1. Pull the tape straight out of the case with one hand.
        2. Hook the metal end on the object (a second worker may need to hold it). The hook is meant to slide slightly: in for inside measurements and out for outside ones. If it binds, the reading could be off by as much as 1/16" (1.6 mm). A bent or damaged hook does the same.
        3. Pull the case to extend the tape a little past the measurement point.
        4. Slide the thumb lock to hold it (some tapes lock automatically and use a thumb release).
        5. Read the measurement at the correct spot on the object.
        • Never let the tape snap back into the case, because the hook can break.
        • Inside measurements (like a shelf between two walls): press the hook against one wall and the back of the case against the other. Read where the tape enters the case, then add the length of the case, which is stamped on its side.
        • The curve of the blade keeps it stiff. How far a tape reaches without bending is called its standout.
        Measure twice, cut onceDouble-check every measurement before you cut.

          Metric and Inch-Pound Measurement Systems

          By the end of this section your student can name common length, weight, volume and temperature units in both systems and convert between them.

          A

          Read it together

          about 50 min

          Most of the world uses the metric system, and many manufacturers list sizes, weights, temperatures and pressures in metric units, inch-pound units, or both. Many crafts need workers who can switch between the two. A measurement always needs a unit. A number alone is not a measurement.

          Metric is simpler because scaling up or down always means multiplying or dividing by 10s. Inch-pound needs many different factors (a mile is 5,280 feet, an inch is 1/12 of a foot).

          Metric prefixes (Table 1)
          PrefixMeans
          micro- (u)one-millionth (0.000001)
          milli- (m)one-thousandth (0.001)
          centi- (c)one-hundredth (0.01)
          deci- (d)one-tenth (0.1)
          deka- (da)10
          hecto- (h)100
          kilo- (k)1,000
          mega- (M)1,000,000
          giga- (G)1,000,000,000

          Length (5.1.0)

          Inch-pound (5.1.1): 12 inches = 1 foot. 3 feet = 36 inches = 1 yard. 5,280 feet = 1,760 yards = 1 mile. Abbreviations: in or ", ft or ', yd, mi. To change inches to yards, divide by 36.

          Metric (5.1.2): 10 mm = 1 cm. 100 cm = 1 m. 1,000 m = 1 km. Abbreviations: mm, cm, m, km. All units relate by 10s, so convert by moving the decimal point.

          • Larger to smaller unit: move the point right (you get more of the smaller unit). 0.45 m = 45 cm.
          • Smaller to larger unit: move the point left. 72 cm = 0.72 m (two places, because centi- is 0.01).

          Between systems (5.1.3): multiply or divide by a conversion factor from a table. Key factors: 1 inch = 2.54 cm, 1 cm = 0.3937 in, 1 foot = 0.3048 m, 1 mile = 1.609 km. Example: 13 cm x 0.3937 = 5.1181 in, which you might round to 5.12 or 5.1 depending on the job. A steel plate needs more precision than a mailbox post.

          Picture the metric units1 mm is about the thickness of a dime. 1 cm is about the width of a paper clip. 1 m is about the distance from a door handle to the floor. 1 km is about the length of six city blocks.

          Weight (5.2.0)

          Weight is the force an object puts on a surface because of its mass and the pull of Earth's gravity. Mass is the quantity of matter present.

          Inch-pound (5.2.1): 16 ounces (oz) = 1 pound (lb). 2,000 pounds = 1 ton (t).

          • Ounces to pounds: divide by 16. 134 oz / 16 = 8.375 lb.
          • Pounds to ounces: multiply by 16.
          • Pounds to tons: divide by 2,000.

          Metric (5.2.2): 1,000 milligrams (mg) = 1 gram (g). 1,000 grams = 1 kilogram (kg). 1,000 kg = 1 metric ton. To change 6,439 mg to grams, divide by 1,000 (move the point three places left) to get 6.439 g.

          Weight factors between systems (5.2.3, Table 7)
          1 of thisequals
          ounce28.35 grams
          pound453.6 grams (0.4536 kg)
          kilogram2.205 pounds
          ton907 kilograms (907,185 g)

          Worked examples:

          • A crane load weighs 1,200 kg. 1,200 x 2.205 = 2,646 lb, so the slings must be rated for more than 2,646 lb.
          • Ounces to grams: multiply by 28.35. Grams to ounces: divide by 28.35.
          • 50 lb x 0.4536 = 22.68 kg. 50 kg x 2.205 = 110.25 lb.
          Water and the gramOne milliliter of pure water weighs exactly one gram. Other liquids weigh different amounts.
          Watch the directionA kilogram is bigger than a pound (1 kg is about 2.2 lb). Ask yourself whether your answer should be larger or smaller before you accept it.

          Volume (5.3.0)

          Volume is the amount of space inside a three-dimensional object. Volume units are length units with the word "cubic" added. A cube has all sides the same length: a cube 1" x 1" x 1" has a volume of 1 cubic inch.

          Inch-pound (5.3.1): 1 cubic foot = 1,728 cubic inches. 1 cubic yard = 27 cubic feet. Abbreviations: cu in or in3, cu ft or ft3, cu yd or yd3.

          Metric (5.3.2): the cubic centimeter (cm3) and cubic meter (m3) are the main units. A cubic meter is a cube 100 cm x 100 cm x 100 cm, so 1 m3 = 1,000,000 cm3.

          Between systems (5.3.3): use the conversion chart. Examples: 1,600 cm3 x 0.061 = 97.6 in3, so a 1,600 cm3 battery fits in a 112 in3 box. 59 yd3 x 0.765 = 45.1 m3. Normally you convert between units of similar size (cubic yards to cubic meters).

          Temperature (5.4.0)

          Temperature measures how fast the atoms or molecules in something are moving. Fast means hot. Slow means cold. Degrees have a symbol and a letter naming the scale (°F, °C).

          The four temperature scales (Figure 22)
          ScaleWater freezesWater boilsRange
          Fahrenheit32°F212°F180°
          Celsius0°C100°C100°
          Rankine491.7°R671.7°R180°
          Kelvin273 K373 K100
          • Absolute zero is the coldest possible temperature, where atoms and molecules barely move. Rankine and Kelvin both start there.
          • A Rankine degree is the same size as a Fahrenheit degree. A kelvin is the same size as a Celsius degree. Kelvin has no degree symbol and you do not say "degrees" for it.
          • Fahrenheit and Celsius are the ones most crafts use. Celsius and Kelvin belong to the metric system.

          The conversion math. Between freezing and boiling there are 180° on the Fahrenheit scale and 100° on the Celsius scale, a ratio of 9 to 5. That is where the formulas come from:

          1. Fahrenheit to Celsius: subtract 32, then multiply by 5/9. (C = 5/9 x (F - 32))
          2. Celsius to Fahrenheit: multiply by 9/5, then add 32. (F = (9/5 x C) + 32)

          Module examples: 77°F to Celsius: 77 - 32 = 45, then 5/9 x 45 = 25°C. 90°C to Fahrenheit: 9/5 x 90 = 162, then 162 + 32 = 194°F.

          Order mattersGoing to Celsius, subtract the 32 first. Going to Fahrenheit, add the 32 last. A quick check: water freezes at 32°F and 0°C, and boils at 212°F and 100°C. If your answer disagrees, recheck your steps.
          Reading thermometersA digital probe and a non-contact infrared thermometer can show both Celsius and Fahrenheit. The infrared kind measures the invisible infrared radiation an object gives off.

            Introduction to Geometry

            By the end of this section your student can classify angles, name common shapes, and calculate perimeter, area and volume.

            A

            Read it together

            about 50 min

            Geometry is practical math. It deals with circles, triangles, squares and rectangles, and the construction industry uses it constantly. Plane geometry studies flat, two-dimensional shapes (length and width). Solid geometry studies three-dimensional shapes (length, width and height).

            Angles (6.1.0)

            An angle is the shape made by two straight lines that meet at a point called the vertex. It is measured in degrees (°) with a protractor. A full circle is 360°.

            Angle types (Figure 24)
            TypeWhat it means
            AcuteMore than 0° and less than 90°. Common ones are 30°, 45° and 60°.
            RightExactly 90°. The two lines are perpendicular, like the sides of a capital L. Drawings mark it with a small square at the vertex. The crafts use it constantly.
            ObtuseMore than 90° and less than 180°.
            StraightExactly 180°, a flat line.
            AdjacentTwo angles that share a vertex and one side.
            OppositeAngles formed when two straight lines cross. They are always equal.

            An angle of 66° is acute because it is less than 90°.

            Common shapes (6.2.0)

            • Rectangle (6.2.1): four sides and four 90° angles that add to 360°. Opposite sides are parallel and equal. Its two diagonals (corner to opposite corner) are equal, and a diagonal cuts it into two identical right triangles.
            • Square (6.2.2): a rectangle with four equal sides. A diagonal cuts it into two right triangles, each with two 45° angles and one 90° angle. (Also, the "square" of a number is the number times itself.)
            • Perimeter: the distance around a shape. For a rectangle, add the four sides, or use (2 x length) + (2 x width). A 14' x 12' room has a perimeter of 52', so buy 52' of molding. For a square, multiply one side by 4.
            • Triangle (6.2.3): three sides and three angles that always add to 180°. A right triangle has one 90° angle. An equilateral triangle has three equal sides and angles (60° each). An isosceles triangle has two equal sides and two equal angles. A scalene triangle has three unequal sides.
            • Circle (6.2.4): every point on the curved line is the same distance from the center. The circumference is the distance around it. The diameter crosses the circle through the center (the longest straight line inside). The radius goes from the center to the edge and equals half the diameter. Pi is about 3.14 (or 22/7) and is used for both the area and the circumference.

            Circumference = pi x diameter. It applies only to circles, while perimeter applies to every shape.

            Rope stretchersThe word geometry comes from Greek words meaning "earth measuring." Ancient Egyptian surveyors, called rope stretchers, used knotted ropes to replace land markers after the Nile flooded each year.

            Calculating area (6.3.0)

            Area is the space inside a flat shape. It is measured in square units: sq in (in2), sq ft (ft2), sq yd, sq cm, sq m. Carpet layers and painters work out area before ordering material.

            Radius, not diameterThe circle formula uses the radius. If you are given the diameter, cut it in half first. A 14-foot-diameter circle has a 7-foot radius: 3.14 x 7 x 7 = 153.86 ft2.
            Check with diagonalsBefore applying a sheet of sheathing, measure both diagonals. If they match, the sheet is a true rectangle.

            Volume of three-dimensional shapes (6.4.0)

            Volume is the space inside a 3D object, in cubic units. Before you multiply, put every measurement in the same unit. A slab 4 ft x 6 ft x 6 in must use 6 in = 0.5 ft: 4 x 6 x 0.5 = 12 ft3.

            • Three-dimensional rectangle (6.4.1): length x width x depth. A slab 50' x 40' x 6" thick: area 2,000 ft2 x 0.5 ft = 1,000 ft3.
            • Cube (6.4.2): side x side x side. An 8-foot cube is 512 ft3.
            • Cylinder (6.4.3): pi x radius x radius x height. A tank 22 ft across (radius 11 ft) and 10 ft high: 3.14 x 121 x 10 = 3,799.4 ft3.
            • Triangular prism (6.4.4): 1/2 x base x height x depth. A base of 6 cm, height of 12 cm and depth of 2 cm gives 1/2 x 6 x 12 = 36 cm2, then x 2 = 72 cm3.

            Concrete is sold in cubic yards, so divide cubic feet by 27 to convert. 512 ft3 / 27 = 18.96 yd3.

            The 3-4-5 ruleTo check that a corner is square, mark 3 units along one side and 4 units along the other. The diagonal between the marks must be exactly 5 units. Multiples work too: 9-12-15, 12-16-20 and 15-20-25.
