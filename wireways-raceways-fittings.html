Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module topics.
  2. Study review points. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each comes twice: plain English first, then the textbook's words.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do electrical work. The NEC is for trained people working under proper guidance. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. This includes powder-actuated tools, which only trained and licensed operators may use. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Conduit, Boxes, and Supports

By the end of this section your student can explain why PVC is chosen and what its limits are, why metal raceways are bonded, what a standoff support does, and how conduit is routed in metal buildings.

A

Read it together

about 25 min

This section is about the pipe and the hardware that holds it: what conduit is made of, how it is grounded, and how it is supported on different kinds of buildings.

Rigid PVC conduit (1.1.7)

The most common rigid nonmetallic conduit is made of polyvinyl chloride (PVC), covered in NEC Article 352. PVC does not corrode, is chemically inert, and does not age, so it is often used in wet or corrosive places. It can break down in extreme sunlight unless it is marked sunlight resistant. It is marked to NEMA or UL standards.

  • Lighter and faster. PVC is lighter than steel, aluminum, IMC or EMT, and joints are made with cement, so no threading is needed.
  • No metal. That lowers the voltage drop of AC conductors compared with the same conductors in steel conduit.
  • Not a ground. PVC does not conduct, so it can not be the equipment grounding conductor. One sized by NEC Table 250.122 must be pulled in each PVC run (except underground service-entrance conductors).
  • It moves with heat. PVC expands and contracts with temperature, so expansion couplings are used. One kind has O-rings inside and may allow up to 6 inches of movement. Even a 10 foot run in a moderate climate needs an expansion joint per the NEC. Check the local rules first.

Key ideaPVC is great against corrosion and fast to install, but it is not a grounding path and it grows and shrinks with temperature.

Bonding conduit (1.2.0)

Most powered equipment with a metal frame is bonded: its frame is connected to the grounding point at the service-entrance equipment. That is done with a wire (the equipment grounding conductor), by bonding the frame to a metal conduit or raceway, or both. A metal raceway can act as the equipment grounding conductor because it gives an uninterrupted, low-impedance path.

NEC 250.96 says metal raceways, enclosures, frames, fittings and other metal parts that serve as the ground path must be bonded so they stay electrically continuous and can safely carry fault current. Paint, enamel or similar coatings must be removed at threads and contact points, or fittings made so removal is unnecessary must be used.

Why it mattersThe ground path gives fault current an easy way back. If a hot conductor touches the equipment frame, the overcurrent device opens the circuit right away, and the voltage a person would feel on the frame is lower.

Standoff supports (1.5.2)

A standoff support (often called a Minerallac, a manufacturer's name) holds conduit away from the surface it hangs on. With one-hole and two-hole straps the conduit must be offset at every fitting. With standoff supports the conduit sits away from the surface, so no offsets are needed at fittings. They can support RMC, IMC, EMT, PVC and flex, and tubing.

Metal buildings (1.6.4)

Many commercial and industrial buildings are prefabricated steel with light-gauge metal roofing and siding. Conduit can run across the roof's structural members, which are beams and purlins or open-web steel joists.

  • Under metal-corrugated roof decking, NEC 300.4(E) keeps the nearest surface of the cable or raceway at least 1 1/2 inches from the decking.
  • Do not drill through beams and purlins. Use anchoring devices made for them, with clamps to hold the conduit.
  • Exposed runs should be plumb, with correct bends and a neat, orderly look.
  • RMC is often required in metal buildings.
  • Many conduits on one path use strut systems (Unistrut and B-Line are brand names), hung from the ceiling on threaded rod. Strut can also be fastened to masonry walls for vertical runs.

    Fasteners and Anchors

    By the end of this section your student can choose between screws and anchors for wood, metal, masonry and hollow walls, and state the safety rules for stud-type guns.

    A

    Read it together

    about 25 min

    Boxes, panels and supports have to be fastened to something. The right fastener depends on what is behind it: wood, metal, concrete or an empty wall.

    Wood screws (2.2.2)

    Wood screws hold better than nails and let equipment be removed later. The shank size usually follows the hole in the box or panel. A good rule is to pick a screw long enough that about 2/3 of its length goes into the wood. Do not drill the pilot hole too large, or the screw loses holding power. Drill it about two-thirds the length of the threaded part. Soap on the threads makes the screw easier to drive.

    Lag screws and shields (2.2.3)

    Lag screws (lag bolts) are heavy-duty wood screws with square or hex heads. They hold heavy equipment to wood, or to concrete when a lag shield is used. A lag shield is a tube split lengthwise but joined at one end. It sits in a predrilled hole in the concrete, and when the lag screw goes in the shield expands and grips.

    • Hard masonry: short shields save drilling time. Soft or weak masonry: longer shields for more holding strength.
    • Lag screw length = thickness of the item being fastened + length of the shield.
    • Drill the hole slightly deeper than the shield (follow the manufacturer).
    • If the head rests on wood, put a flat washer under it, and count the washer's thickness when choosing the length.

    Thread-forming and thread-cutting screws (2.2.5)

    Thread-forming screws, commonly called sheet metal screws, are hard metal. They form their own thread as they are driven, so no tapping is needed. Use the right drill bit for the pilot hole (usually printed on the box). Some also drill their own hole. They fasten light-gauge metal.

    Thread-cutting screws are hardened steel with blunt points and fine threads. They cut threads by removing some metal as they go into a pilot hole. They join heavy-gauge metals, metals of different gauges, and nonferrous metals, and fasten sheet metal to building structure.

    Stud-type guns (2.4.0)

    Stud-type guns (powder, gas, rocket fuel or spring) drive pins and threaded studs into masonry and steel. A powder-actuated tool fires a gunpowder load (typically .22, .25 or .27 caliber). How deep the fastener goes depends on how dense the base material is and how strong the load is.

    • Loads and cases are made for specific tools and are not interchangeable.
    • Loads come in 12 power levels, color coded, and different makers can use different colors. Level 1 is lowest, 12 is highest.
    SafetyPowder-actuated tools may be used only by trained and licensed operators, following the manual. Carry your license. OSHA 29 CFR 1926.302(e) requires training on the specific tool.

    Rules from the module include:

    • Never put your hand over the muzzle end. Never override safety features.
    • Operators and bystanders wear eye and hearing protection and hard hats.
    • Post Powder-Actuated Tool in Use signs within 50 feet of the work.
    • Do a center punch test. Test fire with the lowest recommended power level and go up one level at a time.
    • Hold the tool perpendicular, use the spall guard, never fire through an existing hole or into a weld, and never use it in an explosive or flammable area.
    • Never leave a loaded tool unattended. To unload, remove the powder load first, then the fastener.
    • After a misfire, hold the tool against the work for at least 30 seconds.
    • Store loads and the unloaded tool under lock and key.
    Case historyAn apprentice was killed when a nail from a powder-actuated tool passed through a hollow wall and traveled nearly 30 feet. The operator had no training, and nobody in the area wore protective equipment. These tools are designed mainly for masonry.

    Toggle bolts (2.6.1)

    Toggle bolts fasten items to hollow walls and ceilings. A slotted bolt or screw has spring-loaded wings that open behind the wall as the bolt is tightened. Drill a hole just big enough for the folded wings to pass. Do not completely unscrew the bolt once installed, because the wings fall off and the fastener is useless. Plastic toggle bolts need a smaller hole, and their wings stay in place if the screw is removed.

    1. Pick the right drill bit and toggle bolt, and check for damaged or dirty threads or a bad wing.
    2. Drill completely through the surface.
    3. Put the bolt through the item, then screw the wing on (flat side facing the head).
    4. Fold the wings back and push through until they spring open.
    5. Pull back on the item to hold the wings against the inside surface.
    6. Tighten with a screwdriver until snug.

    Sleeve-type wall anchors (2.6.2)

    Sleeve-type anchors work in concrete, block, plywood, wallboard and hollow tile. The standard type goes in a drilled hole and is tapped until its gripper prongs bite, then the screw draws it tight. The drive type is hammered in with no hole to drill. Then the screw is removed, the item is put in place, and the screw is put back through it.

      Wireways and Other Specialty Raceways

      By the end of this section your student can describe what a wireway is, the fill limits, how often it must be supported, and what an underground duct system includes.

      A

      Read it together

      about 20 min

      A wireway is a metal box-shaped trough for running many wires from one place to another. This section covers how full it can be, how it is held up, and the underground duct systems that carry power below ground.

      Wireways (3.0.0)

      A wireway is a sheet metal trough with a hinged or screw-on removable cover. It houses wires and cables and comes in various lengths. Metal wireways are covered in NEC Article 376 (nonmetallic wireways in Article 378).

      Wireway numbers to know
      RuleLimit
      Conductors and cables at any cross section (376.22(A))No more than 20% of the inside cross-section
      Conductors plus splices and taps (376.56(A))No more than 75% of the cross-section
      Derating (Table 310.15(C)(1))Only when more than 30 current-carrying conductors
      • No conductor larger than the wireway is designed for may be installed.
      • Extensions from a wireway to receptacles or controls use any Chapter 3 wiring method that includes a means for equipment grounding (376.70).
      • The manufacturer's name or trademark must be visible (376.120).

      An auxiliary gutter (NEC Article 366) is a wireway that adds wiring space at switchboards, meters and other distribution points. Its parts match a wireway's, but it is part of an assembly and may hold only conductors or busbars. A wireway carries conductors between points that are far apart.

      Wireway supports (3.2.0)

      Horizontal wireways must be supported securely at each end and at intervals of no more than 5 feet, unless listed for other intervals. The support distance can never be more than 10 feet (NEC 376.30(A)). Wireways can be mounted straight to a surface, or held by hangers or brackets.

      • Suspended hanger (3.2.1): hung from a ceiling or beam on threaded rod with hex nuts, with a beam clamp or anchor at the top.
      • Gusset bracket (3.2.2): an L-shaped wall bracket. The wireway rests on it and is fastened with screws or bolts.
      • Standard hanger (3.2.3): a two-piece hanger combined different ways. The wireway is bolted to it.
      • Wireway hanger (3.2.4): for larger wireways. A piece of strut hangs from threaded rods on beam clamps or ceiling anchors.

      Duct systems (3.3.7)

      A duct is a single enclosed raceway for conductors. Underground duct systems also include manholes, transformer vaults and risers. A duct is conduit or an approved system (such as HDPE) in a trench, covered by earth or concrete. NEC Table 300.5 gives the minimum depth. Concrete around the duct adds strength and helps dissipate heat.

      • Manholes are set at intervals along a duct run, give workers room to install and maintain conductors, and are entered from above through a throat. They are usually brick or concrete and must allow for drainage. A handhole is a similar smaller opening used for splicing.
      • Transformer vaults house transformers, voltage regulators, network protectors, meters and circuit breakers.
      • Duct can be fiber, vitrified tile, rigid metal or nonmetallic conduit, or poured concrete. Inside diameters run 2" to 6" (MD 53 to 155).
      • PVC duct is popular: easy, less labor and low cost, in longer lengths with fewer couplings.
      • Cable-in-duct comes on a reel with the cables already installed. It is most used for street lighting.
      SafetyIn older installations, asbestos/cement duct may have been used. You must be certified to remove or disturb asbestos.

          Handling Raceways

          By the end of this section your student can describe how to move and protect raceway so it is not damaged before it is installed.

          A

          Read it together

          about 10 min

          Raceway is made to strict specifications and careless handling can damage or contaminate it. Use safe handling from delivery until installation is complete.

          Handling raceways (5.1.0)

          • Never drag raceway off a delivery truck or off other lengths, and never drag it on the ground or floor. It damages the ends.
          • Keep thread protection caps on while handling or moving conduit.
          • Keep raceway away from anything that could contaminate it.
          • Flag the ends of long lengths when carrying them to the job site.
          • Never drop, throw, or hit raceway against other objects.
          • Always use two people for long pieces. Stay on the same side with a balanced load, each about 1/4 of the length from an end, and lift and set down together.
