Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do electrical work. The NEC is for trained people working under proper guidance. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 2

Outlet Boxes

By the end of this section your student can name the types of outlet boxes and what each is for, work out how many wires a box can hold, and say how outlet boxes are installed.

A

Read it together

about 20 min

Every electrical job needs boxes. This section is about the kinds of boxes your student will meet, how to count how many wires a box can safely hold, and the rules for putting boxes in.

Why the right box matters

Every box on a job has to be sized, installed and supported to meet the current NEC. The NEC limits the number of conductors, fittings and devices allowed in each box, and the limit depends on the box's size and volume. That is why knowing the types of boxes, and the space inside each one, is essential.

Electrical drawings rarely show the exact type of box, except for boxes in hazardous locations. The electrician has to choose, and a poor choice costs time. For example, a box used with Type AC cable should have built-in clamps. If it is ordered with knockouts only, it needs extra connectors, and every connection takes extra seconds that add up over a large project. Labor is expensive, so extra labor costs more than any money saved on the wrong materials.

Key ideaPlug raceway openings with capped bushings or similar protection so debris does not get in. Tighten all fittings and couplings. All openings in boxes must be closed as Section 314.17(A) requires, and knockout closures are used for this.

The types of boxes

Outlet boxes normally fall into three categories: pressed steel boxes with knockouts of various sizes, cast iron, aluminum or brass boxes with threaded hubs, and nonmetallic boxes. Pressed steel boxes are made either for conduit, electric metallic tubing and cable, or for use with specific surface metal raceways.

BoxWhat to know
Octagon and round (1.1.1)Octagon boxes are 4 inches (100 mm) wide, in depths of 1¼, 1½ or 2⅛ inches. Used mostly for wall-mounted lighting fixtures (luminaires). Round boxes may not have conduit or connectors that need locknuts and bushings entering the side. Those must enter at the top. Round boxes with cable clamps may take Type AC and NM cable at the side or top.
Square (1.1.2)Typically 4 inches (100 mm) and 411⁄16 inches (120 mm) square, in depths of 1¼, 1½ and 2⅛ inches. Extension rings add depth. Single or two-gang device rings (plaster or tile rings) let them hold receptacles or switches. Blank covers make them junction boxes.
Device (1.1.3)Houses switches and receptacles. Made for flush mounting, mainly in homes and some commercial buildings. Some have integral nails for wall studs. In metal studs use self-tapping sheet metal screws. In concrete, special powder-actuated fasteners may be needed.
Handy box (1.1.3)A single-gang box with the mounting ears on the inside. Also called a utility box. Depths of 1½, 1⅞ and 2⅛ inches. Its small volume limits how many conductors it can hold.
Masonry (1.1.4)Used in flat-slab concrete work. A sleeve with outside ears, plus a plate attached after the sleeve is nailed to the deck. Choose one tall enough that the knockouts sit well above the reinforcing rods, so the conduit needs no offsets.

Box covers adapt a box to its job. For example, a 4-inch square box becomes a one-gang or two-gang switch or receptacle box with the right flush device cover.

Good to knowFire-rated walls: boxes on opposite sides of a fire-rated wall between occupancies should be at least 24 inches (600 mm) apart to protect the fire rating.

Holding up lights and fans

Boxes that support luminaires (lighting fixtures) must be listed for the purpose. Most device boxes are not designed or listed to do that (Section 314.27(A)). A floor box has to be listed for floor use wherever receptacles or junction boxes go in a floor, and it comes with a cover and gaskets to keep out surface water and cleaning compounds.

A box at a fan outlet cannot be the only support for a ceiling fan unless it is listed for that job. A ceiling fan up to 70 pounds (32 kg) may hang from a listed outlet box identified for the purpose. A box designed to support more than 35 pounds (16 kg) must be marked with the maximum weight it can hold. The box must be rigidly supported from a structural member of the building (Section 314.27(C)). A paddle-fan box for an existing ceiling uses a bar hanger that is placed between two joists and tightened.

SafetyIf a box is not marked for the weight, do not hang a fan from it. Ask a qualified person to confirm the box is listed and marked for the load.

Wet and damp locations

TypeWhat it meansExamples from the module
WetA location subject to saturation with water or other liquidsPlaces exposed to weather or water, washrooms, garages, interiors that might be hosed down, underground, and in concrete slabs or masonry touching the earth
DampA location subject to some moisturePartially protected outdoor spots such as under canopies, marquees and roofed open porches. Some basements, some barns, cold storage warehouses.

Boxes in wet locations must be listed for the use (Section 314.15). Boxes and fittings in damp or wet places must keep moisture from getting in and collecting. Nonconductive boxes are recommended with nonmetallic sheathed cable or nonmetallic conduit where occasional moisture is likely. Boxes with threaded hubs and gasketed covers normally keep water out, except for condensation inside.

All 15A and 20A, 125 to 250V receptacles in wet locations need a cover that is weatherproof whether or not the receptacle is in use (Sections 406.9(A) and (B)). Raintight or watertight equipment may satisfy the weatherproof requirement. Drip-tight boxes only deflect falling rain and are not waterproof. Watertight boxes use gaskets to keep water out from any angle.

How many wires fit in a box

NEC Table 314.16(A) lists the most conductors allowed in standard boxes, but only when the box has no fittings or devices in it. In real wiring, boxes hold things, and each one takes up room. Add up the conductor equivalents:

What is in the boxCounts as
Each conductor1
Each looped conductor over 12 inches (300 mm)2
Each strap-mounted device (switch, duplex receptacle)2
Each fixture stud or hickey1
Up to four grounding conductors, all together1
One or more cable clamps, all together1

A wire that runs through the box without a splice or tap counts as one conductor. A wire that enters, is spliced or connects to a terminal, and leaves counts as two. Wires that start and stop in the same box (pigtails) are not counted, and neither are grounding clips and screws, wire nuts, cable connectors held by locknuts, or prewired fixture wires. Add a ¼ volume allowance for each grounding conductor over four. Raised device covers and extension rings (Figure 9) add room.

Worked exampleA 3" × 2" × 3½" box holds eight No. 12 wires. Add a cable clamp (1) and a duplex receptacle (2): subtract three, leaving five. Add a ground wire and only four No. 12 wires may be used.
Worked exampleTwo No. 12 conductors enter a box that has one receptacle. That is 2 + 2 = 4 conductor equivalents. A No. 12 conductor needs 2.25 cubic inches (36.9 cm³), so 4 × 2.25 = 9.00 cubic inches (147.6 cm³). The closest box in Table 314.16(A) is a 3" × 2" × 2" box rated at 10.0 cubic inches (164 cm³). With four No. 12 conductors entering, the count is 6, so 6 × 2.25 = 13.5 cubic inches (221 cm³). A 3" × 2" × 2¾" device box rated at 14.0 cubic inches (230 cm³) fits. Any larger box is also allowed.

Installing outlet boxes

Beyond box fill, the NEC has other requirements (Sections 314.15 through 314.30):

  • The box must be listed for its use. A box in a wet location must be listed for wet locations.
  • It must have enough volume (Table 314.16(A)) and enough free space for the conductors (Table 314.16(B)).
  • Conductors entering boxes and fittings must be protected from abrasion.
  • Boxes must be installed and supported properly, and the finished job must be accessible for later repair or maintenance.

Boxes in walls or ceilings of concrete, tile or other noncombustible material must not be set back more than ¼ inch (6 mm) from the finished surface (Section 314.20). Gaps or damage around a box in drywall or plaster must be repaired so there are no gaps wider than ⅛ inch (3 mm) between the box and the wall material (Section 314.21). Repair before the faceplate goes on, with a noncombustible caulking or spackling compound.

Section 210.52 sets the receptacle layout for the main rooms of a dwelling. In most living spaces, no point along the floor line of a wall may be more than 6 feet (1.8 m) from a receptacle, so a 6-foot cord can reach one. Each wall space 2 feet (600 mm) or wider is treated by itself, and floor receptacles count only if they are within 18 inches (450 mm) of the wall. At least one receptacle is also required in each laundry area, near bathroom basins (within 3 feet, or 900 mm), outside the front and back (GFCI protected), on balconies, decks and porches reached from inside, in basements, garages, long hallways (10 feet or more), larger foyers (over 60 square feet), and where HVAC equipment needs service.

Height ruleWhat the module says
Kitchen countertopReceptacles not more than 20 inches (500 mm) above the countertop (Section 210.52(C)(3)). Not face up on countertops. Where allowed, not more than 12 inches (300 mm) below the top, or where the countertop sticks out more than 6 inches (150 mm) past its support.
Other heightsThe NEC sets no general mounting height. Common practice is 15 inches (380 mm) from the floor to the bottom of the box, which homeowners in a survey preferred over the older 12 inches (300 mm) to the center. Heights are measured to the bottom of the box, so a chalk line is easy to follow.
TileAdjust the height so the receptacle sits completely in the tile or completely out of it.

Always check the working drawings, written specifications and local codes first. People who use wheelchairs may need receptacles in specific places.

    Pull and Junction Boxes

    By the end of this section your student can size a pull or junction box for a straight pull, say how an angle pull is different, and explain where these boxes go and how they are installed.

    A

    Read it together

    about 10 min

    Pull boxes and junction boxes are the bigger boxes in a wiring run. This section is about how to size them so the wires fit, and how to place them so the job is easier and safer.

    What they are

    A junction box is an enclosure where one or more raceways or cables enter, and where the conductors can be, or are, spliced. A pull box is a sheet metal box-like enclosure used in conduit runs to make pulling cable easier in long runs, to hold conduit support bushings for the weight of long riser cables, or to allow turns in multiple conduit runs.

    Sizing pull and junction boxes

    Pull boxes should be as large as possible. Workers need room for both hands, and with big wire they need room for their arms to feed it. Section 314.28 sets the minimum size. These rules apply to raceways and cables with conductors of No. 4 or larger:

    Kind of pullThe rule
    Straight pullThe box length is at least 8 times the trade size (or metric designator, MD) of the largest raceway.
    Angle or U pullThe distance from each raceway entry to the opposite wall is at least 6 times the trade size of the largest raceway in a row. Add the trade sizes of all other entries in the same row on the same wall. Work out each row separately and use the row that gives the largest distance.
    Angle or U pull, between entriesThe distance between raceway entries enclosing the same conductor is at least 6 times the trade size of the larger raceway.
    CablesWhen a cable is changed into raceway size, use the smallest raceway trade size needed for the number and size of conductors in the cable.

    The width of the box only needs to be large enough to fit the locknuts and bushings on the conduits. The depth only needs to be enough for the locknuts and bushings to go on properly.

    Worked example: straight pullThe largest conduit is trade size 4" (MD 103). Length = trade size × 8 = 4" × 8 = 32 inches (824 mm). Always use the largest conduit in the group.
    Worked example: angle pullThe largest conduit is 4" (MD 103), with 3" and 2" conduits also entering on that wall. L1 = 6 × 4" + (3" + 2") = 29 inches (749 mm). The same runs are on the next wall, so L2 is also 29 inches (749 mm). The distance between entries for the same conductor is 6 × 4" = 24 inches (618 mm). A 6-inch-deep (152 mm) box is enough.
    Another straight pullTwo raceways, 3 inches and 2 inches. Use the largest: 3" × 8 = 24 inches. The box must be at least 24 inches long.

    Installing pull and junction boxes

    • Do not make very long pulls. Pull boxes put at convenient spots take strain off the conductors. They save time and hard work, remove bends and elbows, and avoid fishing from both ends of a run.
    • Put them where people can work. A pull box set too high forces the electrician onto a ladder and leaves no room for the loop of wire or the pulling tools. Unless the drawings or the engineer say otherwise, chest height lets workers stand on the floor.
    • Junction boxes must be readily accessible. A person has to be able to reach the conductors without removing plaster, wall covering or any other part of the building.
    • Fasten them securely on walls or ceilings, or support them properly. Cover every unused opening, such as knockouts.
    • Plan the knockouts. Some factory boxes have concentric knockouts. Often a box must be custom built, or knockouts cut on the job. For large pull boxes and troughs, shop drawings should show every knockout before the box is built.
    Tips from the moduleA pull box counts as the end of a run for the 360-degree bend rule. Metal boxes must be grounded to the circuit grounding system. Label every junction cover with the circuit number, the panel it came from and where it goes. A knockout punch kit can make a hole where a box has no precut knockout.
    SafetyThe NEC is for trained people working under proper guidance. If you are not sure a box is the right size or is grounded correctly, stop and ask a qualified person.
