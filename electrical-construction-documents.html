Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning and put it in their own words.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do electrical work. Drawings and specifications are read by trained people working under proper guidance. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 8 · 1.1.4

Revision Block

By the end of this section your student can say what a revision block is, why a drawing gets revised, and what a cloud shape on a drawing is pointing to.

A

Read it together

about 6 min

Drawings change. The revision block is where a drawing keeps its own change history, so everyone on the job is working from the latest information.

What it is

A revision block is a small table on a drawing sheet that lists the changes made to that sheet after it was first issued. It is usually found near the title block. Each change gets a line in the block, so you can see what changed and when.

On the drawing itself, the changed area is often circled with a cloud shape. The cloud tells you where to look. The revision block tells you what the change is.

Key ideaA cloud on a drawing means "something here changed." Find the revision block to read the details.

Why it matters on a job

Electricians build from the drawings. If you are working from an old sheet, you can install the wrong thing in the wrong place. Checking the revision block and the cloud shapes is how you know you have the current information.

The drawings you will work from are called architectural drawings (the working drawings for building the structure) and electrical drawings (the part that shows the electrical system). People still call them blueprints, but a blueprint is really an exact copy or reproduction of an original drawing.

Parts of a drawing you will see

  • Dimensions are the sizes or measurements printed on a drawing.
  • A detail drawing is an enlarged view of one area of a drawing, shown separately so you can see it clearly.
  • An electrical drawing packs a lot of exact information into lines, symbols, dimensions and notes, so the people who install the system can read it.

    Floor Plans

    By the end of this section your student can describe a floor plan, tell it apart from a site plan and an elevation view, and explain what a match line and dimensions tell you.

    A

    Read it together

    about 8 min

    A floor plan is the drawing you will use most. It shows the building from above, with the roof and upper part taken away so you can see inside.

    Floor plan

    A floor plan is a drawing of a building as if a horizontal cut were made through it at about window level and the top part removed. What you see is what you would see looking down on the rest of the building. Walls, doors, rooms and (on electrical sheets) outlets, switches and lights are shown from above.

    A plan, in general, is any drawing made as though you were looking straight down on an object from above.

    Other views of the same building

    DrawingWhat it shows
    Site planWhere the building sits on the property. It often shows topographical lines, electrical and communication lines, water and sewer lines, sidewalks and driveways.
    Floor planThe inside layout of the building, seen from above.
    Elevation viewThe outside faces of the building: height and width, but not depth. Usually the front, rear or sides.

    If a question asks which drawing shows the face of a building, the answer is the elevation view.

    Contour lines

    Contour lines are curving lines on a site plan that follow a given elevation. When the lines are close together, the ground is steep. When they are far apart, the ground is fairly level.

    Match lines and dimensions

    Big buildings do not fit on one sheet. A match line shows where the drawing continues on another sheet. Line it up with the same match line on the next sheet. Dimension lines show the real sizes. The number you read is the actual dimension, not a measurement you take with a ruler.

    Why it mattersBefore you run a single wire you need to know where you are in the building. The floor plan and the match lines tell you.

      Sectional Views

      By the end of this section your student can explain what a sectional view shows and what a section line on a plan is telling you.

      A

      Read it together

      about 6 min

      A sectional view lets you see inside something by pretending to slice it open.

      Sectional view

      A sectional view is a cutaway drawing that shows the inside of an object or building. Think of cutting a loaf of bread to see the slices inside. The drawing shows what you would see at the cut.

      Section line

      On a plan, a section line marks where that imaginary cut was made. It shows the location of the section on the plan, so you can match the plan to the sectional view that goes with it.

      Key ideaThe section line is on the plan. The sectional view is the drawing of what you would see if the building were cut there.

      Where you will use it

      A sectional view can show things a floor plan cannot, such as how high something is mounted, or what is inside a wall or above a ceiling. If a plan leaves you wondering how a part of the building is put together, look for a section line and its matching view.

        Module Section 4 of 8 · 2.3.0

        Metric Scale

        By the end of this section your student can explain what scale means, name the three kinds of scales, and work simple scale problems.

        A

        Read it together

        about 10 min

        A drawing cannot be the size of the building, so everything is drawn smaller by a set amount. That amount is the scale.

        Scale

        On a drawing, scale is the size relationship between an object's actual size and the size it is drawn. Scale also means the measuring tool you use to read that relationship.

        Three kinds of scales

        ScaleWhat it measures
        Architect's scaleA special ruler used on architectural drawings. It measures distances in inches and fractions of an inch.
        Engineer's scaleA special ruler that measures distances in decimal units.
        Metric scaleUses a ratio such as 1:1,000. A drawing at 1:1,000 typically shows units in meters.

        On an architect's triangular scale, the ⅜" = 1'–0" and ¾" = 1'–0" scales start on opposite ends of the same edge, so you read one from the left and the other from the right.

        Worked examples

        Architect's scale. A floor plan is drawn at ⅛" = 1'–0". That means every ⅛" on the drawing is 1 foot on the building. A distance of 1⅜" is eleven eighths. Eleven eighths, at 1 foot each, is 11'–0".

        Engineer's scale. At a 1:20 scale, 1 inch on the drawing is 20 feet. So 2 inches on the drawing is 40 feet.

        Key ideaFind the scale first. Then every measurement on the sheet means something. A ratio like 1:1,000 means one unit on paper is one thousand of the same unit in real life.

          Electrical Symbols and Lines

          By the end of this section your student can explain why drawings use symbols, read common receptacle and luminaire symbols with the legend, and say what the main kinds of lines mean.

          A

          Read it together

          about 12 min

          Electrical drawings are written in symbols and lines. There is not room to write out every device, so each one gets a small picture, and the sheet comes with a legend that tells you what each picture means.

          Symbols and the legend

          The symbols are standard, but always check the legend on the drawing you are working from. Letters sometimes sit beside a symbol. For example, SL on an electrical drawing indicates a low-voltage switch.

          Many symbols are variations of one shape. For receptacle outlets, the symbol shows how many receptacles there are. You will see symbols for a single receptacle, a duplex (two), a triplex (three), a quadruplex (four), and a split-wired duplex. Light fixtures have symbols too, with different versions for surface-mounted, pendant, or pull-chain types. Your textbook has these symbols in a figure, and the practice questions below point you to the picture.

          Lines

          • Lines on a plan show branch circuit wiring.
          • A dashed line means the wiring is concealed in the floor.
          • A line with an arrowhead is a branch circuit homerun, the run that goes back to the panel.

          Shop drawings

          A shop drawing is developed by a manufacturer, fabricator or contractor. It shows specific dimensions and other details about one piece of equipment and how it is installed.

          Key ideaNever guess a symbol. Check the legend, and when it is still unclear, ask.

            Identifying Luminaires on a Lighting Floor Plan

            By the end of this section your student can explain how lights are identified on a lighting plan, read AFF, and use the 80 percent rule for a branch circuit.

            A

            Read it together

            about 10 min

            A lighting floor plan tells you which light goes where. A luminaire is the lighting fixture, and each one on the plan is labeled so you can match it to the schedule.

            Identifying luminaires

            On a lighting plan, each luminaire is shown with a symbol and usually a letter or label. That label is matched to a table or schedule that gives the details of that fixture. A schedule is a systematic way of presenting a list of equipment on a drawing in table form.

            Abbreviations

            Drawings are full of abbreviations. For example, AFF means above finished floor. It tells you how high to mount something, measured up from the finished floor.

            Power plans and branch circuits

            A commercial floor power plan shows the electrical panels. Each branch circuit runs back to a panel on its own homerun.

            The 80 percent rule

            The load on a branch circuit should not go over 80 percent of its rated capacity. To find the limit, multiply the rating by 0.8.

            Example. A residential branch circuit rated for 2,400 VA. 0.8 × 2,400 VA = 1,920 VA. The connected load should be no more than 1,920 VA.

            Why it mattersA circuit loaded right up to its limit is a hot circuit. Planning for 80 percent leaves room to stay safe.

              Module Section 7 of 8 · 3.4.2

              Block Diagrams

              By the end of this section your student can tell a block diagram, a power-riser diagram, a one-line diagram and a schematic apart, and say what each is used for.

              A

              Read it together

              about 10 min

              Not every electrical drawing is a floor plan. Some drawings leave out the building and show only how equipment is connected.

              Block diagram

              A block diagram is a single-line diagram that shows electrical equipment and the connections between the pieces. Each piece is a block, and lines show how they connect.

              Power-riser diagram

              A power-riser diagram is a single-line block diagram used to show the electric service equipment, service conductors and feeders, and subpanels. Notes on it identify the equipment, the conduit size, and the number, size and type of conductors. A panelboard schedule is usually included to list what is in each panelboard, such as fuses and circuit breakers.

              One-line diagram

              A one-line diagram (also called a single-line diagram) shows, with lines and symbols, the path of a circuit or system of circuits and the parts along the way.

              Schematic diagram

              A schematic diagram is a detailed diagram of complicated circuits, such as control circuits. It has no scale, but it shows the components and their electrical connections. On a typical motor starter schematic, the symbols T1, T2 and T3 are the motor terminals.

              Schedules

              A schedule is a table. It is the tabular format drawings use to list equipment.

              DiagramBest for
              Block / power-riserHow major equipment connects
              One-lineThe path of a circuit
              SchematicHow a control circuit works

                Module Section 8 of 8 · 4.3.0

                Changes

                By the end of this section your student can explain what written specifications are, how drawings and specifications work together, and the difference between a change order, a request for information and as-built drawings.

                A

                Read it together

                about 12 min

                Plans are not the whole story, and they are not final. Changes happen, and there are set ways to handle them.

                Written specifications

                Written specifications describe what the owner, architect and engineer require in materials and workmanship. Together with the working drawings, they form the basis of the contract. A well-written specification includes things like model and part numbers.

                Specifications are organized by MasterFormat divisions. For example, communications systems are in Division 27, integrated automation is in Division 25, and medium-voltage electrical distribution equipment is in division 261000.

                When changes come up

                DocumentWhat it is
                Request for information (RFI)A formal document from the contractor asking for clarification when errors, omissions or discrepancies are found in or between the construction documents.
                Change orderA formal document from the project manager or owner that changes the drawings, specifications or scope. It also states any change in cost.
                As-built drawingsA marked-up set of drawings, also called red-lines, made at the end of the project to show all the changes made during construction.

                If the drawings and the specifications conflict, the conflict is resolved with a change order.

                Key ideaAsk first (RFI), change it officially (change order), then record what was really built (as-built drawings).
