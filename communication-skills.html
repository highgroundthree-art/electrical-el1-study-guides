Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do any hands-on trade work. The skills here are for any worker, but job-site safety rules always come first. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 2

Communication

By the end of this section your student can describe how a message gets from sender to receiver, give examples of nonverbal communication, use active listening and paraphrasing, and follow good rules for speaking and phone use on the job.

A

Read it together

about 20 min

Some of the most important tools on a job site cannot be held in your hand. They are the ability to listen, speak, read and write. Good communication keeps people safe, keeps the job on schedule and keeps the budget under control.

The communication process

Clear communication takes two basic steps. First, a sender sends a message through a channel to a receiver. Channels include meetings, phones, two-way radios and email. Second, the receiver figures out what the message means by listening or reading carefully. If anything is unclear, the receiver gives feedback by asking for more information. This back-and-forth is called two-way communication, and it is the best way to make sure everyone understands.

So why is it hard? Because of noise, which is anything that gets in the way of a message.

Where the noise comes fromExamples
The senderUses jargon the receiver does not know, does not speak clearly, is disorganized, is not specific, or does not get to the point
The receiverIs tired, distracted or not paying attention, or has poor listening or reading skills
The surroundings and equipmentLoud noise on the site, or static on a phone or radio
Why it mattersMany accidents come from not listening to or not understanding instructions. One OSHA study found that 39 percent of crane operator deaths over ten years were caused by electrocution from contact with power lines. No one can say how many could have been prevented by hearing and following instructions, but it may have been all of them.

Nonverbal communication

Words are verbal communication. Nonverbal communication is everything else. It is hard to ask for a ratchet set with no words, so nonverbal is poor at complex ideas and instructions. But it is very powerful for showing feelings and attitudes. If a doctor yawns and checks his phone while you describe your symptoms, you will not trust his diagnosis, even if he writes a prescription.

CategoryWhat it says about you
GroomingA neat appearance shows self-discipline and awareness
DressDress neatly and appropriately for the work. Casual does not mean messy. Uniforms should be clean.
Personal environmentA cluttered workspace or work vehicle suggests a sloppy work ethic
Use of timeArriving on time shows respect. Ending meetings on time shows humility.
Facial expressionsEye contact shows interest and confidence. An expressionless face can show boredom.
Physical distanceComfortable distance varies by culture. A good rule is to let the more powerful person choose the distance.

When it comes to safety and accuracy, what you say matters more than how you say it. But your nonverbal habits still affect how your words are received.

Listening and speaking on the job

Teaching and learning on the job happen through speaking and listening: giving and taking instructions, listening to presentations, joining team discussions, talking with co-workers and supervisors, and talking with clients. At this stage in your career you will probably do more listening than speaking. People learn by listening, and there is a lot to learn.

Active listening

Hearing happens by itself. Active listening is the process of respecting others, listening to what is said, and understanding it. You have to pay attention and be involved. Here is how.

  • Understand what poor listening can cost: wasted time and money, and even injuries.
  • Do not let yourself get distracted. Jot a word on a notepad if a thought pulls you away, then put it out of your mind.
  • Use body language: eye contact, nodding, and a good posture show you are listening, and they help you listen better too.
  • Do not interrupt or criticize. Wait until the person finishes, then ask questions, especially if the instructions seem incomplete.
  • Take notes, especially when the content is technical, with numbers, times, amounts or temperatures.
  • End by paraphrasing: say back what you heard in your own words, not an exact repeat, so the speaker can confirm you got it right.
Worked exampleThe supervisor says, “Recalibrate the machines according to the specifications on this paper.” You paraphrase: “So you want me to recalibrate these three machines in this building according to the chart posted here.” He answers, “No, I mean the machines in the building next door, and use the chart on the back of this paper.” One short summary saved hours of wasted work.

There are also barriers to listening, and each one has a fix.

BarrierWhat to do
EmotionCount to 10, step away, get a drink of water, and come back when you are calm
BoredomForce yourself to stay focused. The speaker may have information you need.
DistractionsAsk the speaker to move away from the noise. If a personal problem is distracting you, concentrate harder or tell your supervisor.
Your egoNotice if you finish other people's sentences, interrupt, or plan what you will say next. Tone it down.

Speaking on the job

Speaking skills are not just about giving speeches. They are about communicating clearly with others every day, one on one. Effective listening depends on effective speaking, because nobody can understand what was not made clear.

Worked example“Hand me that tool there” is vague. “Hand me the grinder on that bench” is better. “Hand me the 4-inch angle grinder that’s on the bench behind you” is best, because the listener knows which tool and where it is, and no time is wasted asking.
  • Think before you speak, but not at the expense of listening.
  • Organize your ideas. For a longer talk: say what you will tell them, tell them, then say what you told them.
  • Pick the right place and time. To give detailed assembly instructions, choose a quiet place when people are fresh, not just before lunch or the end of the shift.
  • Speak clearly, keep eye contact, and do not over-explain to people who already know the topic.
  • Explain any jargon, which means specialized words used in an industry.
  • Do not talk on the phone, text or listen to music while talking with the work crew.
  • Give people time to ask questions, and make sure they understood.

When there is conflict, deal with it quickly, set a time to talk, stay civil, ask questions first, avoid saying “never” or “always,” and focus on specifics.

Telephones on the job

A cell phone can be a useful tool, but it can distract you. Never make or receive personal calls while working, and wait for a break. Never use a phone where it is a safety hazard, such as while running machinery or driving. Many workplaces ban cell phones because of accidents, wasted time and the risk of workers photographing secret equipment or data, which is theft of intellectual property.

When youDo this
Place a callStart by identifying yourself and asking who you are speaking to. Speak clearly and explain why you are calling. Take notes.
Leave a messageKeep it brief, prepare it first, and leave a number and the best time to reach you.
Receive a callDo not just say “Hello.” Give your name and the company name. Do not leave people on hold for long. Transfer calls courteously. Keep calls brief.
The call is personalContinue working and do not answer. Return it later.
SafetyFollow your employer's policy on phones. If phones are not allowed, leave yours where you are told. Never use a phone while operating a machine, a power tool or a vehicle.

    Reading and Writing

    By the end of this section your student can find information in a technical book, read on the job with a purpose, follow the writing process, and follow good rules for business email and texting.

    A

    Read it together

    about 22 min

    Almost every job in construction runs on written words. Reading the instructions correctly and writing clearly are skills that keep jobs on schedule, protect people and save money.

    Why reading and writing matter

    The construction industry depends on written documents of all kinds, from office paperwork to drawings and building codes. Written documents let workers follow instructions accurately, help managers keep work on schedule and help the company meet its legal obligations. You need to read and understand the documents that apply to your work, and as you gain experience you will be asked to write some of them too.

    Common examples include textbooks (which teach), codebooks (which give codes and standards, such as electrical and building codes) and the instruction and installation manuals that come with tools and equipment. When a person has poor reading skills, they tend to avoid reading technical information, and that can lead to errors and accidents.

    What a construction worker reads

    • Safety instructions and construction drawings
    • Manufacturer's installation instructions
    • Materials lists
    • Signs and labels
    • Work orders and schedules
    • Permits, which are legal documents that allow a task to be done
    • Specifications
    • Change orders, which are written orders from the owner for the contractor to change time, amount or specifications
    • Industry magazines, company newsletters and emails

    Tools for finding information in a book

    FeatureWhat it isUse it when
    Table of contentsA list of chapters or sections, usually at the frontYou want an overview or to find a chapter on a topic
    IndexAn alphabetical list of topics with page numbers, usually at the backYou want every page that mentions a topic, even briefly
    GlossaryAn alphabetical list of terms and their definitionsYou need to know what a word means
    AppendixesMore detailed or specific information placed at the end of a section, chapter or bookYou need extra detail that would slow down the main text
    Tables and graphsTables present text and numbers, and graphs show information as a picture or chart, so facts can be understood at a glanceYou want key facts and figures quickly

    Reading well on the job

    • Have a purpose. If you want one installation procedure, find that section and go straight to it. Do not read the whole book.
    • Read slowly enough to concentrate. Technical writing is packed with details, and rushing leads to mistakes.
    • Avoid distractions such as radios, phones, conversations and machines.
    • Take notes or highlight, and write a note and page number on the blank pages at the front of a book.
    • Skim the chapter titles and section headings first when the book is new to you, and look for visual clues such as bold or italics.
    • Read all the steps before you start, and picture yourself doing the task.
    • Reread complex material, and take a break when you are tired or frustrated.

    Text styles are clues. ALL CAPITALS look like shouting, so use them sparingly. Bold makes important words stand out. Italics are used for names of books, magazines, ships and other vehicles. Underlining has almost the same uses as italics, so pick one and be consistent. A font is the type style of letters and numbers. Serif fonts have small lines on the ends of strokes and are easy to read in books and printed documents. Sans serif fonts have no such lines.

    Writing on the job

    You will probably read more than you write at this stage, but you will have to write eventually. Construction workers write work orders, health and safety reports, punch lists (lists of deficiencies that need correction at completion), memos (informal office notes), emails, change orders and more. Good writing helps you move into jobs with more responsibility.

    StepWhat to do
    PrewriteBrainstorm a list of ideas, cross out the weak ones and put the rest in a logical order as an outline
    ResearchLook up what you need so your statements are appropriate and accurate
    Write a rough draftIt is meant to be rough. Do not try for a masterpiece.
    Rest, review and reviseStep away, then come back, reread and improve it
    ProofreadCheck spelling and grammar. A spell-checker will not catch a wrong word like “four” for “for.” Ask a co-worker to read it too, and try proofreading on paper.

    Good writing has five characteristics.

    BeWhat it means
    ClearThe main point is easy to find and the details are all there. Lists with bullets or numbers help.
    ConciseFewer words are better. “To avoid being shocked, disconnect the main power supply before starting” beats the long version.
    CorrectAccuracy matters. Use the right terms and quantities. Errors cost time and money.
    CompleteSay who you are, why you are writing, and what, when and where the reader should do it, and whom to call with questions
    ConsiderateThink about the reader and their reading level

    The journalist's questions help you cover everything: who, what, when, where, why and how.

    Emails

    Email is the standard way to send written information and files. It is fast, it can be stored permanently, it can be copied and sent to many people at once, and it saves paper. The downside is that emails are not as private as paper documents. They are easy to forward or send to the wrong person by accident, so never put private, sensitive or confidential information in a business email. An email can also be stored for good and can count as a binding agreement.

    • Treat a business email like a formal business letter or memo.
    • Send it to the correct people and start with a clear subject line.
    • Address the recipient and keep the body brief, about one screen, with bullets or numbers if it helps.
    • Write in a positive tone, avoid sarcasm and never type in all capital letters.
    • Name the file and its format when you send an attachment, and give another way to reach you.
    • Check spelling and grammar before sending, because a sent email cannot be retrieved.
    • Never send bad news by email, and do not use email to dodge a face-to-face talk.

    Written documents with handwritten signatures are still the standard for contracts, but electronic signatures, which capture a handwritten signature with computer technology and attach it to a file, are changing that.

    Texting

    Texts are quick and likely to be seen right away. Good uses are extremely brief messages that should be seen immediately and confirming information after a call or meeting. Do not use texts for long or complex information, or for highly emotional messages, apologies or criticism. Speaking is much faster than typing, so call if the conversation will be long. Proofread before you send, because autocorrect can change your meaning.

    SafetySafety always comes before a text message. Never send texts while driving or operating machinery, and follow your company's policy on phones and tablets on the job.
