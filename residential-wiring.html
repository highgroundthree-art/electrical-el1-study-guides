Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning and put it in their own words.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do electrical work. Drawings and specifications are read by trained people working under proper guidance. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 13 · 1.1.1

General Lighting Loads

By the end of this section your student can explain what the general lighting load is, calculate it from floor area, and turn volt-amperes into amps.

A

Read it together

about 8 min

Before anyone can size the electrical service for a house, they have to know how much load the house will put on it. The first piece is lighting.

General lighting load

For a home, the code figures the general lighting load from the size of the living space. It uses 3 volt-amperes (VA) for every square foot. Multiply the floor area by 3.

Example. A small house has 900 square feet. 3 VA × 900 ft² = 2,700 VA.

From VA to amps

To find the amps, divide the load by the voltage. Lighting circuits are 120 volts.

Example. 2,700 VA ÷ 120 V = 22.5 A. And a 3,600 VA load ÷ 120 V = 30 A.

Key ideaArea × 3 VA gives the lighting load. Load ÷ volts gives the amps.

    Small Appliance Loads

    By the end of this section your student can say how many small appliance circuits a kitchen needs, and how the laundry load is counted.

    A

    Read it together

    about 7 min

    Kitchens and laundry areas plug in the heavy hitters, so the code sets aside circuits just for them.

    Appliances

    Appliances are equipment designed for a particular purpose, such as making heat, light, or motion with electricity. They are usually self-contained and are normally made in standard sizes or types.

    Small appliance circuits

    A kitchen area needs at least two small appliance branch circuits, each counted at 1,500 VA. If a dwelling has two kitchen areas, it needs four of these circuits, two for each area.

    Laundry load

    When sizing a home's electrical service, the laundry load is counted as 1,500 VA.

    Key ideaTwo circuits at 1,500 VA for each kitchen area. Laundry is 1,500 VA.

      Lighting Demand Factors

      By the end of this section your student can explain what a demand factor is, and use the examples the module gives.

      A

      Read it together

      about 7 min

      Not every light and appliance in a house runs at the same time. A demand factor is how the code allows for that when sizing a service.

      Demand factors

      A demand factor is a percentage you apply to a load to account for the fact that not everything is on at once. The code uses tables for this.

      • For the general lighting load, the first 3,000 VA is counted at 100%. The rest is counted at lower percentages from the table.
      • Under NEC Section 220.53, a demand factor of 75% may be applied to four or more fixed appliance loads.
      • Under NEC Table 220.54, a household with two dryers has a demand factor of 100%.
      Key ideaA demand factor lowers the load you count, but only where the code table says it can.

        Module Section 4 of 13 · 2.2.0

        Main Bonding Jumper

        By the end of this section your student can explain grounding and bonding basics: the ground ring, the main bonding jumper, and when metal parts must be grounded.

        A

        Read it together

        about 9 min

        Grounding and bonding give fault current a safe path. This section covers a few of the specifics.

        Bonding

        A bonding jumper is a bare or green insulated conductor used to make sure metal parts that must be connected electrically are connected. A bonding bushing is a special conduit bushing with a terminal for a bonding jumper. It can bite into the enclosure wall to bond the conduit when no concentric knockouts are left.

        Main bonding jumper

        When a screw is used as the main bonding jumper, it must be colored green.

        Other grounding facts

        • A ground ring used as a grounding electrode must be at least 20 feet long.
        • Exposed noncurrent-carrying metal parts of fixed equipment must be grounded if the equipment operates with any terminal over 150 volts to ground.
        • Information on equipment grounding for computers is in NEC Section 250.114.
        Key ideaGreen means grounding and bonding. Know where the book says to look.

          Panelboard Locations

          By the end of this section your student can name the parts of a residential service, and say what the module tells you about heights, clearances and where service conductors count as inside the building.

          A

          Read it together

          about 14 min

          Power comes from the utility to the house through a chain of parts. Knowing the names is half the battle.

          The parts of a service

          TermWhat it is
          Service dropThe overhead conductors from the last power company pole to where they connect at the building.
          Service lateralThe underground conductors from the power company's facilities to the building.
          Service entranceThe point where power is supplied to the building, including the main switch or panelboard, meters, protective devices and conductors.
          Service-entrance conductorsThe conductors from the drop or lateral to the main disconnect in the building.
          Service-entrance equipmentEquipment that protects the feeder and service conductors, disconnects them, and measures the energy used.
          Load centerA panelboard normally at the service entrance of a home. It sometimes contains the main disconnect.

          Roughing in is the first stage of an installation: raceway, cable, boxes and other equipment go in before any finish work.

          Numbers from the module

          • The minimum service disconnect rating for a single home is 100 A.
          • Service conductors over residential property and driveways must be at least 12 feet above the ground.
          • Service drop conductors must be at least 10 feet above the ground or other accessible surface.
          • A service conductor without an overall jacket must be at least 3 feet from a porch or balcony.
          • Raceway fittings used with service masts must be identified for that use (NEC Section 230.28).

          Inside or outside?

          Under NEC Section 230.6, conductors installed under at least 2 inches of concrete are considered outside the building. Under only 1 inch of slab, they are considered inside, and a disconnect is required at the meter base.

          Why it mattersHeights and clearances protect people, so these numbers are worth knowing.

            Service-Entrance Cable

            By the end of this section your student can tell NM, MC, SE and SER cable apart, and say where each can and cannot be used.

            A

            Read it together

            about 12 min

            Houses are wired with several kinds of cable. Each has its own rules.

            Nonmetallic-sheathed cable (Type NM and NMC)

            A factory assembly of two or more insulated conductors in an overall nonmetallic jacket. Type NMC has a corrosion-resistant jacket. People often call it Romex®, which is really a trade name for Type NM. In three-wire NM cable the conductors are black, white and red, with a bare or green grounding conductor. Type NM is prohibited in shallow chases of masonry, concrete or adobe.

            Metal-clad cable (Type MC)

            A factory assembly of insulated conductors in an armor of interlocking metal tape or a metallic sheath. Type MC is prohibited where subject to physical damage.

            Service-entrance cable (Types SE and SER)

            For interior wiring, Type SE cable is available with an insulated grounded conductor. Type SER cable may be used as a subfeed under certain conditions.

            Key ideaCheck the type, then check the rules for where it can go.

              Raceways

              By the end of this section your student can say what raceways are for in a house and where conductors are most likely to be run in conduit.

              A

              Read it together

              about 5 min

              Conductors can be protected by running them inside a raceway such as conduit.

              Where raceways show up in a home

              Most finished rooms, like bedrooms, are wired with cable inside the walls. Conductors are most likely to be run in conduit in places like unfinished basements, where wiring is exposed and could be damaged.

              During roughing in, the raceway, boxes and other equipment go in first, before any finishing work.

              Key ideaExposed wiring needs more protection, and conduit provides it.

                Branch Circuits and Feeders

                By the end of this section your student can tell a branch circuit from a feeder and use the 80 percent rule.

                A

                Read it together

                about 8 min

                Power flows from the service to feeders, and from feeders to branch circuits. The names tell you where you are.

                Definitions

                A feeder is any circuit conductor between the service equipment (or other power source) and the final branch circuit overcurrent device. A branch circuit is the part of the wiring system beyond that final overcurrent device.

                The 80 percent rule

                A branch circuit should be loaded to no more than 80% of its capacity.

                Example. A lighting circuit with a capacity of 1,800 VA: 0.8 × 1,800 VA = 1,440 VA maximum load.

                Key ideaFeeders go to the panel. Branch circuits go beyond it. Keep branch circuits at 80 percent or less.

                  240-Volt Circuits

                  By the end of this section your student can explain how a water heater circuit is sized and what 240-volt circuits are used for.

                  A

                  Read it together

                  about 7 min

                  Large loads like water heaters often run on 240 volts. The circuit has to be sized with some room to spare.

                  Water heaters

                  The branch circuit for a storage-type water heater of 120 gallons or less must be rated at least 125% of the nameplate rating. The conductors are sized the same way.

                  Example. A water heater nameplate says 20 A. 20 A × 1.25 = 25 A.

                  Key ideaTake the nameplate amps and multiply by 1.25.

                      Module Section 11 of 13 · 5.3.2

                      Ceiling Fan Boxes

                      By the end of this section your student can explain how boxes are filled and counted, and why a ceiling fan needs the right box.

                      A

                      Read it together

                      about 9 min

                      A box can only hold so many conductors. The code counts each item inside.

                      Box fill

                      Each conductor counts, and so do certain other items. A common set of counts: each insulated conductor counts as one, all the grounding wires together count as one, cable clamps together count as one, and each switch counts as two. A looped conductor over 12 inches counts as two.

                      Example. A metallic outlet box with two No. 12 conductors (2), a ground wire (1), a cable clamp (1), a switch (2) and a looped conductor over 12 inches (2) counts as 8 conductors.

                      Ceiling fans

                      A ceiling fan hangs from the box, so the box must be one made to carry that weight and movement. A plain lighting box is not enough.

                      Key ideaCount everything in the box. For a ceiling fan, use a box made for fans.

                        Receptacles

                        By the end of this section your student can read common receptacle symbols, say which screw is which, and know the rules for GFCI protection near pools and hot tubs.

                        A

                        Read it together

                        about 14 min

                        Receptacles are the outlets people use every day, and they come in many types and symbols.

                        Wiring a receptacle

                        The brass screw connects to the ungrounded (hot) conductor. The silver screw is for the neutral, and the green screw is for the grounding conductor.

                        Receptacle symbols

                        On drawings, receptacles are shown with symbols: single, duplex, split-wired duplex, range receptacle (50 A), dryer receptacle (30 A) and special-purpose outlet. The practice questions below point you to the figures in the textbook.

                        GFCI and wet areas

                        • A single-pole GFCI breaker is rated at 120 VAC.
                        • Around a hot tub, GFCI protection is required for all receptacles within 10 feet of the inside wall of the tub.
                        • In a residential pool area, receptacles must be at least 6 feet from the inside walls of the pool.
                        Key ideaBrass is hot. Water means GFCI. Check the symbol, check the figure.

                          Switch Locations

                          By the end of this section your student can say what a switch and a switch leg are, how four-way switching works, and when stairs need switches at each level.

                          A

                          Read it together

                          about 9 min

                          Switches control lights, and where they go matters.

                          Switch and switch leg

                          A switch is a mechanical device for turning an electrical circuit on and off. A switch leg is a circuit routed to a switch box to control electric lights.

                          Three-way and four-way

                          To control a light from more than two places, the correct operation of a four-way switch requires at least two three-way switches, one at each end.

                          Stairways

                          A wall switch is required to control stairway lighting at each level when the levels are separated by six or more risers.

                          Key ideaTwo three-ways plus one or more four-ways. Switches at each level of stairs with six or more risers.
