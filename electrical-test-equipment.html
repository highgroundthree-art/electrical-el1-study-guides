Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do electrical work. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. Test equipment is used on circuits that can hurt or kill. Only qualified people, following the safety rules, use meters on energized systems. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 2

Identifying Meters

By the end of this section your student can name the main kinds of electrical test equipment, say what each one measures, and explain how to use the common ones safely.

A

Read it together

about 25 min

Electricians use meters to find out what is going on inside a circuit, since you cannot see electricity. Which meter you pick depends on what you are measuring and how accurate the reading needs to be. This section walks through the meters electricians reach for most.

What meters are for (1.0.0)

Test instruments and meters have two main jobs: troubleshooting circuits and equipment, and verifying that circuits and parts work the way they should.

The oldest idea behind meters goes back to 1882, when a Frenchman named Arsene d'Arsonval invented the galvanometer. His design used a stationary permanent magnet and a coil that moves. The d'Arsonval meter movement is still the basis for analog (needle) meters.

Here is how a moving-coil meter works. A coil of very fine wire is wound on a light aluminum frame that can pivot freely between the poles of a permanent magnet. When current flows through the coil, it becomes magnetized and is pushed by the magnet's field. The coil turns against a spring, and a pointer attached to the frame shows how far it moved on a calibrated scale. The more current, the farther it turns.

PartJob
Multiplier resistorsExtend the range of the movement for voltage readings
Shunt resistorsExtend the range of the movement for current readings

Most meters today are digital. They use solid-state parts, have no moving parts, and are easier to read. Analog meters still have a place: testing certain unique systems, working below 50V, checking transformer polarity, and any job that needs a fast response. A digital meter gives one instant value, but a moving needle shows you how the reading is changing, which gives a fuller picture.

Voltmeter (1.1.0)

A voltmeter measures voltage, which is also called potential difference or electromotive force (emf). It is connected in parallel with the circuit or part being measured. An analog voltmeter uses the d'Arsonval movement with internal switched resistors for the different ranges. A digital voltmeter uses an analog-to-digital converter chip to turn what it senses into a number or graphic.

Many digital meters are autoranging, which means the meter finds the right scale on its own. If your meter is not autoranging, start on the highest voltage range and work down until the reading sits between half and three-quarter scale. That gives a more accurate reading and protects the meter.

On many meters, DC is shown as a straight line with three dashes under it, and AC is shown as a sine wave.

Phantom readingsA digital meter is so sensitive that it can show a small "ghost" reading caused by induction from the electric field around nearby energized conductors. The voltage is not really there.
SafetyMeasuring more than 50V can expose you to life-threatening hazards. Follow NFPA 70E, OSHA rules and your employer's policies.

Voltage tester (1.1.1)

A voltmeter gives an exact number. Often an electrician only needs to know whether voltage is present and roughly how much: is it 120V, 240V or 480V? For that, a voltage tester is quicker. The range and type of current (AC, DC or both) it can read are printed on its scale. Some advanced testers add a digital readout, a GFCI test, and a switch between contact and noncontact detection.

Check a voltage tester before every use:

  • Inspect the insulation on the leads for cracks or frays. Faulty leads are a hazard and must be replaced.
  • Touch the probes to a known energized source. The tester should show the source's voltage. No reading, or the wrong reading, means it must be repaired or replaced.

Do not leave the probes on a source longer than needed, because some testers are built for quick readings and can be damaged. Electricians use a voltage tester to confirm power is there when it should be, and to confirm power is off when it should be.

A simple noncontact (proximity) voltage detector glows when it senses voltage in its rated range (the example in the book works from 50V to 1,000V). It does not tell you which value, but it is handy for scanning junction boxes and termination cabinets, and even for tracing circuits through walls.

Live-dead-live testWhen you check that a circuit is de-energized for lockout:
  1. Live: test your meter on a known energized source to prove it works.
  2. Dead: de-energize and test the target circuit to confirm it reads dead.
  3. Live: test the known energized source again before touching the circuit.
This is an OSHA requirement for systems above 600V, and it is good practice at every voltage.

Proving unit (1.1.2)

A proving unit is a safe electronic voltage source used to confirm a meter works before you use it on a real circuit. Connect the meter's leads to the unit's positive and negative terminals and check that the meter shows the expected value. The unit shown in the book supplies a 240V AC or DC test point. Proving units also serve as the safe known "live" source in a live-dead-live test.

Ohmmeter (1.2.0)

An ohmmeter measures resistance. It can also find open circuits and shorted circuits. Inside, it has a DC current meter movement, a low-voltage DC source (usually a battery) and current-limiting resistors, all in series.

  • Zero it first. Touch the leads together to cancel the leads' own resistance. On analog meters, turn the zero-adjust knob until the needle reads zero ohms, and repeat it every time you change range.
  • Autoranging digital ohmmeters pick the scale and show ohms, K-ohms or M-ohms. Analog ones make you choose.
  • Continuity test. Most digital meters beep when the resistance is very low or zero. That means a closed (complete) circuit, which is why the meter doubles as a continuity tester.
  • You can test a part without fully removing it. Often disconnecting just one lead isolates it, though that can be slow.
SafetyBefore using an ohmmeter, use a voltmeter to confirm both sides of the circuit are de-energized. Voltage on the circuit can push current through the meter, damaging the meter and the circuit and injuring you.

Try this: pinch a resistor between the leads with your fingers and watch the reading change. Your body adds a second path (it is in parallel), so the meter reads lower than the true value. A resistor held in clips reads correctly.

Clamp-on ammeter (1.3.0)

A clamp-on ammeter (clamp meter) measures current without touching any bare wire. It works by sensing the electromagnetic field around the conductor, using simple transformer action: the conductor being measured acts as the primary, and the meter's jaws act as the secondary. With a 1,000-to-1 ratio, 1 amp in the conductor puts 0.001 amp (1 mA) into the meter.

  • Open the jaws, close them around the conductor, make sure the jaws are clean and fully closed, then read the display.
  • Hold freezes the reading so you can remove the meter from a tight spot and read it later.
  • Min/Max (Peak) catches the highest value, such as the inrush when a motor starts.
  • Pick a range at least as high as the current. A digital meter shows OL (overload) when the current is too high. An analog needle will peg past the scale, which can damage it.
Think about itWhat happens if you loop the conductor so the jaws surround two turns? The meter counts the same current twice, so the reading is doubled.
SafetyA clamp meter can put you near energized equipment. Use it only if you are qualified and following NFPA, OSHA and your employer's procedures.

Multimeter (1.4.0)

The multimeter, also called a volt-ohm-milliammeter (VOM), combines the three meters above in one instrument. On an analog VOM you choose the voltage type (DC or AC), the function (volts, amps or ohms) and the range. On a digital VOM you choose the voltage type, and most autorange for size. Using a VOM is the same as using the separate voltmeter, ohmmeter and ammeter. Before use, check it on a known source such as a proving unit, and always follow the maker's instructions.

  • Current clamps plug into most multimeters to read AC and DC current above the milliamp level. They come in ranges from 50A to several thousand amps, with round, rectangular or flexible jaws.
  • Some multimeters read frequency, which helps diagnose harmonic problems.
  • Min/Max memory records the lowest and highest readings over a set time.
  • Other features: capacitance, diode and transistor tests, temperature, and true RMS for accurate readings at different frequencies.
  • Clamp-on multimeters have both a clamp and test leads, so they read current, voltage, resistance and more.

Specialty meters (1.5.0)

Beyond the common meters, there are special ones for special jobs: megohmmeters, motor and phase rotation testers, circuit testers, irradiance meters, recording instruments and remote readout meters.

Megohmmeter (1.5.1)

A regular ohmmeter cannot read resistances of millions of ohms, such as the insulation on conductors or between the windings of a motor or transformer. For that you use a megohmmeter, also called a Megger or insulation resistance tester. Some run on AC, some on a battery, and some are hand-cranked. The reading should fall within the range in the manufacturer's specifications.

Megohmmeter safety
  • It makes high voltage. On a 600V class system, typical test voltages are 500V and 1,000V. Only qualified people may use it, with the right PPE.
  • De-energize and verify before connecting, and make sure capacitors are discharged.
  • Disconnect the item under test from the rest of the circuit if possible.
  • Do not exceed the maker's recommended test voltage. It can depend on the age of the cable or equipment.
  • Never touch the leads while the meter is powered.
  • After the test, discharge the circuit by grounding the conductor or equipment for as long as the test lasted.
  • If exposed parts are far from where you are testing, secure or barricade them so no one touches the test voltage.
  • When testing switchgear, disconnect all electronics first, since the test voltage can damage them.

Motor and phase rotation testers (1.5.2)

Before wiring a three-phase motor, the motor's leads (T1, T2, T3) have to be matched to the circuit's phases (L1, L2, L3) so the motor turns the right way. Wrong connections can damage the motor and the machine it drives.

  • A motor rotation tester identifies the motor's legs. Connect the three motor wires to the tester's T1, T2 and T3 leads, turn the shaft about a half turn while pressing Test, and see which LED lights (clockwise or counterclockwise). If the direction is wrong, swap a pair of leads and retest. Then tag the wires.
  • A phase rotation tester (phase sequence indicator) identifies the phase sequence of the circuit. De-energize and lock out, connect the three leads, safely energize and read the LEDs, and note the lead colors with the sequence shown. De-energize and lock out again before disconnecting.
SafetyNever connect a motor rotation tester to energized equipment. Phase rotation testers are for qualified people only, with PPE. Putting phase tape on the tester's leads that matches the circuit's color code helps prevent mix-ups. Noncontact phase rotation testers also exist.

Circuit tester (1.5.3)

A circuit tester plugs into a grounded outlet. Its lights show the circuit's condition: open ground, open neutral, open hot, reversed polarity or a good circuit. GFCI circuit testers are also available.

Irradiance meter (1.5.4)

An irradiance meter is used when installing and testing solar PV systems. It measures solar radiation energy on a surface, usually in watts per square meter. Some also measure roof pitch and orientation, air and module temperature, and log data wirelessly.

Recording instruments (1.5.5)

A recording instrument keeps a permanent record of a measurement over time, on paper strip or in electronic memory. The electronic kind are called data loggers. They record things like voltage, current, power, resistance and frequency, and some non-electrical values such as temperature. Set them to take readings at chosen intervals, then review later to find peak loads and voltage swings. Some upload to a PC for live graphs.

Remote readout meters (1.5.6)

Some newer meters have a removable screen so you can read them from a safe distance. Others pair with a smartphone app by Bluetooth, which is handy for sharing readings with engineers or maintenance staff who are somewhere else.

    Category Ratings and Safety Requirements

    By the end of this section your student can pick a meter with the right category rating for a job and name the main safety hazards of using electrical test equipment.

    A

    Read it together

    about 20 min

    A meter is only safe if it is the right meter for the job. Picking one means checking its category rating, and using it means following the safety rules every time. This section covers both.

    Why meters have ratings (2.0.0)

    Picking the correct meter, and using it safely and by the manufacturer's instructions, prevents injury and equipment damage. One of the most important things to check is the meter's category rating.

    Meter category ratings (2.1.0)

    Short bursts of extra voltage, called transients or power spikes, are a serious hazard. Systems and loads are getting more complex, which raises the risk. Lightning strikes on outdoor transmission lines and switching surges from normal switching can make dangerous high-energy transients. Motors, capacitors, variable speed drives and power conversion equipment can make spikes too.

    To protect electricians, test equipment has safety built in. The International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) wrote a safety standard for test equipment (IEC 1010), which was adapted in the US as UL 3111-1. It defines four overvoltage installation categories: CAT I, CAT II, CAT III and CAT IV. The higher the number, the greater the risk, because those places have more power available and higher-energy transients.

    Overvoltage installation categories (Table 1)
    CategoryWhere you find it
    CAT IElectronic equipment and circuitry (lowest risk)
    CAT IISingle-phase loads such as small appliances and tools, and outlets more than 30 feet from a CAT III source or 60 feet from a CAT IV source
    CAT IIIThree-phase motors, single-phase commercial and industrial lighting, switchgear, and busduct and feeders in industrial plants
    CAT IVThree-phase power at the meter, service entrance or utility connection, and any outdoor conductors (signs, pumps, solar or wind generation) (highest risk)

    Selecting a meter (2.1.1)

    The closer you get to the utility power source, the greater the danger from transients. The more short-circuit current available, the greater the risk and the higher the category.

    • One installation can have areas with different ratings. Always pick a meter rated for the highest category you will work in.
    • Then match the voltage level, amperage and frequency to the job.
    • Make sure the test leads are rated as high as the meter.
    • Choose meters independently tested and certified by UL, CSA or another recognized lab. The category rating is marked on the meter's housing.
    SafetyUse a meter with the right category rating for where you are working. Before each use, check the test leads for wear, cracks in the insulation or other damage.

    Meter calibration (2.1.2)

    All meters must be calibrated following the manufacturer's instructions.

    • The calibrating standard must be more accurate than the instrument being tested, typically ten times more accurate.
    • ANSI says field and laboratory instruments must be calibrated at least once a year.
    • A dated calibration label must be visible on all test equipment, and written records of the dates and results must be kept.

    Electrical test equipment safety hazards (2.2.0)

    Safety is the first responsibility of everyone on a job site. Safe installation, maintenance and operation take strict attention to local and national codes, safety standards and company policy. Carelessness can cause serious injury or death from shock, burns, falls and flying objects. After an accident, investigators almost always find that simple precautions would have prevented it. It is your personal responsibility to spot and fix unsafe conditions and unsafe acts.

    BackfeedOpening the main supply switch does not necessarily de-energize every circuit in a piece of equipment. A source people often miss is backfeed, power coming in from another source, such as a generator during an outage or a solar PV system. Rescuing someone shocked by backfeed is slowed by the time it takes to find the source and shut it off. Turn off all power inputs, lock out and tag out, then check with a working voltage tester that the equipment is safe.
    Other energyLockout/tagout also covers other forms of energy that may be present, such as water pressure, steam, springs and gravity.

    Always follow these precautions:

    • The common 120V supply is not "low and harmless." It has caused more deaths than any other voltage.
    • Inspect all test equipment before each use. Look for broken leads or knobs, damaged plugs and frayed cords. Do not use equipment that is wet or damaged.
    • Make sure the rating of leads and accessories meets or exceeds the meter's rating.
    • Do not work on energized equipment unless you are qualified and approved by your supervisor.
    • Never shortcut safety. Follow all energized work policies and procedures.
    • When testing circuits, test at higher ranges first, then work down to lower ranges.
    • Always have a standby person during hot work, who knows whom to call in an emergency and how to disconnect the power.

    Putting it all together

    The book asks which instrument fits each job. The jobs and the sort of meter that fits:

    • Find a short circuit in house wiring: an ohmmeter (de-energized, checking continuity).
    • Measure the secondary voltage of an AC transformer: a voltmeter.
    • Find a blown fuse: an ohmmeter continuity check.
    • Identify the contact layout of a three-way switch or multi-pole relay: an ohmmeter continuity check.
    Hands-on tasks (with your instructor)Your student will measure voltage in the classroom from line to neutral and from neutral to ground, and use an ohmmeter to measure the value of different resistors. These are done only under the instructor's supervision.
