Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do electrical work. Meters and live circuits are for trained, qualified people only. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. Module Section 1 of 3

Atomic Structure and Electricity

By the end of this section your student can name the parts of an atom, explain why conductors and insulators behave differently, say how magnetism and electricity work together, and describe how power travels from a plant to a home.

A

Read it together

about 10 min

Every light, motor and outlet depends on one idea: tiny charged particles that either push apart or pull together. Start with the atom, and the rest of electricity starts to make sense.

What a circuit needs

An electrical circuit has at least three things: a voltage source (such as a battery), a load (the part that uses the energy, such as a light bulb), and conductors (wires) to carry the current. Most circuits also have a switch to start and stop the current.

Electricity is cause and effect. Voltage, measured in volts, is like electrical pressure. When there is voltage across a closed circuit, current flows. Current is measured in amperes, also called amps. How much current flows also depends on resistance, measured in ohms, which is how hard the load makes it for current to move. The amount of energy a device uses is called power, and it is measured in watts.

The atom

The textbook explains the atom using the simplest one, hydrogen. It has a nucleus with one proton in the middle and one electron circling it. Two forces balance each other to keep the electron in its path: one tends to throw it outward as it circles, and the other, the electrostatic force, pulls it in toward the nucleus.

An atom is made of three kinds of particles.

ParticleChargeWhere it is
ProtonPositive (+)In the nucleus
NeutronNoneIn the nucleus
ElectronNegative (−)Orbiting the nucleus

The number of protons in the nucleus decides which element an atom is. An electron's negative charge is equal to a proton's positive charge. When an atom becomes positively or negatively charged, the charged particle is called an ion.

Key ideaLike charges repel and unlike charges attract. A proton repels another proton. An electron repels another electron. A proton attracts an electron. This is the Law of Electrical Charges.

Conductors and insulators

Electrons live in groups of orbits called shells. The outer shell is the valence shell, and the electrons in it are valence electrons. These are the electrons that break loose most easily, so they are the ones that matter most for electricity.

Every material falls into one of three groups, based mostly on how many valence electrons its atoms have.

GroupValence electronsWhat it doesExamples
ConductorUsually 3 or fewerLets current flow easilyCopper, silver
Semiconductor4In between a conductor and an insulatorSilicon, germanium
InsulatorUsually 5 or moreResists current flowPorcelain, plastic
Why it mattersSome trainees ask why they should learn theory if they just want to install things. Think of a car: if you only know how to swap parts, you cannot find out why it will not start. When you understand why something works, you can troubleshoot it instead of guessing.

Magnetism in electrical devices

Motors, relays, transformers and solenoids all work because of magnetism. A magnet has a north pole and a south pole. Opposite poles attract and like poles repel.

Electricity also makes magnetism. Current flowing through a wire makes a small magnetic field around it. Coil that wire around an iron bar and you get an electromagnet. That is the idea behind electric motors.

How power gets to you

Electricity comes from generating plants run by utilities. Steam from coal or nuclear plants spins huge generators called turbines. Hydroelectric, solar and wind plants make electricity too. Transformers step the voltage up for long-distance transmission, and then step it down again at substations and near your building. Transmission lines can carry as much as 750,000 volts.

WhereTypical voltage
Long-distance transmission linesAs high as 750,000V
Home, as supplied by the utilityAbout 240V
Wall outlet for small appliances such as a TV or toasterAbout 120V
Stoves, dryers, water heaters, central airThe full 240V

    Electrical Units of Measurement

    By the end of this section your student can define current, voltage and resistance, name their units, and use Ohm’s law to find a missing value.

    A

    Read it together

    about 12 min

    Three measurements run nearly everything in electrical work: current, voltage and resistance. Ohm's law ties them together, so if you know two of them you can find the third.

    Current

    Current is the movement, or flow, of electrons. It is shown by the letter I and measured in amperes, or amps (A). One amp means one coulomb of charge passing a point in a conductor in one second. A coulomb is a big counting unit for charge: about 6.25 quintillion electrons.

    Charge and current are not the same thing. Charge is an amount of electricity. Current is how fast that charge is moving.

    Coulomb's law gives the force between two charges. The force gets bigger as the charges get bigger, and it gets weaker as the charges move farther apart. Charges that are alike repel, and charges that are opposite attract.

    There are two ways to describe which direction current flows. Electron flow theory says current moves from negative to positive. Most electricians use it, and this curriculum does too. Many electronics engineers use conventional flow, which describes the opposite direction. Know which one you are using so you do not mix up directions or numbers.

    What is measuredUnitSymbol in a formula
    CurrentAmpere (A)I
    Voltage (force of current)Volt (V)E
    ResistanceOhm (Ω)R
    PowerWatt (W)P

    Voltage

    A charge can do work by pulling or pushing another charge. That ability is called potential. When two charges are different, there is a difference in potential between them. That difference is called potential difference, electromotive force (emf), or voltage. The letter for it is E.

    One volt is the potential difference between two points when one coulomb of charge does one joule of work. The joule is the unit of work.

    A battery is one way to make voltage. It uses a chemical reaction to build a big reserve of free electrons at the negative (−) terminal. The positive (+) terminal has had electrons removed, so it will accept electrons if there is a path for them. A battery that can no longer do this is dead. Batteries are rated in volts, and large ones are also rated in ampere-hours, where one ampere-hour is one amp supplied for one hour.

    Resistance

    Resistance is the opposition to current flow. All conductors have very low resistance and insulators have very high resistance. A resistor is a part made to have a known, specified resistance. Resistance is measured in ohms, shown by the symbol Ω, and the letter for it in a formula is R. One ohm is the resistance that limits the current to one amp when one volt is applied.

    For a wire, resistance depends on a few things.

    What changesEffect on the wire's resistance
    A longer wireMore resistance
    A bigger cross-sectional area (a thicker wire)Less resistance
    The materialEach material has its own. Copper building wire has less resistance than aluminum.
    The temperatureResistance changes with temperature
    The type of powerNo effect

    Ohm's law

    Ohm's law links current, voltage and resistance. If you know any two, you can find the third. There are three ways to write it, and each solves for a different value.

    • Current: I = E ÷ R
    • Resistance: R = E ÷ I
    • Voltage: E = I × R

    In a DC circuit, current goes up when voltage goes up, and current goes down when resistance goes up. The Ohm's law circle is a memory trick: cover the value you want to find, and the other two show how to work it out.

    You knowYou wantWorkAnswer
    E = 120V, R = 30ΩI120 ÷ 304A
    E = 240V, I = 20AR240 ÷ 2012Ω
    I = 15A, R = 8ΩE15 × 8120V
    I = 30A, R = 4ΩE30 × 4120V
    Key ideaCover the one you want with your thumb. If E is on top, with I and R side by side below it, then E ÷ R finds I, E ÷ I finds R, and I × R finds E.

      Module Section 3 of 3

      Reading Schematic Diagrams

      By the end of this section your student can read a resistor’s color bands, tell a series circuit from a parallel circuit, say which meter measures what and how it connects, and calculate power.

      A

      Read it together

      about 15 min

      A schematic is the shorthand electricians use to draw a circuit. Once you can read the symbols and tell a series circuit from a parallel one, you can follow the circuit and pick the right meter to test it.

      Schematics

      A schematic uses graphic symbols to show the parts of a circuit and how they connect. A simple circuit might show a battery, a switch and a lamp, joined by connecting wire. The textbook also has a chart of standard symbols for common parts. Many of them are shaped like what they stand for. For example, a battery shows + and − marks, a resistor has a jagged line, a transformer shows two coils, and a circuit breaker shows a line with a break in it.

      Resistors

      A resistor gives a circuit a particular resistance. With a known current and resistance, you can predict the change in voltage across it, called the voltage drop. In a 10V circuit with one resistor, the whole 10V drops across that resistor.

      There are two common types. A wire-wound resistor is nickel wire wound on a ceramic tube and covered with porcelain. A carbon composition resistor is molded from carbon and insulating material. Carbon composition resistors are smaller and cheaper, but the wire-wound type is tougher and can handle much more power. Fixed resistors have set values. Variable resistors, such as the rheostat and the potentiometer, can be adjusted.

      Small carbon resistors use a color code instead of printed numbers.

      • Bands 1 and 2 are the first two digits.
      • Band 3 is the multiplier, which is the number of zeros. Gold means × 0.1 and silver means × 0.01.
      • Band 4 is the tolerance. Gold is ±5% and silver is ±10%. No fourth band means ±20%.
      ColorDigitColorDigit
      Black0Violet7
      Brown1Gray8
      Red2White9
      Orange3Gold× 0.1, or ±5%
      Yellow4Silver× 0.01, or ±10%
      Green5
      Blue6
      Worked exampleBrown, black, red, gold. Brown is 1, black is 0, red means two zeros, so the value is 1,000Ω. Gold means ±5%, so the real value can be anywhere from 950Ω to 1,050Ω.

      Series circuits

      A series circuit has only one path for current. The total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances. In a 12V series circuit with two 30Ω loads, the total is 60Ω, so the current is 12 ÷ 60 = 0.2A. The current is the same through every load. The voltage drops across the loads add up to the voltage applied to the circuit.

      If a series circuit opens at any point, no current flows anywhere in it. If you have five bulbs in series and one blows, all five go out. Loads wired in series are uncommon.

      Parallel circuits

      In a parallel circuit, each load connects directly to the voltage source. Every load gets the same voltage, and the current divides between the loads. The total current is the sum of the individual currents. The total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance.

      Take a 12V parallel circuit with three 30Ω loads. Each load draws 12 ÷ 30 = 0.4A, so the total is 1.2A. The total resistance is 12 ÷ 1.2 = 10Ω. For two resistors in parallel, you can also use RT = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2). For 10Ω and 15Ω, that is 150 ÷ 25 = 6Ω.

      Unlike series circuits, parallel circuits keep working when one branch opens. Household circuits are wired in parallel, and almost every load circuit you will meet is parallel.

      SeriesParallel
      Paths for currentOneOne for each load
      CurrentSame through every loadDivides; the currents add up
      VoltageDrops add up to the applied voltageSame across every load
      Total resistanceAdd the resistancesLess than the smallest resistance
      If one load opensEverything stopsThe others keep working

      Electrical meters

      Electricians use test meters all the time. The most common is the volt-ohm-milliammeter (VOM), also called a multimeter. An analog meter has a pointer that moves in proportion to what is being measured, and the user reads a scale. A digital meter shows the number on a screen. Only qualified people may use these meters, so follow your company's safety policy.

      MeterMeasuresHow it connectsGood to know
      Clamp-on ammeterCurrentJaws around a single conductorThe circuit does not have to be opened
      In-line ammeterCurrentIn series, so the circuit must be openedLess common than the clamp-on type
      VoltmeterVoltageIn parallel, across the part being testedVoltage must be checked with power applied
      OhmmeterResistance and continuityAcross the part being testedAlways used with the system power shut off

      Measuring current

      • Put the clamp-on ammeter's jaws around one conductor at a time. Clamping two conductors at once gives a wrong reading.
      • Dirty or misaligned jaws give wrong readings.
      • Meters are precision tools, so handle them carefully.
      • On an analog meter, start at the highest range and work down. Too much current for the selected scale can damage it.

      Measuring resistance and checking continuity

      An ohmmeter has its own battery inside, so resistance is always measured with the system power shut off. An ohmmeter can measure the resistance of a load, such as motor windings, but more often it is used to check continuity. A wire or a closed switch has almost no resistance, so the meter reads zero. The circuit has continuity. If something is open, there is no path, and the meter shows infinite resistance.

      A continuity tester is a simple device with a battery and a buzzer or light. It can test a wire, and it can help identify wires inside a conduit. Touch the probe to the wires one at a time until it sounds, then tag both ends of that wire. Note that it only shows there is a path. It does not tell you the resistance value.

      SafetyA voltage tester shows whether voltage is present, and it can be used as a safety check before touching any terminals or conductors. Resistance is measured with the power off, and voltage is checked with the power on. Never mix those up.

      Calculating electrical power

      Power is the rate of doing work, or how fast energy is being used. It is measured in watts (W). One watt is one joule per second, which is the power used when one amp flows through one volt. So power equals current times voltage.

      • Power: P = I × E
      • Voltage: E = P ÷ I
      • Current: I = P ÷ E
      You knowYou wantWorkAnswer
      E = 100V, I = 2AP2 × 100200W
      P = 1,200W, I = 5AE1,200 ÷ 5240V
      P = 600W, E = 120VI600 ÷ 1205A
      I = 20A, E = 120VP20 × 1202,400W, or 2.4kW

      The prefix kilo means one thousand and mega means one million. So 1,000W is 1kW, and 1,000,000W is 1MW. One horsepower is 746 watts. Large amounts of energy are measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), which is kilowatts times hours. A 0.3kW light bulb running for 4 hours uses 0.3 × 4 = 1.2kWh.

      The power rating of a resistor

      Too much current heats a resistor. That heating is called I²R heating, and it is power lost as heat: P = I² × R. Every resistor has a wattage rating that says how much of this heating it can take. A 1W resistor will burn out if the circuit makes it give off heat faster than 1W. If you know the rating, you can find the most current it can carry: I = √(P ÷ R). A 4W resistor of 1Ω can carry at most √(4 ÷ 1) = 2A.

      Key ideaRatings assume the resistor sits in open air at 104°F (40°C) or cooler. In a small, crowded space, or somewhere hotter, a resistor can burn out before it reaches its rating.
