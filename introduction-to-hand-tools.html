Why this guide exists

You do not need to own a toolbox to support a student who is learning the trades. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning and put it in their own words. Hand tools are a great subject for that, because every tool has a name, a job, and a right and wrong way to use it.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 20 to 30 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Good to know before you start

This guide explains hand tools so your student can talk about them. It does not replace hands-on training, an instructor's or supervisor's directions, the employer's safety program or the manufacturer's instructions for any tool. Sizes, angles and ratings come from the textbook. Always follow the rules that apply at your student's school or job site, and wear the protective gear the job calls for. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 3

Common Hand Tools

By the end of this section your student can name common hammers, demolition tools, chisels, punches, screwdrivers, wrenches and pliers, say what each one is for, and use them the safe way.

A

Read it together

about 45 min

Every craft has its tools, and how a worker handles and cares for theirs shows their skill and their respect for the craft. A quality hand tool costs more at first, but if it is used and kept properly it can last for years.

Gear and working at height (1.0.0)

Some protective gear is worn all the time on a job site: safety glasses, safety shoes, a hard hat and gloves. When someone is working up high, every tool has to be handled with care so it cannot fall and hurt a person below. A tool should be tied to a tool belt with a lanyard.

Hammers (1.1.0)

Hammers come in different sizes and weights for different kinds of work. The safest ones have heads made of special alloys or drop-forged steel. Titanium hammers are light and strong. A hammer with a cast head (poured into a mold) is brittle and can chip or break, so it does not belong in construction work.

The hammers in this module
HammerWhat it is for
Claw hammer (1.1.1)Drives nails with the face and pulls them with the claw. Finish hammers are lighter (about 16 to 20 oz, and as light as 7 oz for brads). Framing hammers are heavier (up to about 28 oz), with a longer handle and often a milled face.
Drywall hammer (1.1.2)Light (12 to 14 oz) with a rounded face so it does not dent drywall. The hatchet-shaped end cuts small pieces and helps grab and carry sheets.
Ball peen hammer (1.1.3)Metalworking. A flat face for striking and a rounded end for peening metal or rivet heads. Weighs 4 oz to 2 lb. Used to strike chisels and punches.
Sledgehammer (1.1.4)Heavy duty: driving posts, demolition, breaking cast iron or concrete. High-carbon steel head of 2 to 20 lb, on a long or short handle.

Claw hammer faces and claws. A slightly rounded (convex) face lets a skilled worker drive a nail flush without seriously damaging the surface. A milled (waffled) face is mostly for framing, where the marks get hidden behind finish work. A curved claw gives extra leverage for pulling nails. A rip claw is flatter and is best for ripping boards and woodwork apart.

Driving a nail. Put on safety glasses. Rest the hammer face on the nail to find your starting spot, draw back and tap it to start the nail, then move your fingers away. Swing with your wrist, elbow and shoulder and strike the nail squarely with the center of the face. Hold the handle near the end, even with the lower edge of your palm, because holding closer to the head means you have to swing harder.

Pulling a nail. Slip the claw under the nail head and pry until the handle is nearly vertical. For a tight nail, slide a small block of wood under the hammer head for more leverage. The block also keeps the claw from scarring the workpiece.

NeverNever use a ball peen hammer to drive nails. Its head is made of milder steel, and pounding nails can damage the face. Ball peen and woodworking hammers are not interchangeable.

Using a sledgehammer (1.1.4)

  1. Wear gloves, eye protection and safety shoes, and inspect the sledgehammer for defects.
  2. Make sure no one is close enough to be hit on the backswing.
  3. Hold it with both hands apart, one hand over the other, and stand directly in front of the target.
  4. Lift it straight up above the target and set the head on the target.
  5. Start with short blows and slowly make the swing longer and stronger.
WarningDo not swing a sledgehammer past vertical and over your head. It can hurt your back and you lose control. Never hold an object with your hands while someone else swings. Crushed or broken bones can result.

Nail pullers and demolition tools (1.1.5)

Taking apart a wooden structure takes the right tool. The module covers three:

Nail pullers and wrecking bars
ToolWhat to know
Cat's pawA straight steel rod with a curved claw. Pulls nails driven flush or a little below the surface, lifting the head so another tool can finish. Its tight curve may not give enough leverage for long nails.
Chisel barA claw at one end and a broad, chisel-like beveled end at the other. The chisel end can be driven into wood joints or under a nail head.
Wrecking barAlso called a ripping bar or wonder bar. Has a nail slot, a wedge-shaped end for prying and a claw. Typically 12 to 42 inches long, and longer means more leverage.
WarningWear a hard hat, safety glasses and gloves. Never pull a bar toward your face. Pull it toward your shoulder. Do not push hard away from your body, because you can lose your balance. Always be ready for the sudden release of resistance.
Did you knowSome demolition tools combine jobs. The module's example, the Stanley Fubar, joins a pry bar and a hammer, with three striking surfaces and a chopping edge for softer materials like drywall.

Hammer care and safety (1.1.6)

  • Check that the handle is set tightly in the head and replace cracked or broken handles.
  • Keep the face clean. Do not use a hammer with a chipped or damaged head, or a cast head.
  • Hold it near the end of the handle and hit the nail squarely. Do not strike with the cheek or side unless the hammer was made for that.
  • Never strike two hammer heads together.
  • For sledgehammers: use the right amount of force, keep your hands away from the object, and look behind you for people and objects before swinging.
  • For ripping and nail pulling: use two hands, and make sure the wood holding the nail is braced. Many accidents happen when a pry bar slips and the worker falls.

Chisels (1.2.1)

A chisel is a metal tool with a sharpened, beveled edge, made from heat-treated steel. Wood chisels are used only on wood. Cold chisels are used on metal and stone. The name comes from working with cold metal rather than metal heated by a torch.

Chisels in the module
ChiselWhat to know
Flat cold chiselMost common cold chisel. Tip ground to 60 degrees. Cuts through rusted or damaged bolts and hardware that wrenches cannot remove.
Cape chiselThe cutting tip is slightly wider than the body, so it does not bind in the cut. Cuts single grooves or a series of parallel grooves.
Round nose chiselSpecialized work like forming flutes and channels.
Diamond-pointed chiselSquare tip with a single bevel. Cleans up the inside angles of channels and chips through thin plate.
Bevel-edged wood chiselSlightly angled edges that push easily into corners, such as when finishing dovetail joints.
Paring chiselLonger and thinner. Reaches deep into long joints for a precise fit. Most are for hand use only and are never struck with a hammer. If the handle has no metal striking surface, do not strike it.

Using a cold chisel. Wear eye protection, secure the work in a vise, set the blade where the cut will go, and strike with a ball peen hammer. Repeat as needed.

Using a wood chisel. Outline the area. Set the chisel at one end of the outline with the edge on the cross-grain line and the bevel facing the recess. Tap lightly with a mallet, then do the other end. Make a series of cuts about 1/4 inch apart. Then pare the notched wood away, bevel side down, slicing inward from the end.

Punches (1.2.2)

A punch uses a hammer's impact to indent metal, start a drill bit, drive pins or line up holes. Punches are made of hardened and tempered steel. To use one, hold it straight with one hand and strike it squarely.

  • Center punch: heavier, with a 60-degree tip. Its wide dent is best for marking a spot to drill.
  • Prick punch: shallower angle and smaller dent. Best for general layout on sheet metal.
  • Tapered (straight) punch: blunt end, used to drive out pins and align holes in mating parts.

Chisel and punch care (1.2.3)

  • Wear gloves and safety goggles.
  • Keep a wood chisel's bevel at 25 degrees and a cold chisel's at 60 degrees. Sharpen on an oilstone, and sharpen punches on a bench grinder.
  • Do not use a chisel, punch or hammer with a mushroomed (flattened, spread-out) head.
WarningStriking a mushroomed chisel or punch can break off metal chips that cause serious eye injuries. Pull it out of service until it is repaired or replaced under company policy.

Screwdrivers (1.3.0)

Screwdrivers are named for the screw they fit. The most common are slotted and Phillips. The module also covers four specialized types. Torx, Robertson and Allen give the most grip and can take more torque without stripping the head.

Screwdriver and screw head types
TypeWhat to know
SlottedAlso called straight, flat or standard tip. Very common but gives the least grip, so it limits torque.
PhillipsThe most common crosshead. Better grip than slotted.
Clutch-driveHourglass-shaped head with good grip. Common where vandal resistance matters.
TorxStar-shaped tip. Used in mechanical assembly and repair and in residential construction.
Robertson (square)Square drive for extra grip and torque. Also popular in residential construction, inside and out.
Allen (hex)For socket-head screws with a six-sided recess. The L-shaped steel bars are generally called wrenches.

Parts. The handle gives you a grip, the shank is the metal between the handle and the blade, and the blade is the formed end that fits the screw head. Good ones are made of tempered steel and have the shank running through the whole handle.

Fit and use. The blade should fit the screw head snugly and not be too wide, too narrow, too thick, too thin or loose. The wrong size can damage the tool or the screw. Even a Reed and Prince screw, which looks like a Phillips, will slip and get damaged with a Phillips driver. Wear gloves, hold the shank square (at a right angle) to the work, press firmly and steadily, and turn clockwise to tighten and counterclockwise to loosen. Do not let the bit spin inside the head.

Screws versus nailsScrews hold better than nails in most situations because their threads grip the material. Self-tapping screws cut their own threads, but in woodworking a small starter hole helps keep the wood from splitting.

Screwdriver care (1.3.1)

  • Keep screwdrivers free of dirt, grease and grit so they do not slip.
  • Inspect before use. Repair or replace one with a worn handle or a tip that is not straight and smooth.
  • Never use a screwdriver as a punch, chisel or pry bar.
  • Do not use or lay one near energized conductors, and do not expose the handle to excessive heat.
  • Carry screwdrivers with the tip down.

Wrenches (1.4.0)

Wrenches hold and turn nuts, bolts, pipes and anything that needs leverage to turn. They fall into two groups: non-adjustable (fits one size) and adjustable. Wrenches and hardware come in both Imperial and metric sizes, and the two are not interchangeable.

Non-adjustable wrenches (1.4.1)
WrenchWhat to know
Open-endEasy to slip around a fastener but grips less. Most have two sizes, one at each end.
Box-endWraps all the way around the head, so it grips firmer and slips less. Has 6 or 12 flats. Most have two sizes.
Ratcheting box-endPopular for assembly, but shorter, so less leverage. Not the best for freeing corroded or tight hardware.
CombinationOne open end and one box end, both the same size.

Sizing. The wrench size is the distance between the flats of the wrench opening, and it has to match the distance across the fastener head. A 1/2-inch bolt head needs a 1/2-inch wrench. Bolts are sized by the thread diameter, not the head, so a 5/16-inch bolt usually has a 1/2-inch head and a 3/8-inch bolt a 9/16-inch head. Six flats are less likely to round off a bolt. Twelve flats give twelve positions to settle onto the fastener instead of six, which helps in tight spots.

Use. Always use the right size, snug on the fastener. Pull the wrench toward your body to turn it. Pushing away is more likely to cause an injury.

Adjustable wrenches (1.4.2)
WrenchWhat to know
Adjustable (Crescent)One fixed jaw, one movable jaw, and an adjusting nut that moves the jaw. Lengths from 4 to 30 inches. Flexible, but a fixed-jaw wrench is usually better and safer. The two are often used together.
Pipe wrenchSerrated teeth on both jaws grip smooth pipe. The spring-loaded jaw lets you reposition without readjusting.
Spud wrenchSmooth jaws that do not grip round pipe. Used on large nuts in plumbing and piping.

Set the jaws close to the size, apply the pressure on the fixed jaw and not the movable jaw, then tighten the jaws fully (a light shake while you turn the adjusting nut helps). Pull toward your body. Most hardware has right-hand threads: clockwise tightens, counterclockwise loosens. It is easy to turn a fastener the wrong way when trying to remove it, which only makes it tighter.

WarningBadly adjusted jaws can slip, and you can lose your balance. If you must push on a wrench, brace yourself with your free hand. Wear gloves.

Socket wrenches (1.4.3)

A socket wrench uses a ratchet: the socket turns the fastener one way and spins freely the other way, so you do not have to remove and refit the wrench after every turn. The square shaft that holds the socket has a drive size, usually 1/4, 3/8 or 1/2 inch (up to 2-1/2 inches for heavy industrial work). That size describes the drive, not the hardware, and there are no metric drive sizes even though metric sockets are common.

  • Most sockets have 6 or 12 flats, and 6 flats grip better.
  • Deep sockets fit a bolt that sticks out past the nut. Extensions come in many lengths and reach into tight spaces.
  • The knob or lever on the ratchet head reverses the free-spinning direction. Longer handles give more leverage.
  • A hinged handle, or breaker bar, is for breaking a stubborn fastener loose when no ratcheting is needed.
  • A torque wrench applies a set amount of torque. Torque is distance times force (inch-pounds, foot-pounds, or newton-meters; 1 inch-pound is 0.113 newton-meter).

Wrench care and safety (1.4.4)

  • Pull the wrench toward your body, never toward your face or away from you.
  • Check the handle and jaw surfaces before use. Keep adjustable wrenches clean so mud and grease do not clog the adjusting screw.
  • Do not use a wrench as a hammer. Never go beyond its capacity, and never slip a cheater bar or pipe over it, because it can snap the tool or break the bolt head off.
  • Clean sockets and ratchets after every use.
Tool lanyardsA dropped tool at height can seriously injure or kill someone, and it can destroy the tool. Tethering tools to the belt or wrist prevents injuries, protects the tools and saves the time spent retrieving them.

Pliers and wire cutters (1.5.0)

Pliers are hinged tools that hold, cut and bend wire and soft metals. They should not be used to tighten or loosen nuts and bolts, because they round off the corners and then wrenches no longer fit. Good pliers are hardened tool steel.

Common pliers
PliersWhat to know
Slip-joint (1.5.1)Hold and bend wire and grip parts during assembly. Adjustable jaws with two or three positions.
Long-nose (1.5.2)Also called needle-nose. Reach into tight places, grip small parts and bend angles or loops in electrical wire. Most have wire-cutting jaws near the pivot.
Lineman's (1.5.3)Also called side cutters. Wider, heavier jaws. Cut electrical wire and hold workpieces. To cut, point the loose end down and squeeze at a right angle to the wire.
Tongue-and-groove (1.5.4)Serrated teeth grip flat, square, round or hex objects. The jaw sets in several positions. Large ones are used on pipe. Jaws can be straight, smooth, V-shaped or curved.
Locking (1.5.5)Clamp onto an object like a vise. Often called Vise-Grips (a brand name). Good for holding metal together for welding and for gripping a damaged nut or bolt. A knob adjusts the jaw, and a lever releases the lock.

Plier safety (1.5.6)

  • Wear protective gear, especially when cutting wire. Hold the scrap end of cut wire to stop flying metal bits.
  • Hold the pliers near the end of the handles to avoid pinching your fingers in the hinge. Do not lengthen the handles for more leverage. Use a larger pair.
  • Cut at right angles. Do not rock the pliers side to side or bend the wire back and forth against the blades.
  • Never use pliers on energized circuits, even with plastic-coated handles, because they are not always rated for shock.
  • Keep them lubricated, away from excess heat, and never use them as a hammer.

    Measurement and Layout Tools

    By the end of this section your student can choose and read a measuring tool, tell level from plumb, and use levels, squares, a plumb bob and a chalk line.

    A

    Read it together

    about 30 min

    Good work starts with good measurements. Wrong measurements and crooked parts cause problems on the job, so craftworkers learn to take precise measurements and to set things plumb (perfectly vertical) and level.

    Four measuring tools (2.1.0)

    The module says to weigh accuracy, ease of use, durability and readability, and to make sure the tool suits the length being measured.

    Rules and measuring tools
    ToolWhat to know
    Flat steel rule (2.1.1)Usually 6 or 12 inches (15 or 30 cm). Longer shop versions are 1 yard or 1 meter. Accurate, and also used as a straightedge for layout. Thin and flexible, or thicker and rigid.
    Tape measure (2.1.2)Marked in 1/16 inch or 1 mm. The blade curves (concave) to stay rigid when extended. The hook slides slightly to allow for its own thickness on inside and outside measurements. For an inside measurement, add the width stamped on the case. Replace the tape if the hook is damaged.
    Wooden folding rule (2.1.3)Marked in sixteenths. Imperial versions come in 6 and 8 foot lengths (1 and 2 meters in metric). Rigid, so better than a tape for things like vertical measurement. May show wall-framing marks at 16, 19.2 and 24 inches.
    Laser measuring tool (2.1.4)Battery-powered. Point it, press a button, and the beam's travel time gives the distance. Reports in Imperial and metric and can store measurements.

    Laser tool pros. Long vertical measurements from the ground. Longer distances (some to 600 feet, sometimes with a target plate). Built-in area and add-or-subtract functions. Stored readings and Bluetooth. Some have built-in levels.

    Laser tool cons. Accuracy can suffer at long distances, and some workers simply do not trust electronic readings. Very hot or smoky air scatters the beam. A shiny target can interfere, so stick a piece of matte tape on it.

    Care of measuring tools (2.1.5)

    • Put a few drops of lubricant on the spring joints of a folding rule and steel tape from time to time.
    • Wipe moisture off steel tapes to prevent rust where the coating is worn.
    • Do not kink or twist steel tapes. The damage is permanent.
    • Do not use a steel measuring device near energized electrical parts.
    • Keep laser tools away from water and excess heat.
    Precision toolsMicrometers and calipers measure machined parts down to 0.0001 inch. A standard micrometer's smallest division is 0.001 inch, and digital ones can read as small as 0.00005 inch.

    Levels (2.2.0 and 2.2.1 to 2.2.4)

    A level shows how close a surface is to true horizontal (level) or true vertical (plumb).

    • Spirit level. The most common leveling tool. The vials hold colored ethanol (alcohol is called "spirits") because it does not freeze like water and moves more freely. Most have three vials: the center vial checks level and the two end vials check plumb. When the bubble is centered between the lines, the surface is level or plumb. Clear debris off the surface first.
    • Torpedo level. A short spirit level that also shows a true 45-degree angle. Often has a magnetic or V-shaped base, so it is handy for pipefitting.
    • Post level. Straps to a post so you can see plumb in two directions at once. Many have magnets for steel posts.
    • Digital level. A digital readout of slope in degrees plus rate of rise, for laying out stairs and roofs or checking the fall of a drain line.
    • Laser level. Lets one worker establish plumb, level or square. Used for foundation elevations, drainage slopes, square framing, lining up plumbing and electrical lines, and hanging suspended ceilings. Works over distances well beyond a regular level.
    WarningNever look directly into a laser beam or its source. It can permanently damage your eyes.
    • Replace a level if a vial cracks or looks loose. Keep levels clean and dry. Do not bend them, drop them or throw them.

    Squares (2.2.5 and 2.2.6)

    A square is used to mark, to measure and to check that a joint is square. The kind you use depends on the job and your preference.

    Types of squares
    SquareWhat to know
    Carpenter's (framing) squareL-shaped, with a 24-inch blade and 16-inch tongue (600 mm and 400 mm in metric). Squares up baseplates and walls, and marks square lines on wide lumber. Tables and formulas are printed on the blade. A pipefitter's square looks the same but carries pipefitting formulas.
    Rafter angle (speed) squareCast aluminum or tough plastic. Combines a protractor, try square and framing square, with degree marks for quick rafter layout. Small and easy to carry.
    Try squareA fixed 90-degree angle, mostly for woodworking. Lays out 90-degree lines and checks joints, and checks lumber for warps or cups.
    Combination squareA ruled blade that slides in a head with 45- and 90-degree faces. Many have a small spirit level and a scribe for metal. The most versatile layout square.

    Marking with a combination square. For 90 degrees: set the blade long enough to cross the material, hold the front edge of the head firmly against the material, and draw along the blade. For 45 degrees: hold the back of the head against the edge and draw along the blade at an angle.

    Care. Do not pry or hammer with a square, and protect the edges and blades. Keep it dry with a light coat of silicone spray. Do not drop it, since the angle can change (a try square is easier to damage than a carpenter's square). To check a square, scribe a line on straight lumber, flip the square over and scribe in the same place. The two lines should match.

    Did you knowSome combination square sets can be set to any angle with a rotating protractor head, and a centering head finds the center of round stock. Basic geometry like this shows up every day in the crafts.

    Plumb bob (2.2.7)

    A pointed weight on a string. Gravity pulls the string perfectly vertical. Common weights are 20, 16, 12 and 8 oz, and heavier ones hold steady better on long drops.

    1. Make sure the line is attached at the exact top center of the bob.
    2. Hang it from a horizontal member (a door jamb, joist or beam). Do not drop it on its point.
    3. When it hangs free and stops swinging, the string is plumb. Outdoors, wind can swing it.
    4. Mark the point directly below the tip. It is straight below the point of attachment.

    Use it to check a wall or door jamb or to find the spot on a floor directly under a beam that needs a support post.

    Chalk lines (2.2.8)

    A reel of string coated with chalk, used to mark long straight lines on smooth surfaces. Pull the line out (it chalks itself), secure one end, pull it tight between the two points, then pull the string straight up and let go to snap a line. Spray the chalk with clear lacquer so it does not wash away or get scuffed, and store the chalk in a dry place because wet chalk is unusable. Some chalk lines can double as a lightweight plumb bob.

    Did you knowBricklayers use a straight level and a plumb line to keep each course of bricks level and the wall straight. An uneven wall is weak as well as unattractive. People have used bricks in construction for more than 10,000 years.

      Other Common Hand Tools

      By the end of this section your student can match a saw, clamp, file, knife or shovel to the job it does best and use each one safely.

      A

      Read it together

      about 35 min

      Handsaws have been used since before 1500 BC, and clamps and vises are still things a power tool cannot replace. Power tools do much of the old handwork now, but these hand tools remain on the job site every day.

      Saws (3.1.0)

      Every saw has cutting teeth. The shape and size of the teeth set how smooth the cut is and how fast it goes. Fewer teeth per inch (tpi) means a coarser, faster cut. More teeth per inch means smaller teeth and a slower, smoother cut.

      Common saws (Figure 42)
      SawWhat to know
      Backsaw8 to 14 inch blade, 11 to 14 tpi. Broad, flat blade with a reinforced back that keeps it straight. Smooth cuts for finish lumber and miter joints.
      Keyhole saw12 to 14 inch blade, 7 to 8 tpi. Cuts curves quickly in wood, plywood or wallboard. Cuts holes for large pipes, vents and electrical boxes. Named for cutting keyholes in doors.
      Coping sawNarrow, flexible blade in a U-shaped frame, 10 to 20 tpi. Tight curves and circles in finish carpentry. Usually mounted to cut on the pull stroke.
      Drywall sawLong, narrow, sharp-pointed, for quick cuts in soft building materials. The blade can be fixed or retractable.
      Handsaw26 inch blade. Crosscut saws have 8 to 14 tpi and cut across the grain. Ripsaws have 5 to 9 tpi and cut with the grain.
      Hacksaw8 to 16 inch blade, 14 to 32 tpi. Cuts metal such as bolts and pipe. The blade goes on with the teeth facing away from the handle, and it cuts on the push stroke.
      WarningSaw teeth are very sharp. Use gloves, do not handle teeth bare-handed, and keep your fingers clear of the teeth while cutting.

      Handsaws and how to use them (3.1.1)

      A handsaw blade is tempered steel so it stays sharp and does not buckle. The teeth are set (angled) alternately to each side so the cut, called the kerf, is a little wider than the blade. A crosscut saw cuts slower than a ripsaw but leaves a smoother surface. A 24 or 26 inch blade is a good general choice, since longer strokes make a better, more consistent cut.

      1. Put on safety glasses and work gloves. Mark the cut with a square, and put an X on the scrap side of the line. The kerf should always come out of the scrap side.
      2. Support the work on a sawhorse and clamp it. Plan to support the scrap end too, so it does not break off as the blade nears the end of the cut.
      3. Set the teeth on the edge of the wood farthest from you, just outside the mark on the scrap side.
      4. Start by pulling the blade toward you, near the handle end, with the saw at about 45 degrees to the wood, to make a small kerf.
      5. Then push to cut. Start slowly and lengthen the strokes as the kerf deepens. The push stroke does the cutting, so it is the more forceful one.
      6. Guide the saw with the thumb of your free hand so the blade stays perpendicular to the work.
      7. Do not force the saw. Let its weight set the pace. If it wanders, work it back toward the line. If it binds, put a wooden wedge in the kerf.

      A ripsaw is started the same way but, once the cut is going, it is held at a steeper 60 degrees.

      Saw care (3.1.2)

      • Brace yourself so you are not thrown off balance when the scrap separates. Clamp the work.
      • Clean blades with fine emery cloth or steel wool, and use silicone lubricant if rust starts.
      • Lay a saw down gently. A bent or buckled blade cannot be repaired.
      • Do not saw into stone, concrete or metal with a handsaw. It ruins the teeth.

      Clamps (3.2.0)

      Clamps are sized by the largest opening of the jaws. The throat (depth) decides how far from the edge you can clamp.

      Types of clamps (Figure 44)
      ClampWhat to know
      C-clampMultipurpose, with a C-shaped frame and a screw with a metal shoe, tightened with a sliding T-bar handle. Strong and durable, usually cast iron or steel.
      Locking C-clamp pliersPliers that also work as a clamp. A knob in the handle sets jaw width and tension. Close the handles to lock, press the lever to release.
      Spring clampSpring-operated. Jaws are usually cushioned to protect the work.
      Bar clampA steel or aluminum bar with a fixed jaw and a sliding jaw. Padded jaws, quick release trigger, and the jaw can be flipped to work as a spreader.
      Pipe clampUses any length of pipe as the spine, with a fixed jaw and a movable jaw. Popular for large and wide clamping jobs.
      Hand-screw clampBroad wooden jaws spread pressure over a wide area. The two jaws work independently on two threaded rods and can be angled or kept parallel.
      Web (cargo, band) strapNot technically a clamp. A canvas or nylon strap with a ratcheting head that applies even pressure around a bundle or secures a load.

      When clamping soft material, put rubber pads or thin wood blocks between the jaws and the work. Protect the corners of finish lumber when strapping it.

      Clamp care (3.2.1)

      • Store clamps lightly clamped to a rack. Throw away clamps with bent frames or spines (a pipe clamp spine is easy to replace).
      • Clean and lightly lubricate the moving parts, but not too much, since oil collects dirt and dust.
      • Never use any of these clamps to hoist work, and never add leverage with a pipe or other tool.

      Files, rasps and utility knives (3.3.0)

      Files and rasps shape wood and metal, remove burrs and smooth rough spots. Utility knives are general purpose and common to all the crafts, but they also cause many injuries. Many companies have rules for them, and some job sites require self-retracting blades and ban the rest.

      WarningFiles and knives have sharp points and edges. Never use a file or rasp without a handle on it.

      Files and rasps (3.3.1)

      • Files have slanting, parallel rows of teeth and are used on metal. Rasps and wood shavers have deeper, individual teeth and are for wood and similar soft materials. A wood shaver has an open, grater-like design that lets debris pass through.
      • A file is sized by the length of its body, not the handle. The sharp point that fits into the handle is the tang. Lengths run from 4 to 16 inches.
      • Choose a profile that fits the area: half-round for large concave or flat surfaces, round (tapered) for small curves and enlarging holes, and triangular for angles and inside corners. Others include flat, square, mill and hand.
      • Files for soft material have wider-spaced teeth, and files for hard material have closer teeth. A soft-material file on metal chips and dulls. A metal file on wood clogs.
      • Single-cut files have one direction of teeth. Double-cut files have crisscrossing rows in a diamond pattern.
      • Textures from roughest to smoothest: rough, coarse, bastard, second cut (medium), smooth, dead smooth. Smoother files leave the smoothest finish but remove the least material per stroke.

      Using a file. Put on gloves and safety glasses, and check that the handle is tight. Hold the work in a vise or clamp at about elbow height. Stand back with your feet about 24 inches apart (dominant-side foot ahead) and do not lean over the work. Hold the handle in one hand and steady the tip with the other. Press only on the forward stroke, lift slightly on the back stroke, and keep the file flat on the work.

      Utility knives (3.3.2)

      A utility knife has a thin, replaceable, razor-sharp blade and cuts roofing felt, shingles, vinyl or linoleum floor tile, fiberboard and drywall. It also trims insulation and opens cartons. Some retract the blade when you let go of the blade control. The carpet knife blade has two sharpened edges, so it can be turned around to last longer, because carpet is hard on blades. Wear appropriate gloves (Kevlar offers more protection).

      Using one. Put a protective barrier, like scrap wood, under the cut. Put on gloves and push the blade out. Cut away from your body or across it, never toward it, and keep your free hand out of the line of the cut. Retract and lock the blade when done.

      File and knife care (3.3.3)

      • Use the right file for the material and always put a handle on it. Brush filings out of the teeth with a file card or wire brush, along the teeth.
      • Store files dry and separate so they do not chip each other. Do not let the work vibrate in the vise.
      • Replace utility knife blades when they stop cutting and start tearing, since sharp blades are safer than dull ones. Keep the blade closed and locked when not in use. Blades are brittle and can snap, leaving a jagged edge.

      Shovels and picks (3.4.0)

      Shovels and digging tools
      ToolBest use
      Round shovelDigging holes and moving soil.
      Square shovelMoving gravel, sand and cleaning up construction debris on a hard surface.
      SpadeShaping trenches or holes with smooth, straight sides. Flatter, with no curved sides.
      PickBreaking hard or rocky soil, loosening soil, leveling stones and pavers, and breaking up stone and concrete. Wooden handle 36 to 45 inches, forged steel head 2 to 3 lb.
      MattockBreaking hard soil and digging trenches. Much better than a pick for clearing tree roots. One wide blade, and the other side is a pick or a second blade turned 90 degrees. Handles rarely longer than 36 inches.

      Round shovels and spades work best on fresh earth: place the tip, put a foot on the step at the top of the blade and press down. Handles are usually 47 to 48 inches (long) or 27 inches (short, often with a D-ring). A shovel is not a lever, so clear roots and obstructions with other tools first.

      For a pick or mattock, choose a length to suit your size and the job. Wear gloves and steel-toe boots or shoes. Hold one hand at the end of the handle and the other about two thirds of the way up. Raise it, bend your knees, and let the weight of the head and the leverage of the handle deliver the blow.

      WarningDo not swing a pick or mattock past vertical and over your head. It can hurt your back and you lose control.

      Shovel and pick safety (3.4.1)

      • Check that the blade or head is fixed firmly to the handle, with no cracks or splits.
      • Wear eye protection, gloves and safety shoes or boots. Brush or rinse the blade clean after use.
      • Make sure no one is in the swing path, use a pick of the right length and weight for your size, and use maximum swings only when necessary.
