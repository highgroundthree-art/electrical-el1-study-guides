Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do electrical work. Never use it in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 4

Electrical Hazards

By the end of this section your student can describe what electricity does to the body, how to prove a circuit is off, what causes incidents, and how to rank ways of reducing a hazard.

A

Read it together

about 8 min

You cannot see electricity, and it gives no warning. Electrical safety starts with assuming the danger is there, then choosing protection before the work begins.

What hurts people on construction sites

OSHA, the government agency for workplace safety, names four hazards that cause most deaths on construction sites. They call them the Focus Four: falls from high places, electric shock and burns from electric arcs, being crushed by falling materials or equipment, and being struck by flying objects or moving equipment like trucks and forklifts. Together they account for about 90 percent of construction deaths. Most injuries, from small to deadly, can be prevented.

How a shock happens

Electricity flows through a conductor. Metals like copper and steel are great conductors, and so is the human body, especially when the skin is wet or sweaty. A shock happens when your body becomes part of the circuit. That can happen three ways: touching both wires of a circuit, touching one wire and the ground, or touching a metal part that has become live while you are also touching the ground.

Small numbers, big effects

Current is measured in amps. A milliamp (mA) is one-thousandth of an amp. Here is what the module says different amounts can do:

CurrentWhat can happen
1 mAA slight tingle
5 mAA slight shock, but you may jerk away and fall
6 to 30 mAPainful shock and loss of muscle control
50 to 150 mAExtreme pain, breathing can stop, death possible
1,000 to 4,300 mAThe heart fibrillates, usually fatal

For a sense of scale, a 60-watt light bulb uses about 500 mA. How badly someone is hurt depends on three things: how much current flows through the body, what path it takes, and how long it lasts. Often the shock is only the start of a chain, and the real injury comes from a fall.

Because shock can injure tissue deep inside, anyone who gets a shock, even a minor one, should see a medical professional.

Three kinds of burns

  • Electrical burns happen when current heats the tissue it passes through. A small mark on the skin can hide severe damage inside.
  • Arc burns come from an arc, a bright electrical blast between metal parts. An arc can reach about 35,000°F, roughly four times hotter than the surface of the sun, and can burn people several feet away.
  • Thermal contact burns come from touching hot or molten metal thrown out by the blast. The blast can also throw people, cause deafness, and send shrapnel flying.

Habits that keep you safe

  • Take off rings, watches, bracelets and necklaces before working on electrical equipment. Jewelry conducts electricity.
  • Plan the job before you start. If the work changes, stop and ask your supervisor.
  • When you come back from a break, look over your surroundings again.
  • Know where the nearest phone is and what number to call.

Prove it is off

Always assume a circuit is live until you have proved it is not. The proof is called a live-dead-live test:

  • Make sure the circuit is locked and tagged out.
  • Test your meter on a known live source, to prove the meter works.
  • Test the circuit, wearing the right PPE. The reading should be zero.
  • Test your meter on the known source again, to prove it still works.

Even after all that, test before touching any conductor.

Why incidents happen

There are three basic causes: unsafe conditions, unsafe equipment, and unsafe acts. Common examples are cords with damaged insulation, missing ground prongs, no ground-fault protection, contact with overhead power lines, and equipment used in a way it was never designed for.

Ranking ways to reduce a hazard

The module lists six controls, from strongest to weakest. The first three work at the source of the hazard and do not rely on people being perfect.

  • Elimination: remove the hazard, for example by locking out the power.
  • Substitution: replace it with something less hazardous, for example 24-volt instead of 120-volt circuits.
  • Engineering controls: isolate the hazard with barricades, guards or interlocks.
  • Awareness: warn people with signs and labels.
  • Administrative controls: change how people work, with procedures, training and schedules.
  • PPE: protective gear, the last line of defense.

    Using PPE to Reduce the Risk of Injury

    By the end of this section your student can explain why gear must match the hazard and be checked every time, and can name the main pieces of electrical protective equipment.

    A

    Read it together

    about 8 min

    PPE stands for personal protective equipment. It is the last line of defense. It protects you, but it does not make a job safe on its own.

    Why last?

    When a hazard cannot be removed or blocked off, PPE is the only thing left between the worker and the hazard. Gear can fail, be damaged, be the wrong type, or not be worn. That is why the safest plan removes the hazard first and uses PPE as the backup.

    OSHA rules say employers must provide protective equipment that fits the body part and the work, and that equipment must be kept in safe, reliable condition and checked or tested on a schedule. Workers must also use insulated tools near live parts, and fuses may only be handled with insulated equipment.

    Head, eyes and face

    Electrical workers wear hard hats rated for electrical hazards. A Class E hard hat is tested to withstand 20,000 volts for three minutes. A Class G hard hat is tested to 2,200 volts for one minute. Safety glasses are worn at all times near energized equipment, and a face shield goes over them when there is danger from an arc or flash.

    Rubber gloves

    Rubber insulating gloves must match the voltage of the work. The tag color tells you the class and the highest voltage:

    ClassTag colorMaximum voltage
    00Beige500 V
    0Red1,000 V
    1White7,500 V
    2Yellow17,000 V
    3Green26,500 V
    4Orange36,000 V

    Gloves only protect if they are in good shape, so there is a routine:

    • The worker inspects the gloves every day before use. Look for cuts, pinholes, deep scratches, snags, and anything stuck in the rubber.
    • Do an air test: stretch the glove, roll it to trap air, squeeze, and listen for leaks. Never use compressed gas to inflate gloves.
    • A testing lab must test rubber gloves every six months. The test date is stamped on them, so check it.
    • Wear leather protectors over the rubber gloves to guard against cuts, punctures and some arc flash.
    • Never wear a watch or ring under rubber gloves.
    • Keep gloves away from oil and petroleum products. Store them cool, dry, dark, and right side out.
    • If gloves fail an inspection, turn them in. Never leave a damaged glove lying around.

    Rubber blankets are used to cover energized equipment. They are tested every year and inspected before each use. Blankets are not for floors. Floors use switchboard matting.

    Clothing

    Around live equipment, workers wear arc-rated clothing. Underneath, wear natural fibers or flame-resistant fabric. Never wear polyester, nylon or acrylic, because those melt onto skin in a flash and make burns worse.

    Special tools

    • A hot stick is an insulated pole used to operate disconnect switches, put in or pull fuses, and place or remove temporary grounds from a distance. Wipe it clean and inspect it before every use. Hang it up vertically when stored.
    • A fuse puller is an insulated plastic or fiberglass tool. Fuses are installed or removed only when the circuit is safely de-energized.
    • A shorting probe is a special tool qualified people use to safely discharge stored electricity from capacitors on circuits that are already de-energized.
    Case historyAn electrician was cleaning a high-voltage switch cabinet. He did not test every part inside it for voltage, and he was not wearing rubber gloves or arc-rated clothing. A cobweb falling across the open switch set off an arc blast that lasted about one-sixth of a second. He suffered third-degree burns and needed skin grafts. The lesson: test everything, and wear the right PPE.

      Electrical Safety Standards

      By the end of this section your student can explain who is responsible for safety, how lockout/tagout works, what the safe-distance boundaries are, and how workers prevent mistakes.

      A

      Read it together

      about 10 min

      Safety rules exist because people got hurt. This section covers the main rules electricians follow and the habits that keep human mistakes from turning into injuries.

      OSHA and who is responsible

      OSHA is the federal agency that sets and enforces workplace safety law. Two sets of OSHA rules matter most to electricians: Part 1910 covers general industry, and Part 1926 covers construction.

      • Employers must train workers, provide PPE, tell workers about hazards, give clear written instructions, and make sure safe procedures are followed.
      • Employees must read and follow the safety rules, inspect and use PPE, look out for themselves and others, and stay within the job plan.

      Staying inside the job plan matters. Many incidents trace back to what the module calls task creep. For example, you are taking a reading at a motor starter and notice a loose connection. Do not grab a screwdriver and tighten it. That puts you in contact with a live part. Stop, and plan it properly.

      Basic job-site rules

      • Wear hard hats and safety shoes, and safety glasses with side shields.
      • Skip loose or torn clothing and anything with exposed metal zippers or buttons.
      • Use tools that are either grounded (three-prong plug) or double-insulated.
      • Use three-wire extension cords, protected from damage.
      • Cover any hole bigger than 2 inches and mark it "HOLE" or "COVER."
      • All temporary 125-volt outlets rated 15, 20 or 30 amps must have GFCI protection. A GFCI compares the current going out with the current coming back. If the difference reaches about 5 mA, it shuts the circuit off.

      Lockout/tagout

      Lockout/tagout, often shortened to LOTO, keeps equipment from being switched on while someone is working on it. A lock physically holds the switch or breaker off. A tag warns everyone and says who put the lock there. The module's example procedure goes like this:

      • Tell everyone affected that lockout/tagout is happening, and why.
      • Shut the equipment down the normal way, then open and lock the energy sources.
      • Each worker puts on their own lock and tag.
      • Release or drain any stored energy.
      • Test with a meter to prove the equipment is de-energized.
      • If you leave and come back, test again.

      To restore power: make sure all people and tools are accounted for, put everything back together, put safety controls back, remove locks and tags (each worker removes only their own), tell everyone affected, then turn the power on. Only if a worker is gone or a key is lost may an authorized supervisor remove the device.

      NFPA 70E and who may do what

      OSHA writes the law. NFPA 70E is the national standard that spells out practical safe work practices for electrical hazards. It requires safety training, and retraining at least every three years.

      The standard uses two labels. A qualified person has shown the skills and knowledge for that equipment and has had safety training to spot and avoid its hazards. An unqualified person is anyone who is not. These labels have nothing to do with having a license or how long someone has been in the trade. An unqualified person must be told about the hazards and escorted by a qualified person if they go inside certain boundaries.

      The invisible boundaries

      Picture invisible lines around exposed live parts. The higher the voltage, the farther out they sit.

      • Limited approach boundary: there is a shock hazard inside it. Only qualified people may cross. Unqualified people must be escorted.
      • Restricted approach boundary: closer in, where the chance of shock is higher. Only qualified people with the required PPE, insulated tools, and permission may enter.
      • Arc flash boundary: how far back you must stay to avoid a second-degree burn if an arc flash happens. How far depends on the electrical fault and how long it lasts, so it can be bigger or smaller than the shock boundaries.

      Before work, an arc flash risk assessment studies the hazard. Warning labels on equipment then show the voltage, the arc flash boundary, the shock boundaries, and the level of protection needed.

      People make mistakes, so plan for them

      The module's idea is simple: all people make mistakes, and situations that cause mistakes can be predicted and prevented. Situations that raise the odds of a mistake are called error precursors. Four groups:

      • Task demands: heavy workload, rushing, boring or repeated work.
      • Work environment: distractions, interruptions, surprises.
      • Individual capabilities: a new task, poor communication, a poor attitude.
      • Human nature: stress, habits, assumptions, overconfidence, shortcuts.

      Tools that prevent mistakes:

      • Pre-job briefing: everyone learns their role before the task starts.
      • Job-site review: find hazards and barriers early. Post-job review: learn what to do better next time.
      • STAR self-check: Stop, Think, Act, Review. Pause and say out loud what you are about to do before you do something you cannot undo.
      • Three-way communication: the sender gives a message, the receiver repeats it back, and the sender confirms it.
      • Stop when unsure. Phrases like "I think" and "I'm pretty sure" are warning signs.
      • Flagging and blocking: mark the right equipment and physically block access to the wrong ones.

        Module Section 4 of 4

        Hazards and Safety Requirements

        By the end of this section your student can name the safety rules for ladders, scaffolds, lifting, tools, confined spaces, dangerous materials and fall protection.

        A

        Read it together

        about 12 min

        Electricians get hurt by more than electricity. This section covers the other hazards on a job site, and the best protection is knowing about them before the day starts.

        Ladders

        About half of the injuries from contact with energized equipment involve ladders or scaffolds, because a shock can make a worker fall. Many contractors have a "Ladders Last" policy: they would rather use a lift, build a scaffold, or move the work to ground level.

        • Pick the right ladder for the job and inspect it first. Look for loose or missing rungs, cracks, and bent parts. If it is damaged, do not use it. Report it.
        • For electrical work, always use a ladder made of nonconductive material, like fiberglass.
        • Face the ladder and keep three-point contact: both feet and one hand, or both hands and one foot.
        • Never lean out. Keep your belt buckle between the rails. If something is out of reach, climb down and move the ladder.
        • Straight ladders: set the base about one-fourth of the working height out from the wall (the 4-to-1 rule), secure the ladder, and let the side rails extend 36 inches above the top support. It takes two people to raise an extension ladder.
        • Stepladders: open them all the way and lock the spreaders. Never stand on the top two rungs, never use one as a straight ladder, and never leave tools on top.

        The module also offers the Fireman's Rule for angle: stand with your toes against the ladder's feet, stand straight, and hold your arms out in front of you. If your palms reach the rails, the angle is right.

        Scaffolds

        • Build them on firm footing, straight and plumb.
        • Guardrails and toe boards are needed on platforms more than 10 feet up (many companies require them lower).
        • Planks must extend 6 to 12 inches past their support for platforms 10 feet or less.
        • Everyone who builds or uses a scaffold must be trained.
        • Scaffolds conduct electricity. In one case, a crew moving a scaffold touched a 12,000-volt power line, and four of five people were killed.

        Lifting, lifts and tools

        • Never walk or stand under a load, and heed warning signals around cranes and hoists.
        • Lift with your legs, not your back. Bending at the waist puts about ten times the load on your lower back. Remember B.A.C.K.: Balance, Alignment, Contract and close, Knees. Ask for help with heavy loads.
        • Use a tool only for its designed purpose. Inspect it first and take damaged tools out of service.
        • Power tools: read the manual, wear eye protection, never carry a tool by its cord, never remove guards, and use a grounded tool on a GFCI.
        • Treat nail guns and stud guns like loaded firearms.

        Confined spaces

        A confined space is hard to enter and exit, not meant for people to stay in, and may hold hazards such as bad air, electrical hazards, or the chance of being trapped. Manholes, tanks, vaults, pits and sewers are examples. Entry takes training and a permit signed by a qualified person. Air quality must be watched, a rescue plan must exist, and the permits are kept in the job journal for one year.

        Dangerous materials

        • Safety Data Sheets (SDS) tell you what a product contains, its hazards, and first aid. They must be easy to reach on the job.
        • Solvents give off fumes. Ventilate, and wear the right PPE. A paper mask does not stop vapors. Respirators need a medical evaluation and a proper fit.
        • Asbestos was used in insulation, floor tiles, and more. Breathing the fibers causes lung disease and cancer. Assume any building built before 1980 may have it. Electricians do not drill or disturb it.
        • Batteries can give off hydrogen gas, which is explosive. No smoking, no flames. Battery acid burns skin, so know where the wash station is.
        • PCBs are toxic chemicals found in some older transformers and capacitors and in the ballasts of older fluorescent lights. Do not come in contact with them. Mercury lamps must be recycled.
        • Lead paint (homes built before 1978) and silica dust (from cutting or drilling concrete and stone) have their own rules, covered in the module.

        Fall protection

        OSHA requires fall protection when working 6 feet or more above a lower level. The best choice is always to remove the hazard. If that cannot be done, protection is chosen in this order:

        • Guardrail system (top rail at 42 inches, mid-rail at 21 inches, and a 4-inch toe board).
        • Controlled access zone: barricades or other controls that keep people back from the edge.
        • Fall restraint: a short lanyard that cannot reach the edge.
        • Personal fall arrest system (PFAS): catches a worker after a fall.

        A PFAS has three parts: a full-body harness (the only acceptable type), a lanyard no longer than 6 feet, and an anchor point that can hold 5,000 pounds. Inspect all of it before every use. If someone falls, never pull them up by the lanyard. Call for rescue right away, because hanging in a harness is dangerous. Anyone who falls needs medical attention even if they feel fine.
