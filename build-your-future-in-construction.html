Why this guide exists

You do not need to work in construction to support a construction student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and think about where this path could take them.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Good to know before you start

This module is about careers, not tools, so there are no hands-on tasks. The pay, job-growth and training numbers come from the textbook and are national averages from the years it was written. Real numbers change by trade, region and year, so use the textbook's website, www.byf.org, and your student's instructor for current figures. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 4

Overview of the Construction Industry

By the end of this section your student can explain what construction is, why the industry needs more skilled workers, which myths about construction are wrong, and some of the big moments in construction history.

A

Read it together

about 25 min

Most people picture a hammer or a bulldozer when they hear "construction." The real industry is far bigger: it builds and keeps up almost everything society runs on, and it has more open jobs than people to fill them.

What is construction? (1.1.0)

At its simplest, construction means to build. As an industry, it covers homes, offices, roads, bridges, dams and industrial plants. It also covers the less obvious work of designing, managing, planning, repairing, maintaining and even demolishing those structures.

Construction is both an art and a science. A craft or trade is one of the many construction specialties, and a craft professional is a trained, skilled person who designs and builds things and follows the technical and ethical standards of the trade. Craft professionals use technical knowledge, but they also use creativity: seeing a job site or a piece of wood and knowing how to turn it into a finished product.

State of the industry (1.1.1)

  • The industry builds over $1.3 trillion in structures per year, with over 700,000 construction companies employing more than 7 million people (figures from the Associated General Contractors of America).
  • Construction added over $887 billion to the US economy in 2019, about 4 percent of everything the country produced.
  • The market is steadily growing. One reason is future spending on aging infrastructure: the transportation, water, electrical and telecommunications systems that let a country operate.

Growing demand for skilled craft professionals (1.1.2)

There are not enough skilled workers to meet today's demand or what is coming. About 80 percent of construction companies plan to hire more people in the next few years, and many struggle to fill positions.

Why the shortage?

  • Retirement. By 2030, an estimated 40 percent of today's construction workforce is expected to retire. Baby Boomers hold many leadership roles, and they will leave a big gap.
  • Fewer young people entering. More people choose a four-year degree. In 2018, 34 percent of men and 35 percent of women in the US had at least four years of college, compared with 13 percent of men and 8 percent of women in 1968. Many young people simply do not know how many construction careers exist.

Common construction myths (1.1.3)

Myth versus what the module says
MythWhat is true
People in construction are unskilledCraft professionals do detailed, specialized work learned through years of classroom and on-the-job training. Many also earn licenses, credentials or certifications.
Construction pays minimum wageA 2018 U.S. News & World Report survey found the lowest-paid 25 percent made almost double minimum wage (over $28,520 a year). The highest-paid 25 percent made almost double that. The average was over $40,000, not counting overtime or bonuses.
Construction is not for womenAbout 1.1 million women work in construction, roughly 10 percent of the workforce. Companies see this as an opportunity. BLS reports women in construction earn more than 99 cents for every dollar a man earns, compared with 81 cents across the US.
Construction does not use technologyFunding for US construction technology grew by about 320 percent in recent years. Examples: computers, mobile apps and IoT devices (share data and communicate faster), drones (survey sites, spot hazards), robots (lift and move heavy objects), simulators (practice safely) and design software.
Construction is dangerousIt has real risks, but it is safer than ever. Reasons: higher government safety standards (OSHA, formed in 1971, has helped injuries drop a lot), more safety training, better technology such as drones, and the fact that a company's safety record affects whether it wins bids.
Construction is bad for the environmentThe industry is leading on sustainability: recycling materials, installing renewable energy, and green construction. The US Green Building Council (formed 1993) created LEED, an international standard for certifying environmentally sound buildings.
Green examplesPreassembly off site (more material reuse), wind power, solar panels and water reclamation. The module's example is a Walt Disney World solar facility on 270 acres that will cut greenhouse gas emissions by more than 50,000 tons a year.

History of construction (1.2.0)

Construction began thousands of years ago, out of necessity, as people built shelters by hand. Over time people became specialists such as carpenters, bricklayers and blacksmiths, which made more complex buildings possible. Steam-powered machinery in the 19th century let people build bigger structures much faster. Computers in the Information Age changed construction even faster, making it a high-tech business.

Famous examples (1.2.1)

  • Eiffel Tower (Paris): wrought iron, late 1800s.
  • Empire State Building (New York): the world's tallest building when finished in 1931.
  • Golden Gate Bridge (San Francisco): the longest suspension bridge in the world when finished in 1937.
  • Hoover Dam (Nevada/Arizona border): built by a workforce that peaked at more than 5,000 people in 1934.
  • Burj Khalifa (Dubai): finished 2009, still the tallest artificial structure at 2,722 feet.

Important innovations (1.2.2)

  • PPE (personal protective equipment): hard hats, safety glasses, face shields, safety-toed footwear, gloves and earplugs.
  • CAD (computer-aided design): invented in the 1960s. Designs that used to be drawn by hand are sketched on a computer in 2D or 3D so others can see how a structure will look and work.
  • BIM (building information modeling): software reads building data to make a virtual picture of a facility before it is built. It tracks things like time, cost, operation and safety, so problems can be fixed before building starts.
  • VR (virtual reality): a 3D model that people can step into to see the final product and spot problems before work begins.
Did you know?President Nixon signed the Occupational Safety and Health Act in 1970, which created OSHA. The first CAD system, Sketchpad, was built in the 1960s by Ivan Sutherland.

    Module Section 2 of 4

    Benefits of a Career in Construction

    By the end of this section your student can describe how construction work helps communities and the environment, and explain the money and career benefits of a construction path.

    A

    Read it together

    about 15 min

    Construction is a career that lets you build something that lasts, earn a good income, and often start without college debt. This section covers the benefits the module highlights.

    Making a difference (2.1.0)

    The state of a society's infrastructure depends on the skills of craft professionals. They take pride in being part of something bigger than their own job, since great things take many people working together.

    Providing essential services (2.1.1)

    The light switch, the faucet, the road to school and the school building all exist because craft professionals built and maintain them. After hurricanes, tornados, floods and earthquakes, craft professionals often act as first responders, restoring utilities such as water and electricity and rebuilding homes. Restoring essential services quickly reduces the harm of an emergency.

    Working to help the environment (2.1.2)

    Today's workforce focuses on cutting environmental impact. Besides using different building methods, craft professionals install devices and systems that help homes and businesses save energy, reduce environmental impact and save money.

    Competitive salaries (2.2.1)

    Every other year, NCCER publishes a craft salary survey of the national average of base salaries, drawn from more than 350,000 industrial and commercial construction employees. For the crafts surveyed, salaries run from $47,000 to $92,000. These numbers leave out overtime, bonuses and other incentives, so real earnings can be higher.

    Starting out debt-free (2.2.2)

    • College tuition debt is the second-highest source of debt in the US, behind only mortgage debt. Student debt is over $1.5 trillion.
    • Some people choose a bachelor's degree, especially for careers like civil engineer, project manager or architect, but most construction careers do not require one. Seven out of every ten jobs in the US economy need less than a four-year degree.
    • Craft training is available through technical schools, community colleges, associations and contractor programs. It is shorter and costs far less per year than a university.
    • Craft training follows an Earn as You Learn model: trainees learn on the job and in class, are paid for field work, and get raises as skills grow. Many companies also pay extra education costs.
    • Finishing a program brings immediate marketability. By contrast, a New York Federal Reserve study found only 27 percent of college graduates work in a field related to their major.

    Career advancement and mobility (2.2.3)

    Construction offers clear paths to move up. Many people start by learning to use tools, then earn licenses and certifications to get promoted. Leadership skills like communication, teamwork and decision-making help people move from trainee to craft professional to management, and some start their own businesses. Because skills are in demand in many places, craft professionals can travel or relocate, even sometimes to other countries.

    Success storyBoyd Worsham started as a carpenter's helper out of high school in 1980. He rose to journeyman, foreman, superintendent and vice president of construction support at Haskell, which paid for his MBA. He became president and CEO of NCCER in 2020.

      Construction Craft Careers

      By the end of this section your student can name the four construction sectors, the main roles on a project team, and describe several popular crafts, what they pay, and what they require.

      A

      Read it together

      about 25 min

      The module says construction offers more than 45 crafts, plus management roles. This section shows how projects are organized and what several careers look like.

      Construction industry sectors (3.1.0)

      The four sectors
      SectorWhat it includes
      ResidentialSingle-family homes, multi-family homes such as apartment buildings, public housing, and garages and sheds
      CommercialSchools, government buildings, medical facilities, hotels, stadiums, shopping centers and large office buildings
      IndustrialManufacturing plants, oil refineries, electrical generating plants, chemical processing plants and large mills
      Heavy civil / infrastructureOften called horizontal construction: bridges, roadways, airports, tunnels and dams

      Who does what on a project (3.1.0)

      • The project owner starts the project and usually pays for it.
      • The contractor oversees many parts of the project: resources, budget, code compliance, quality and materials. It works directly with the owner or the owner's representative.
      • The contractor hires subcontractors / specialty contractors, who employ craft professionals with specialized skills such as HVAC, electrical or plumbing.
      • The crew leader (also called the foreman) manages a crew of craft professionals and is responsible for correct, on-time, safe work.
      • The site superintendent manages day-to-day activity on the site and supervises the subcontractors' work. It can be a former crew leader who has completed supervisory training, or a graduate of a university construction management program.
      • The project manager plans, schedules and runs projects on time and within budget, and is not always on site. They usually hold a university degree.

      Advancement depends on skill level, a willingness to keep growing, and the quality of your work. Most craft careers need only a high school diploma or equivalent to start.

      Carpenter (3.2.1)

      Carpenters build, repair and install building frameworks and structures from wood and other materials. A rough carpenter does framing and formwork. A finish carpenter adds the finishing touches: trim, crown molding, bookshelves, baseboards and wooden stairways. Useful skills: physical fitness, balance, hand-eye coordination, attention to detail, blueprint reading, basic math and problem solving. Some carpenters complete an apprenticeship, and NCCER offers a four-year carpentry course that can cover the classroom requirement.

      Electrician (3.2.2)

      Electricians install and maintain the electrical and power systems of homes, businesses and factories. In large factories they usually do more complex maintenance, such as repairing motors, transformers, generators and electronic controllers. A related career, the limited-energy integrator or electronic systems technician, installs low-voltage systems such as audio and video, security, fire protection, networks, CCTV and access control. Useful skills: physical fitness, good color vision (to tell wires apart), detail focus, good math, problem solving, customer service and skilled hands.

      LicensingRequirements vary by state. In most states electricians must be licensed by passing a test on national, state and local codes. They usually take continuing education on safety, code changes and products to keep the license. Specialty areas often need extra training.

      Welder (3.2.3)

      Welders join metals with a high-intensity electric arc at 6,000°F to 10,000°F and also cut steel. Specializations include pipe welder, stainless steel welder, weld inspector, underwater welder and robotic welder. Some jobs need a general certification such as the American Welding Society's Certified Welder credential, and underwater welders also need a diver's certification.

      Pipefitter (3.2.4)

      Pipefitters install, assemble, fabricate, maintain and repair mechanical piping systems for water, gases, liquid chemicals, solids and fuels in places like chemical plants, refineries and paper mills. They need to understand blueprints, basic math (especially geometry), welding, pipe bending, fittings and valves. Most complete a four-year craft training or apprenticeship program, and several states require a license for gas lines.

      Heavy equipment operator (3.2.5)

      These operators drive and control machines such as backhoes, bulldozers, dump trucks, forklifts and excavators to clear, grade, lift and move materials. Most specialize in one or more types. OSHA requires forklift (powered industrial truck) operators to be licensed or certified, and hauling equipment between sites may require a commercial driver's license.

      Crane operator (3.2.6)

      Crane operators use joysticks, levers and pedals to hoist heavy materials. They regularly use trigonometry and physics to calculate the maximum load for a crane's setup. Tower crane operators control cranes that grow taller as the building rises. OSHA requires crane operators to be certified or licensed (written exams and physical tests).

      Ironworker (3.2.7)

      Ironworkers place and install steel beams, columns and other materials to build and reinforce structures. Despite the name, they mostly work with steel. They need physical fitness, agility, balance, comfort at heights and mechanical, math and problem-solving skills. A welding certification improves job prospects.

      HVAC technician (3.2.8)

      HVAC technicians install, maintain and repair heating, ventilation and air conditioning systems, including motors, pumps, fans, thermostats and computerized controls. Some states require a license, or certification for working with certain chemicals.

      Project manager (3.2.9)

      Project managers plan, coordinate, budget and supervise projects from start to finish. They prepare cost estimates, explain contracts, manage people, solve project problems and make sure safety codes are followed. Unlike most construction careers, they typically need a bachelor's degree, and some states require a license. Skills: communication, sound decisions, business sense, time management, customer service and leadership.

      More careers (3.2.10)

      NCCER trains for more than 45 crafts. NCCER's Build Your Future website, www.byf.org, has salaries by craft, career paths and labor demand by state.

        Module Section 4 of 4

        Starting Your Construction Career

        By the end of this section your student can explain the training paths into construction: career and technical education, craft training and apprenticeships, community college and university.

        A

        Read it together

        about 15 min

        There is more than one way in. The module lists technical school, apprenticeship programs, community college or university, and industry training programs. Most craft professionals learn through a mix of technical school, on-the-job learning, craft training and apprenticeships.

        Career and technical education (4.1.0)

        CTE classes are offered in high schools and technical education centers (some middle schools offer introductory classes too). Students explore the construction crafts, and some build hands-on skills in competitions such as SkillsUSA. A school guidance counselor can explain what is offered. CTE emphasizes:

        • Real-life application. Practicing skills improves retention, and linking lessons to a future career keeps students committed.
        • Nontechnical skills. Communication, time management, attention to detail, critical thinking and a customer service attitude make a student more employable.
        • Career exploration. Learning from teachers with industry experience helps students find their strengths and set goals.

        After CTE, students usually have the qualifications to move on to an industry apprenticeship, community college or a university degree.

        Success storyAt The Villages Charter School in Florida, a senior construction class helps build a home for Habitat for Humanity each year, practicing blueprint reading and scheduling. Their teacher's goals: prepare them, get them in the industry, and set them up for success.

        Craft training and apprenticeships (4.2.0)

        • Students learn from experienced professionals while being paid. Companies often train in-house or pay for classes at a local construction association or community college.
        • Learning is split about 20 percent classroom and 80 percent on the job.
        • It usually takes two to four years and requires at least 2,000 hours of on-the-job learning and 144 hours of classroom instruction each year.
        • The US Department of Labor lists five components: business involvement, structured on-the-job learning, related instruction, rewards for skill gains, and nationally recognized credentials.
        • Benefits: full-time paid work, real experience, little or no education debt, and valuable industry connections through mentors.

        Ways to find a program: the national apprenticeship database at www.apprenticeship.gov; local associations such as ABC and AGC; online job boards and social media; local employers; and national construction organizations' websites.

        Community colleges and universities (4.3.0)

        • Community college (4.3.1): usually two-year programs and associate degrees. Companies often send trainees to a community college for the classroom part. An associate degree brings college credit plus industry credentials. Next steps: an industry apprenticeship, an entry-level job or a university degree.
        • Four-year university (4.3.2): bachelor's degrees in areas such as design, construction management, estimating, human resources, architecture, engineering and safety. A bachelor's degree typically needs at least 120 hours of credit work. A degree can speed up moving up the ladder and open higher-paying decision-making roles.
        • The catch: tuition is expensive, it is hard to work full time while in class, and a degree does not guarantee a specific construction job. Without on-the-job experience, you may still start at an entry-level position.
