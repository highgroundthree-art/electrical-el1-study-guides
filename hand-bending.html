Module Section 1 of 2

Selecting and Using Hand Bending Equipment

By the end of this section your student can describe how a hand bender and a hickey are used, work out where to mark a 90° bend, explain gain, and describe offsets, parallel offsets and saddle bends.

A

Read it together

about 25 min

Bending conduit is a skill built on a few simple ideas: know your bender, use a little geometry, and measure before you bend. This section covers the hand bender and the hickey, the geometry behind a bend, 90° bends, back-to-back bends, offsets, parallel offsets, and saddles.

The hand bender (1.0.0)

Hand benders are handy on the job because they are portable and need no electrical power. Their shape supports the walls of the conduit while it is bent. They are used for smaller conduit, trade sizes 1/2 inch to 1 1/4 inch (Metric Designator MD 16 to MD 35). Most hand benders are sized for both rigid conduit and electrical metallic tubing (EMT), because the matching sizes have nearly the same outside diameter. One bender can bend either 3/4 inch EMT (MD 21) or 1/2 inch rigid conduit (MD 16). The next larger bender bends either 1 inch EMT (MD 27) or 3/4 inch rigid conduit (MD 21).

The first step toward a good bend is getting to know your bender. The manufacturer's papers usually give starting points, the distance between offset bends, and other values for that bender. There is no substitute for reading them. It makes the job go faster and the bends come out better.

Key ideaKeep the conduit on a stable, firm, flat surface for the whole bend. Use one foot and both hands, with constant foot pressure and force on the handle, so the bend comes out uniform. If the conduit rises up, or you bend on soft ground, the conduit can be distorted outside the bender. Keep a firm grip on the handle so it cannot slip and hurt you.

A hickey is not a hand bender. It works very differently, and it is used for RMC and IMC only. With a hickey you form the bend and its radius yourself, and it gives the conduit walls very little support, so take care not to flatten or kink the conduit. A hickey is a segment bending tool: make a small bend of about 10°, move the hickey to a new spot, make another small bend, and keep going until the bend is done. Use a hickey for conduit stub-ups in slabs and decks. Kinks come from bending too small a radius with a hickey.

Make bends by the NEC rules for the conduit type: Article 342 (IMC), 344 (RMC), 352 (rigid PVC) or 358 (EMT).

Bending PVC

PVC is bent with a heating unit, either electric or liquid propane (LP). Turn the PVC regularly in the heater so it heats evenly. Take it out, bend it by hand, and then a damp sponge or cloth is often used so it sets up faster. For PVC 2 inch (MD 53) or larger there is a risk of wrinkling or flattening the bend. A plug set solves that: put a plug in each end and use a hand pump to pressurize the conduit to about 3 to 5 psi before heat is applied. The plugs must stay in until the pipe is cool and set.

SafetyThe heating unit case can get very hot and cause burns. Make sure it is cool before you store it, because a hot unit is a fire hazard. If you use an LP unit, keep a fire extinguisher nearby.

The geometry behind a bend (1.1.0)

A right triangle is any triangle with a 90° angle. The side opposite the 90° angle is the hypotenuse. The side it sits on is the base, and the upright side is the height. When you make an offset, the offset itself forms the hypotenuse of a right triangle.

A circle is a closed curve whose points are all the same distance from the center. That distance is the radius. The distance all the way across through the center is the diameter, which is always twice the radius. The distance around is the circumference. A circle divides into four equal quadrants of 90°, for a total of 360°. A 90° bend uses one quadrant, or 1/4 of a circle. Concentric circles share a center but have different radii, and that is the idea behind concentric bends: every bend is 90°, they share a center point, and each has a different radius.

FormulaWhat it gives you
C = π × D (π = 3.14)Circumference from the diameter
C = 2πRCircumference from the radius
Length of arc = 0.25 × 2πR = 1.57RThe arc of a 90° quadrant: 1/4 of the circle, or 1.57 times the radius

Making a 90° bend (1.2.0)

The 90° stub bend is probably the most basic bend of all. Before you start you need two measurements: the desired rise (the height of the stub-up) and the take-up of the bender (the amount of conduit the bender uses up to form the bend). Take-up distances are usually in the manufacturer's manual. Typical ones are in Table 1.

  1. Subtract the take-up from the stub-up height and mark that distance on the conduit, all the way around, measured from the end. That mark is where the bend starts. In the book's Figure 8, 1/2 inch EMT with an 18 inch stub-up and a 5 inch take-up is marked at 13 inches.
  2. Line up the bender. Match the mark on the conduit with the starting mark (often an arrow) on the bender. Pay attention to which side of the mark the bender sits. The wrong side is a gross error.
  3. Bend. Hold the conduit steady with one foot, heel on the floor for balance. Put constant pressure on the bender with the other foot. Keep the handle perpendicular to the floor and as far up as possible for the most leverage. Bend in one smooth motion, pulling as steadily as you can, and avoid overstretching.
  4. Check the bend. With the back of the bend on the floor, measure to the end of the stub-up to see that it is the right length. Check the 90° with a square, a wall and floor corner, or a torpedo level.

If you overbend a little past the angle you want, the bender can bend it back. That procedure makes a one-shot bend, a single bend that forms the whole conduit bend. A segment bend is a bend formed by a series of small bends of a few degrees each. Each single bend in it is a shot. Segment or sweep bends must follow the NEC.

Gain (1.2.1)

Because conduit bends in a curve and not at a sharp corner, the conduit needed is a little shorter than the two straight distances added together. The distance saved by the arc of a 90° bend is the gain. Knowing the gain lets you precut, ream, and prethread both ends of the conduit before you bend it, which is easier while it is still straight.

Key ideaDeveloped length = (A + B) − gain. The developed length is the actual length of the conduit that will be bent.

Back-to-back bends (1.2.2)

A back-to-back bend is two 90° bends on one piece of conduit with a straight section between them. Make the first bend (X) the usual way. For the second, measure the required distance between the bends from the back of the first bend (distance L). Turn the bender around on the conduit, line up the bender's back-to-back mark at point Y, and complete the second bend. Outside measurements from X to Y are used.

Offsets (1.3.0)

An offset is two equal bends of less than 90°, a set distance apart, that let conduit pass over beams and other conduit or enter meter cabinets and boxes. Offsets trade space for effort: the bigger the angle, the harder the wire is to pull. Use the shallowest angle that still clears the obstruction and fits the space.

An offset uses up some conduit length. If the offset goes into the area, allow for that shrinkage. If the offset angle goes away from the obstruction, you can ignore it. Table 2 gives the multiplier and the shrinkage per inch of rise.

Table 2. Shrinkage calculation
Offset angleMultiplierShrinkage (per inch of rise)

Key ideaDistance between bends = depth of offset × multiplier. Example from the module's exercises: a 30° offset with a 6 inch depth has 6 × 2.0 = 12 inches between bends and 6 × 1/4 = 1 1/2 inches of shrink.

The multipliers are the cosecant (CSC) of the offset angle. You find one by dividing the hypotenuse of the offset triangle by the depth of the offset. Table 3 in the module lists the distance between bends and the shrinkage for the common angles at depths of 2 to 10 inches, and the manufacturer's bender papers list them too. When you bend with the take-up method, put the bender on the conduit facing the end you measured from, and mark all the way around the conduit with a narrow pencil or marker line. That is called girdling.

Parallel offsets (1.3.1)

When several pieces of conduit bend around the same obstruction, the offsets are parallel. Because the bends sit along a common radius, you must adjust each piece or the ends come out uneven. Find the center of the first bend of the inside conduit. Move each next conduit's bend start farther from the end by:

Key ideaAmount added = center-to-center spacing × tangent (TAN) of 1/2 the offset angle.

Example from the book: a 30° offset, 3 inch center-to-center spacing, and the first pipe's bend starting at 12 inches. The second pipe starts at 12" + (3" × TAN 15°) = 12" + (3" × 0.2679) = 12.8037", about 12 13/16". The outermost pipe starts at 12 13/16" + 13/16" = 13 5/8". Check a vertical rise for plumb with a torpedo level.

Matching 90° bends in parallel rigid runs: put the smaller conduit in the larger shoe so both bends have the same radius. This only works on rigid conduit. On EMT it flattens the pipe. With more than two parallel runs you may need concentric bends.

Saddle bends (1.3.2)

A saddle bend takes conduit around an obstruction such as a pipe. Making a saddle shortens the center of the saddle by 3/16 inch for every inch of saddle depth (Table 4). A 2 inch pipe, for example, shortens the conduit 3/8 inch on each side, 3/4 inch in all. A 4 inch obstruction shortens the center by 3/16" × 4 = 3/4".

  1. Find center mark A. Use the size of the obstruction to work out the shrink, and add it to the measurement from the conduit end to the obstruction's centerline. If that measurement is 15 inches and the pipe is 2 inches, A is at 15 3/8".
  2. Find marks B and C. Measure 2 1/2 inches for every 1 inch of saddle depth from A. For a 2 inch depth, B is 5 inches before A and C is 5 inches after A.
  3. Bend. Make a 45° bend at A and a 22 1/2° bend at B and at C. Check the manufacturer's specifications.

Four-bend saddles and planning your bends (1.3.3)

The more bends between pull points, the harder the wire is to pull. Plan ahead, avoid sharp bends, and use as few bends as you can. The NEC allows a total of 360° of bends in a single run between pull points, which is less than it sounds. Two 90° room corners plus two 45° offsets at a panelboard and a junction box use it all.

A four-bend saddle is two offsets made back-to-back, and all four bends must lie on exactly the same plane. Extra time laying it out saves scrapping the piece. The steps: determine the height of the offset, determine the spacing for the first offset and mark it, bend the first offset, mark the start of the second offset at the trailing edge of the obstruction, mark its spacing, and bend the second offset.

Book example (1/2 inch EMT, MD 16; box 6 inches high and 8 inches wide; 36 inches to the obstruction; two 30° offsets):

StepWorkResult
Start of first bend36" − (6" × 2.0) + 1 1/2"25 1/2"
Total length of first offset25 1/2" + 12" + 1 1/2"39"
Start of second offset8" + 39"47"
Spacing of second offsetSame rise and angle as the first12"
Practice tipBend a piece of No. 10 or No. 12 solid wire to look like the bends you need. It shows you how to bend the conduit and helps you spot problems early. Floor tiles also make a handy grid to judge distances and angles on 45° offsets.

    Cutting, Reaming, and Threading Conduit

    By the end of this section your student can describe how to cut conduit with a hacksaw or pipe cutter, ream it, thread RMC and IMC, and cut and join PVC.

    A

    Read it together

    about 15 min

    Conduit comes in standard 10 foot (3 m) lengths (RMC, IMC and EMT), so on the job it is cut to fit. This section covers cutting with a hacksaw or pipe cutter, reaming the cut edge, threading, and cutting and joining PVC.

    Cutting with a hacksaw (2.1.0)

    Conduit is normally cut with a hacksaw. Always wear safety goggles and gloves, because the edges are sharp and cutting throws off metal debris that can hurt your eyes.

    1. Check the blade. A blade with 18, 24 or 32 teeth per inch is recommended. Use a higher tooth count for EMT (32) and a lower tooth count for rigid conduit and IMC. When you replace a blade, point the teeth toward the front of the saw.
    2. Secure the conduit in a pipe vise.
    3. Cut. Rest the middle of the blade on the mark, with the end of the blade pointing slightly down and the handle slightly up. Push forward gently to start the cut, then make even strokes until it is done. Use gentle strokes at the end to avoid bruising your knuckles on the newly cut pipe.
    4. Check the cut. The end should be straight and smooth (Figure 23 shows good and bad cuts). Then ream it.

    Cutting with a pipe cutter (2.2.0)

    A pipe cutter can also cut RMC and IMC. Wear safety goggles and gloves.

    1. Secure the conduit in a pipe vise and mark the cut.
    2. Open the cutter and put the cutter wheel on the mark.
    3. Tighten the cutter with the screw handle. Do not overtighten it. That can break the cutter wheel and distort the conduit wall.
    4. Rotate the cutter to start the cut. The rotation must pull the cutting blade into the conduit (Figure 24).
    5. Tighten the handle 1/4 turn for each full turn around the conduit, without overtightening.
    6. Add a few drops of cutting oil to the groove and keep cutting. Avoid skin contact with the oil.
    7. When the cut is almost done, stop and snap the conduit to finish it. That reduces the ridge that can form inside.
    8. Clean the conduit and cutter with a shop towel, then ream the conduit.
    Shop tips from the bookTo hold conduit while cutting, put it in the bender head and brace your foot against the bender. Wrap a piece of tape around the conduit as a guide for a straight cut line.

    Reaming (2.3.0)

    A cut leaves a sharp inside edge that can damage wire insulation as the wire is pulled through. A reamer smooths that edge. Wear goggles and gloves.

    1. Put the conduit in a pipe vise.
    2. Put the reamer tip into the conduit.
    3. Apply light forward pressure and start turning the reamer, using a downward motion (Figure 26). Turning it the wrong way can damage the reamer. It should bite as soon as you apply the proper pressure.
    4. Pull the reamer back while still turning it, check your progress, and reinsert it. Stop when every burr is gone.
    Key ideaIf you do not have a conduit reamer, use a half-round file (the tang needs a handle). For EMT you may also use the nose of diagonal cutters or a small hand reamer. A special EMT reaming tool slips over a square-shank screwdriver and is turned back and forth to deburr.

    Threading (2.4.0)

    Only RMC and IMC have walls thick enough to thread. A die cuts the threads. Conduit dies cut a taper of 3/4 inch per foot, with 8 to 18 threads per inch depending on the conduit size. A thread gauge measures the threads per inch. The die sits in a die head, and the die head works with a hand-operated ratchet threader (Figure 27) or a portable power drive. Wear goggles and gloves.

    1. Put the conduit in a pipe vise fastened to a strong surface, with supports if needed.
    2. Pick the correct die and head. Inspect the die for damage such as broken teeth. Never use a damaged die.
    3. Seat the die in the head, with the right die in the correctly numbered slot.
    4. Find the correct thread length for the conduit size, matching the manufacturer's thread length.
    5. Lubricate the die with cutting oil at the start and all through the threading. Avoid skin contact with the oil.
    6. Cut the threads. The conduit must enter the tapered side of the die. Apply pressure and turn the head, backing off each quarter-turn to clear chips.
    7. Remove the die when the cut is right. Threads should be cut only to the length of the die. Overcutting leaves threads exposed to corrosion.
    8. Inspect the threads. They should be clean, sharp and properly made. A finished end allows a wrench-tight fit with one or two threads showing.
    After threadingReam the conduit again to remove burrs and edges. Swab the cutting oil from the inside and outside of the conduit. Put a drip pan or sandbox under the threader to catch drips and shavings. Start with a square cut, or the conduit will not thread properly, and oil the threader often for smoother work.

    Threading machines hold and rotate the conduit while the die is fed onto it. They are often used on larger conduit and where there is a lot of threading. Secure the machine's legs properly and follow the manufacturer's instructions.

    Cutting and joining PVC (2.5.0)

    PVC is easy to cut with a handsaw. For 2 inch (MD 53) and larger, use a miter box or similar device to keep the cut square. Use a pocket knife to deburr the ends. For smaller PVC, up to 1 1/2 inch (MD 41), use a PVC cutter. In an awkward spot, a nylon string can cut PVC in place, but a PVC cutter is best for the smaller sizes.

    To join PVC sections, or to attach PVC to plastic boxes:

    1. Wipe all contact surfaces clean and dry.
    2. Brush or spray a coat of cement on both pieces being joined.
    3. Press the conduit and fitting together and rotate about a half-turn to spread the cement evenly.
    SafetyPVC solvents and cements are hazardous. Wear gloves and eye protection, follow the product instructions, and work in a well-ventilated area. Cement the PVC quickly and use the cement the manufacturer recommends. In the field, forming PVC takes a special tool called a hot box or other special methods. PVC may not be threaded for electrical work.
