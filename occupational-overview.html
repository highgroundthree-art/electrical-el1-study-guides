Why this guide exists

You do not need to be an electrician to support an electrical student. Your job is to help your student explain what they are learning, put it in their own words, and know when safety means stop and ask a qualified person.

If your student can read it and explain it, they are learning it. That is the whole idea.

How to use each section

Plan about 15 to 25 minutes per section. The student does most of the talking. You do not need the answers ahead of time.

  1. Read it together. Plain-English version of the module section.
  2. Study review points. The must-know facts. Tick each one when your student can say it back.
  3. Trade-term cards. Tap a card to flip it.
  4. Ticket out the door. A quick recall check, just for practice.
  5. Talk it over. Questions and a real-world scenario.
  6. Practice questions. Each one comes twice: first in plain English, then in the textbook's words, so your student learns to recognize both.

Important safety note

This guide is for learning and talking. It is not permission to do electrical work. Never use this guide in place of instructor supervision, employer procedures, required training, or properly rated equipment. No student names or grades appear here, and nothing you tick or tap on this page is saved or sent anywhere.

Module Section 1 of 3

Identifying Sectors and Career Options in the Electrical Industry

By the end of this section your student can describe what goes into residential, commercial and industrial wiring, and name the main career paths in the electrical trade and what each one does.

A

Read it together

about 25 min

Most people only think about electricity when the power goes out. It takes a lot of skilled people to produce it, deliver it, install it and keep it running. This section covers the three main areas of electrical work and the many career paths inside the trade.

Three areas of electrical work (1.0.0)

The electrical field splits into three broad areas: residential (homes), commercial (offices, stores and similar buildings) and industrial (plants and factories). Every trainee eventually picks an area. Some electricians settle into homes or businesses. Others like big industrial sites, such as petrochemical plants, where they install or maintain large systems with motors and controls. Some go on to start their own contracting company or to teach the trade.

Residential wiring systems (1.1.0)

A home's electrical system includes an electrical supply, the electrical service, nonmetallic-sheathed cable, nail-on device boxes, panelboards and fixtures. House wiring happens in phases: rough-in, trim-out, testing and troubleshooting.

The two main wiring stages
StageWhenWhat gets installed
Rough-inBeginningPanelboard, raceway system, wiring and boxes
Trim-outFinalDevices and fixtures, installed and terminated (connected)

The book's Figure 1 shows six main parts of a home system: a pad-mounted transformer, the residential electrical service, a nail-on device box, nonmetallic-sheathed cable, an interior panel (subpanel), and a luminaire (lighting fixture).

Key ideaThe interior panel enclosure usually goes in, and is partly connected, during rough-in. Devices and fixtures are connected during trim-out.

Commercial wiring systems (1.2.0)

Commercial buildings, such as offices and stores, have many of the same parts as homes. One big difference: in commercial and industrial work, the conductors usually run inside metal raceways, so the electricians need to be skilled at bending conduit. A well-trained electrician can install a metal raceway system with little or no wasted conduit. A beginner may go through several pieces before getting a bend right.

The book's Figure 2 lists parts of a commercial system: a pad-mounted commercial transformer, the commercial electrical service, a conduit system, a fire alarm system, and office and outdoor lighting.

Industrial wiring systems (1.3.0)

Many industrial sites handle hazardous materials, so their electrical systems must follow strict National Electrical Code (NEC) requirements. Homes and businesses have strict code rules too.

In potentially explosive areas, called classified locations, conduit must be sealed against outside vapors and gases. Anything that could spark or arc must sit inside a special enclosure so it cannot ignite those vapors.

Industrial electricians usually fall into two groups: installers and maintenance personnel. At a large plant, contract electricians (who work for a contractor the plant hired) install the conduit, conductors, motors and equipment. The finished system is then handed over to maintenance electricians who work directly for the plant (on-site personnel). They keep it running once it is energized. At a smaller plant, the same electricians may do both jobs.

The book's Figure 3 shows industrial equipment: distribution switchgear, a rigid metal conduit (RMC) system, and a motor control center.

Career paths in the electrical industry (1.4.0)

Every electrical system, here and around the world, has to be installed and maintained by someone qualified. Industry growth, new technology, upgrades to older equipment, and workers retiring all open jobs for trained electricians.

Real-world example from the bookIn 2017, electricians replaced the "Crown of Lights" on the 99th floor of Chicago's John Hancock Center. The old crown used 552 fluorescent tubes drawing 57,408 watts. The new LED fixtures use only 9,400 watts, can show hundreds of color presets from a touchscreen, and did away with $80,000 a year in maintenance for changing colored tube covers.

Residential electrician (1.4.1)

Provides the complete electrical system in a home: service entrance equipment, branch circuit conductors, device boxes, panel enclosures, circuit breakers, lighting, smoke detectors and other fixtures. Residential electricians work for residential electrical contractors, or as independent contractors who pay their own taxes and insurance, bring their own equipment, and are often paid by piece work (a fixed price per house). They frequently wire tract homes (subdivisions). With the right license, a residential electrician can run their own contracting business.

Commercial electrician (1.4.2)

Installs power, lighting and control wiring in apartment buildings, stores, offices, service stations and hospitals. Commercial electricians are often employed by electrical contractors who work as subcontractors for a general contractor. Many contractors do both residential and commercial work but hire specialists for each.

Service electrician (1.4.3)

Works in the residential or light commercial market and mostly answers calls for repairs or upgrades to existing systems. A service electrician needs a strong understanding of electrical theory in order to troubleshoot. They also add wiring, new receptacles and new lighting in existing buildings or outdoors, and sometimes change out a building's electrical service. Unlike construction electricians, they usually do not work on new construction. Many run their business as a sole proprietorship (a one-owner business).

Remodeling contractor (1.4.4)

Focuses on updating electrical systems in existing homes and businesses. Jobs range from simple installs to rewiring an entire older building. Remodeling work can include new lighting, ceiling fans, switches or dimmers, smart home devices, pool or hot tub wiring, and panelboard changes.

Industrial electrician (1.4.5)

Needs extra training because of all the specialty equipment in industrial sites. Industrial electricians must understand the code rules for hazardous locations, including the different classes and divisions. They also work with three-phase power, motors and motor controls, may install conduit and wiring for process control instrumentation, and must troubleshoot systems that fail during testing. They are usually employed by large corporations or contractors that build industrial facilities.

Electrical maintenance technician (1.4.6)

Works in commercial and industrial facilities, usually directly for the owner or management. At big facilities they are part of a maintenance group led by a maintenance manager or supervisor.

In industrial plants, maintenance electricians often handle both electrical and instrumentation systems and are called E&I technicians (electrical and instrumentation). Instrumentation is its own craft and takes training beyond this course. Most maintenance electricians are employees of the facility, but some contract maintenance crews work side by side with plant staff.

Key ideaEvery maintenance electrician must know the magnetic motor starter. They fail often in industrial settings, so maintenance electricians must be able to take them apart, put them back together, troubleshoot them and repair them.

Utility substation electrician (1.4.7)

Trained to build, maintain and operate substations so the public gets reliable power. They work on transmission and distribution substations, including installing and starting up (commissioning) the equipment that delivers power. Their work includes monitoring, troubleshooting, repairing and testing circuit breakers, transformers, voltage regulators, reclosers and other equipment. They also install grounding systems, cable tray, conduit and other raceways, and they must know how to size, select and install substation cable, from control cable up to medium-voltage cable. They work for utility companies, city (municipal) power providers and some independent power providers.

Maritime electrician (1.4.8)

Shipbuilders and ship repair yards are big employers of electricians. Maritime electricians do not follow the NEC. They follow standards from the American Bureau of Shipping (ABS), the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), and US Navy standards.

On a vessel they work on steering systems, alarm and monitoring systems, and power distribution. They install batteries and generators and work on motors, pumps and navigation systems.

    Training and Apprenticeship Process for Electricians

    By the end of this section your student can list the Department of Labor (DOL) requirements for apprenticeship and describe the kinds of training in the electrical field, from on-the-job learning to licensing.

    A

    Read it together

    about 15 min

    Skilled electricians are in high demand, and the pay is near the top of all construction jobs. An apprenticeship lets you earn a paycheck while you learn the trade. This section covers how apprenticeships work, what they require, and what comes after.

    Why the trade needs you (2.0.0)

    New homes, schools, offices, malls, airports and plants are built every day, and the US Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that this creates a big demand for skilled workers every year. Electrical work is demanding but rewarding, with plenty of variety: one day you may be indoors installing boxes or hooking up motor controllers, the next you may be outside on a ladder or running conduit on a pipe rack high in the air. Electricity is needed everywhere, so electricians are needed everywhere.

    DOL apprenticeship standards (2.1.0)

    Apprenticeship goes back thousands of years and still works the same way: you learn from people who have mastered the craft, and you learn by doing. Some theory is taught in the classroom, always tied to the skill you are learning.

    The US Department of Labor (DOL) Office of Apprenticeship sets the minimum standards for apprenticeship programs across the country. Programs combine required classroom instruction with on-the-job learning (OJL). During OJL you are paid to learn, which is a big advantage over a college student paying tuition.

    DOL apprenticeship numbers
    RequirementPer year (minimum)Typical full program
    Classroom instruction144 hours576 hours (144 × 4 years)
    On-the-job learning (OJL)2,000 hours8,000 hours (2,000 × 4 years)

    After completing a typical program, apprentices receive journeyman certificates from registered apprenticeship programs. OJL must be supervised by a journeyman or master electrician.

    NCCER builds its curriculum on the DOL minimums. The NCCER electrical program is a four-year program and is competency-based: trainees must show the instructor they have the knowledge and skills to safely do the hands-on tasks in each module. When a certified instructor signs off, the record goes into NCCER's Registry, which can confirm a worker's training as they move from state to state or company to company. Whatever program you join, work for an employer or sponsor that supports nationally standardized training with credentials.

    Types of training (2.2.0)

    Every apprenticeship includes on-the-job learning. Training can start after high school graduation, or before it through a youth apprenticeship program. After training, electricians may sit for one or more licensing exams.

    On-the-job learning (2.2.1)

    OJL is broken into specific tasks, each with a required number of hours. The total is traditionally 8,000 hours, or four years. A competency-based program may let you shorten this by testing out of tasks with performance exams. In a traditional program you earn OJL in steps of 2,000 hours a year.

    Apprentices log all work time (the book's Figure 10 is a monthly record page) and turn it in to the apprenticeship committee. After each 1,000 hours of related work, an apprentice typically gets a pay raise.

    Helpful background: a high school or technical school education, and classes in shop, mechanical drawing and general math. Good hand skills, physical condition and quick reflexes matter, along with solving problems quickly and working well with others. Strong safety awareness is a must.

    What the apprenticeship committee may ask for
    • Aptitude test results: the General Aptitude Test Battery (GATB Form Test), usually given by the local Employment Security Commission
    • Proof of education (school transcripts sent to the committee)
    • Letters of reference from past employers and friends
    • Proof of age
    • For veterans, a copy of Form DD214
    • A record of construction-related technical or pre-apprenticeship training

    Some companies also have physical requirements. In general, apprentices must wear proper safety gear and personal protective equipment (PPE), buy and maintain the tools the contractor requires, turn in a monthly OJL report, tell the committee about any change in employment, and attend classes and follow class rules such as attendance.

    Informal on-the-job training from an employer is usually less thorough than a formal apprenticeship. A small contractor may train you in one area, while a large company may train you in several.

    Youth Apprenticeship Program (2.2.2)

    Students can start apprenticeship training while still in high school. Students who enter in eleventh grade can finish up to two years of the four-year NCCER program by graduation. Working with local employers, the program lets students work in the trade and earn money while in school. After graduation they can start at a higher level, with more pay, than someone just beginning. Official transcripts let students continue the program wherever it is offered, and some colleges may accept the credits.

    Licensing (2.2.3)

    After training, most electricians take a state or local licensing exam. A license shows you are qualified to install or maintain electrical systems, and it lets you work independently and earn more. A licensed electrician is responsible and liable for their own work, and for the work of anyone working for them.

    Key ideaLicensing requirements vary from state to state and may vary by city. Contact your local building department to find out the rules where you live. Many places also require continuing education to renew a license.

      Module Section 3 of 3

      Employee and Employer Responsibilities

      By the end of this section your student can identify what an employee owes the job (professionalism, honesty, showing up, working safely) and what an employer owes its workers under OSHA and other laws.

      A

      Read it together

      about 25 min

      Safe, cost-effective electrical work takes employers and employees working together. Both sides have responsibilities. This section covers what a good employee brings to the job, why safety is everyone's job, and what the law requires of employers.

      Employee responsibilities (3.1.0)

      To succeed, you need to use current materials, tools and equipment to finish work quickly and well, keep up with new technology, and keep learning the skills to use it. A professional never takes chances with their own safety or anyone else's.

      Professionalism (3.1.1)

      Professionalism is the overall behavior and attitude expected at work. Management support helps, but each person is responsible for their own. It includes honesty, productivity, safety, civility, cooperation, teamwork, clear communication, being on time and coming ready to work.

      Professionalism carries into writing, too. For work email:

      • Start with a clear subject line that says what the message is about.
      • Address the person you are writing to.
      • Keep it brief and to the point.
      • Make clear what response or action you need.
      • Use a positive tone and avoid blaming.
      • Do not type in all capital letters.
      • Avoid sarcasm. It is easy to misread.

      Texting is best for short messages that need to be seen right away. It is not for complicated messages or emotional ones, such as an apology or criticism. Proofread so autocorrect does not change your meaning, avoid shorthand unless you know the person understands it, and never text while driving or operating machinery.

      Honesty (3.1.2)

      Successful professionals are punctual and dependable, and they never cut corners or skimp on materials. They protect tools, materials and property belonging to the employer, customers and other trades from damage or theft. Dishonesty, whether stealing from the job site or lying about your work, always catches up with you. Honesty also means a fair day's work for a fair day's pay. Owning up to a mistake builds trust: errors in judgment can be forgiven, but dishonesty is much harder to forgive.

      Loyalty and respect (3.1.3)

      Employees expect employers to look out for them, provide steady work and promote them when openings come up. Employers expect loyalty back: keeping the company's interests in mind, speaking well of it, keeping small problems inside the company, and keeping business matters confidential. Loyalty is earned, not demanded. Respect, on the other hand, should always be there: treat others how you would like to be treated, do not insult or put people down, and watch your tone and body language.

      Learning, responsibility, cooperation and rules (3.1.4 to 3.1.7)

      • Willingness to learn: every company does things its own way. Be ready to adapt and learn new methods quickly, even when you are experienced.
      • Taking responsibility: once you understand an assignment and its safety rules, own it without needing reminders.
      • Cooperation: work as part of a team to get the job done efficiently, safely and on time. Helpful suggestions and compliments work better than negativity.
      • Rules and regulations: they are needed on every job and must be followed by every employee.

      Tardiness and absenteeism (3.1.8)

      Tardiness means being late. Absenteeism means being off the job. An occasional absence cannot be helped, but frequent lateness or absence signals poor work habits, unprofessional conduct and a lack of commitment.

      Key ideaEven if you are not paid when you are absent, it still costs the employer. Your health insurance still has to be paid, and jobs are bid and scheduled around a set crew size. If you must be absent, call in as soon as possible so your employer can find a replacement.

      Safety (3.1.9)

      In exchange for your job's benefits, you are obligated to work safely and to make sure anyone you supervise or work with does too. Your employer is obligated to keep the workplace safe. Safety is everyone's responsibility.

      Keeping the workplace safe means two things:

      1. Follow your company's rules for proper work procedures.
      2. Report any unsafe equipment or condition directly to your supervisor.

      Never ignore something unsafe, even if you think it does not affect you. It will not fix itself. Do not worry that your boss will be upset about lost time; you are more likely to be criticized for not reporting it. A little lost time is nothing compared with a major accident that shuts down the whole job. OSHA regulations require you to report hazardous conditions, on every kind of construction job, big contractor or small.

      SafetyAlways report unsafe conditions. Ignoring them could cause serious injury or even death.

      Beyond OSHA, there are standards just for electrical work:

      • The National Electrical Code (NEC) sets the minimum standards for the safe installation of electrical systems. For example, NEC temporary power rules are stricter than OSHA's and can be enforced by the inspector and by OSHA.
      • NFPA 70E, Standard for Electrical Safety in the Workplace, covers safe work practices near exposed energized parts: how to put a circuit into an electrically safe working condition, safe approach distances, and the PPE needed against electric shock and arc flash, such as the arc flash hood in the book's Figure 12.
      Ethical principles for the construction trades (sidebar in 3.1.9)
      • Honesty: be truthful, keep your commitments, never mislead.
      • Integrity: do what is right, even under pressure.
      • Loyalty: be worthy of trust.
      • Fairness: never take advantage of someone else's mistakes; treat people equally.
      • Respect for others: be courteous to everyone.
      • Obedience: follow laws, rules and regulations.
      • Commitment to excellence: stay prepared and keep building your skills.
      • Leadership: be a positive role model.

      Employer responsibilities (3.2.0)

      Employers' duties come from the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970. OSHA's job is to set safety and health standards for all workplaces, enforce them, make sure employers provide and maintain a safe workplace, and support safe work with research and education.

      OSHA requires every employer to provide a safe, hazard-free workplace, and it requires employees to follow OSHA rules about worker conduct. To get compliance, OSHA can do spot inspections, issue fines and even stop work until the site is safe.

      Key idea: your right to safety trainingUnder OSHA, your employer must:
      • Show you how to do each job safely
      • Provide the required personal protective equipment
      • Warn you about specific hazards
      • Supervise you for safety while you work

      Federal and state safety inspectors enforce the law and can fine violators. States may run their own safety programs, but the US Secretary of Labor must approve them first. Where a state has none, federal OSHA standards apply.

      Safety standards that apply to construction
      StandardCovers
      29 CFR 1926OSHA standards for the construction industry
      29 CFR 1910OSHA standards for general industry
      NFPA 70EElectrical safety in the workplace
      NECMinimum standards for safe electrical installation

      OSHA's main general requirements for construction employers:

      • Post signs where employees can easily see their rights and responsibilities.
      • Keep the job site free of serious hazards and in line with OSHA rules.
      • Post warning signs, posters and labels wherever required.
      • Inspect equipment on the job site often and regularly.
      • Teach every employee to recognize and avoid unsafe conditions and to know the rules for the job.
      • Allow no tools, equipment, machines or materials that do not meet 29 CFR 1926.
      • Let only qualified people operate tools, equipment and machines.
      • Provide medical training and exams when OSHA requires them.
      • Employers with more than 10 employees must keep records of work-related injuries and illnesses, available to employees.
      • Never discriminate against employees who use their OSHA rights.

      The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA) adds more: a worker with a disability who is qualified for the job has equal rights to employment. The US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission and state and local civil rights agencies enforce the ADA.
